# Richard H. Thaler

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 2
**Key Contribution:** Co-founder of behavioral economics; demonstrated that fairness norms constrain profit-seeking, documented the endowment effect, developed mental accounting theory, and pioneered the "nudge" approach to policy.

## Overview

Richard H. Thaler is one of the founding figures of behavioral economics and a Nobel laureate in Economics (2017). His work bridges psychology and economics by documenting systematic ways in which real human behavior departs from the predictions of standard economic models based on rational self-interest. Thaler is a frequent collaborator with [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]] and Jack L. Knetsch, and his contributions span multiple domains: fairness in markets, the [[endowment-effect]], [[mental-accounting]], [[myopic-loss-aversion]], and policy applications through "libertarian paternalism" (nudging).

In *Choices, Values, and Frames*, Thaler appears as author or co-author of multiple chapters spanning Part Three (Loss Aversion and the Value Function), Part Four (Framing and Mental Accounting), and Part Five (Applications). His work consistently demonstrates that psychological insights from [[prospect-theory]] have direct, measurable consequences for market behavior, firm strategy, and public policy.

## Key Ideas

- **[[fairness-as-constraint]]:** With Kahneman and Knetsch, established through telephone surveys that community standards of fairness constrain firm pricing and wage-setting. The principle of dual entitlement governs: transactors are entitled to the terms of reference transactions, firms to their reference profit. Passing on cost increases is acceptable; exploiting demand surges is not ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch18-fairness-constraint]])
- **[[endowment-effect]]:** With Kahneman and Knetsch, demonstrated that people demand much more to give up an object they own than they would pay to acquire it, with selling prices roughly double buying prices -- challenging the assumption that willingness to pay and willingness to accept should be approximately equal ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch8-endowment-effect]])
- **[[mental-accounting]]:** Developed the theory that people maintain separate mental accounts for different categories of spending and income, violating the fungibility assumption of standard economics. Mental accounting matters because it affects how outcomes are framed, bundled, and evaluated ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch14-mental-accounting]])
- **[[myopic-loss-aversion]]:** With Shlomo Benartzi, proposed that the equity premium puzzle can be explained by combining [[loss-aversion]] with frequent portfolio evaluation. When investors check their returns annually (where stocks frequently show losses), they demand a high equity premium; with longer evaluation periods, the premium should shrink ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch17-myopic-loss-aversion]])
- **Toward a positive theory of consumer choice:** Argued that economics needs a descriptive theory of consumer behavior that incorporates psychological realities like the endowment effect, sunk cost sensitivity, and self-control problems ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch15-positive-consumer-choice]])
- **Nudge:** With Cass Sunstein, developed the concept of "choice architecture" -- designing decision environments to steer people toward better outcomes without restricting freedom of choice

## Connections

- **Builds on:** [[kahneman]] and [[tversky]] ([[prospect-theory]], [[loss-aversion]], [[reference-dependence]]), Herbert Simon (bounded rationality), Arthur Okun (implicit contracts and fairness in markets)
- **Collaborators:** [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]], Jack L. Knetsch, Shlomo Benartzi, Cass Sunstein, [[camerer|Colin Camerer]], George Loewenstein, Linda Babcock, Sendhil Mullainathan
- **Extended by:** Cass Sunstein (nudge policy), behavioral public policy movement, behavioral finance, neuroeconomics
- **Contradicts:** Standard rational-agent models in economics, efficient market hypothesis (in its strong form), the assumption that fairness is merely instrumental to long-run profit maximization
