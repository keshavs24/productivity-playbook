# Robyn M. Dawes

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 2
**Key Contribution:** Demonstrated that even improper (non-optimally weighted) linear models outperform expert clinical judgment, establishing that "the whole trick is to know what variables to look at and then know how to add"

## Overview

Robyn M. Dawes (1936-2010) was a psychologist at Carnegie Mellon University (previously at the University of Oregon), known for his forceful advocacy of statistical over clinical prediction. Building on Paul Meehl's foundational work, Dawes showed that the superiority of models over human judges extends even to **improper** linear models -- those with equal weights, random weights, or bootstrapped weights -- demolishing the argument that models succeed merely because of optimized weighting.

Dawes was known for his clarity, wit, and willingness to confront professional resistance to his findings. His work has profound implications for any domain where experts must combine multiple pieces of information to make predictions or decisions.

## Key Ideas

- [[improper-linear-models]] -- Even crudely weighted linear models outperform expert judgment
- [[clinical-vs-statistical-prediction]] -- The consistent finding that mechanical combination beats intuitive judgment
- [[bootstrapping-judgment]] -- Building a model of a judge's behavior and using it in place of the judge
- "The whole trick is to know what variables to look at and then know how to add" (Dawes & Corrigan, 1974)
- The distinction between variable selection (human strength) and variable integration (model strength)
- "Cognitive conceit" -- the ethical problem of relying on inferior clinical judgment when better methods exist

## Connections

- **Builds on:** [[meehl|Paul Meehl]] -- *Clinical Versus Statistical Prediction* (1954)
- **Extended by:** Modern actuarial prediction, algorithmic decision-making, machine learning approaches
- **Contradicts:** Claims of clinical superiority, configural judgment, the value of unstructured interviews
- **Applied in:** Graduate admissions, clinical diagnosis, hiring, policy decisions (Denver bullet case)
