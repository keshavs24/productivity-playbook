# Paul Meehl

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 2
**Key Contribution:** Established the clinical vs. statistical prediction debate with his 1954 book demonstrating that statistical models outperform clinical judgment

## Overview

Paul Meehl (1920-2003) was a clinical psychologist and philosopher of science at the University of Minnesota. His 1954 book *Clinical Versus Statistical Prediction: A Theoretical Analysis and a Review of the Evidence* is one of the most consequential works in the history of psychology. It reviewed studies showing that formal (proper) linear models consistently outperform the clinical intuition of trained professionals when predicting numerical criterion variables from numerical predictor variables.

By 1965, Meehl could conclude that there was only a single disputed example showing clinical judgment to be superior -- and even that exception was immediately challenged. The "Meehl problem" remains one of the most robust and unsettling findings in psychological science.

## Key Ideas

- [[clinical-vs-statistical-prediction]] -- Mechanical combination of information consistently outperforms expert intuitive judgment
- The finding has held for 70+ years with no clear counterexample
- Extended by [[dawes|Robyn Dawes]] to show that even [[improper-linear-models]] beat clinical judgment
- Meehl also contributed to philosophy of science, construct validity theory, and taxometric methods

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Actuarial science, psychometrics, early studies of clinical accuracy
- **Extended by:** [[dawes]] -- improper linear models, [[bootstrapping-judgment]]
- **Contradicts:** Claims of clinical superiority, the value of unstructured expert judgment
- **Applied in:** Clinical diagnosis, risk assessment, personnel selection, any domain requiring expert prediction
