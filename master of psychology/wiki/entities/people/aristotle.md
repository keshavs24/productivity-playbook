# Aristotle

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Key Contribution:** Systematized rhetoric as a comprehensive art of persuasion grounded in logic, psychology, and ethics — creating the foundational framework of ethos, pathos, and logos that underlies virtually all subsequent Western communication theory

## Overview

Aristotle (384-322 B.C.E.) was a Greek philosopher born in Stagiros, northern Greece. His father, Nicomachus, was personal physician to the king of Macedon, a connection that would shape Aristotle's life. At seventeen, he entered Plato's Academy in Athens, where he studied for twenty years and developed interests in dialectic, logic, and rhetoric. After Plato's death in 347 B.C.E., Aristotle left Athens, eventually tutoring the young Alexander the Great (343-340 B.C.E.) before returning to Athens in 335 to found his own school, the Lyceum.

Aristotle's intellectual range is unparalleled in Western philosophy. He made foundational contributions to logic, metaphysics, ethics, politics, biology, physics, aesthetics (the *Poetics*), and rhetoric. His treatise [[on-rhetoric]] (*Peri rhetorike*), composed over decades (with early lectures from the 350s and revisions through the 330s), is the most systematic and influential work on persuasion ever written. It was the first to identify the [[three-modes-of-persuasion]] (ethos, pathos, logos), to classify rhetoric into three genres (deliberative, judicial, epideictic), and to analyze the [[enthymeme]] as the core logical tool of public discourse.

Of particular significance for psychology, Book 2 of the *Rhetoric* contains the earliest known systematic treatment of human emotions — defining anger, fear, pity, shame, envy, and other emotions in terms of their cognitive causes, targets, and effects on judgment. This predates any comparable psychological analysis by over two thousand years.

## Key Ideas

- **[[Three-modes-of-persuasion]]** — the tripartite framework of [[ethos]] (character), [[pathos]] (emotion), and [[logos]] (argument) as the complete set of artistic means of persuasion
- **[[Enthymeme]]** — the rhetorical syllogism, drawn from probable premises with audience-supplied assumptions; the "body" of persuasion
- **[[Three-genres-of-rhetoric]]** — deliberative (future/advantageous), judicial (past/just), epideictic (present/honorable)
- **[[Topoi]]** — twenty-eight common lines of argument applicable to any subject, plus nine fallacious patterns
- **[[Rhetorical-emotions]]** — systematic analysis of emotions as cognitive states that alter judgment, with structured definitions, causes, and targets for each
- **Rhetoric as morally neutral art** — Aristotle was the first to clearly recognize that persuasion techniques can be used for good or ill; the difference between a *rhetor* and a *sophist* is moral choice, not skill

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Plato (adapted the philosophical rhetoric proposed in *Phaedrus* to practical situations; responded to criticisms in *Gorgias*); the sophist tradition (systematized what they taught informally); earlier rhetorical handbooks (criticized them for narrowness but incorporated their techniques)
- **Extended by:** Cicero (*De Oratore*, *On Invention*); Quintilian (*Institutio Oratoria*); the entire tradition of Western rhetoric and communication theory
- **Contradicts:** Plato's *Gorgias* (where rhetoric is dismissed as flattery); the handbook writers (who focused only on judicial rhetoric and emotional appeals)
