# David M. Eddy

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 2
**Key Contribution:** Demonstrated that physicians systematically fail at probabilistic reasoning in clinical medicine, producing the landmark mammography screening example of [[base-rate-neglect]]

## Overview

David M. Eddy is a physician, mathematician, and health policy researcher who became one of the most influential figures in evidence-based medicine and medical decision analysis. His chapter "Probabilistic reasoning in clinical medicine: Problems and opportunities" in the Kahneman, Slovic, & Tversky (1982) collection is a landmark paper that bridged cognitive psychology's findings on [[base-rate-neglect]] with real-world medical practice.

Eddy's mammography analysis is perhaps the single most cited example of how Bayesian reasoning failures produce consequential real-world errors. By showing that approximately 95 out of 100 physicians misestimate the probability of cancer after a positive mammogram by nearly tenfold, he demonstrated that the biases documented in laboratory settings by [[kahneman]] and [[tversky]] operate with devastating effect in high-stakes professional contexts.

Beyond documenting the error, Eddy performed a systematic review of published medical literature and policy statements, showing that the confusion between [[retrospective-vs-predictive-accuracy|retrospective and predictive accuracy]] was embedded in the profession's written knowledge base -- not merely in individual physicians' reasoning.

## Key Ideas

- [[base-rate-fallacy-in-medicine]] -- The systematic confusion of P(pos|disease) with P(disease|pos) by physicians
- [[retrospective-vs-predictive-accuracy]] -- The critical distinction between sensitivity/specificity and positive/negative predictive value
- **Biopsy threshold concept** -- The probability of disease at which physician and patient agree that biopsy is warranted; a key parameter for rational use of diagnostic information
- **Asymmetric use of diagnostic information** -- Clinical policy allows tests to confirm suspicion but not to reduce it, wasting half the test's diagnostic potential

## Connections

- **Builds on:** [[kahneman]] and [[tversky]]'s work on [[base-rate-neglect]] and [[representativeness]]; Bayes' theorem (1763)
