# Colin F. Camerer

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 2
**Key Contribution:** Demonstrated that prospect theory explains 10 distinct real-world economic phenomena across finance, labor, consumer goods, insurance, and gambling markets.

## Overview

Colin F. Camerer is a behavioral economist who has made foundational contributions to bridging the gap between laboratory findings in decision science and naturally occurring economic data. His chapter "Prospect Theory in the Wild: Evidence from the Field" (Ch. 16 in *Choices, Values, and Frames*) is a landmark catalog of real-world applications of [[prospect-theory]], showing that three simple features of the theory -- [[loss-aversion]], the [[reflection-effect]], and nonlinear probability weighting -- can parsimoniously explain anomalies that expected utility theory can only address with contradictory ad hoc assumptions.

Camerer is also a co-author of the influential NYC cab driver study (with Babcock, Loewenstein, and Thaler) demonstrating that cab drivers exhibit negative wage elasticity due to daily income targeting -- a key real-world demonstration of loss aversion and [[reference-dependence]] in labor supply. He is a pioneer in neuroeconomics and experimental economics.

## Key Ideas

- **Prospect theory has real-world explanatory power:** 10 field phenomena across diverse economic domains are anomalies for expected utility but consistent with prospect theory ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch16-pt-in-the-wild]])
- **Narrow bracketing is essential:** All 10 applications require the assumption that people isolate decisions rather than integrating them into broader portfolios
- **Three PT ingredients explain everything:** [[loss-aversion]] (equity premium, cab drivers, price elasticities, consumption stickiness, status quo bias), [[reflection-effect]] (disposition effect, end-of-day betting, consumption insensitivity), nonlinear probability weighting (favorite-longshot bias, lotto demand, wire repair insurance)
- **EU explanations are ad hoc and contradictory:** Convex utility explains lotteries but predicts stocks should return less than bonds; liquidity constraints explain cab drivers but make wrong predictions for teachers
- **NYC cab driver study:** Daily income targeting produces downward-sloping labor supply; negative hours-wage correlation strongest for inexperienced drivers ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch20-cab-drivers]])
- **Overconfidence and excess entry** (Ch. 23 in CVF, with Dan Lovallo): Experimental evidence that overconfidence drives excessive market entry
