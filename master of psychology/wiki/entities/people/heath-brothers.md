# Heath Brothers (Chip Heath & Dan Heath)

**Type:** Person (collaborative pair)
**Phase(s):** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Key Contribution:** Developed the [[success-framework|SUCCESs framework]] for designing ideas that stick — a practical, research-backed checklist that synthesizes decades of research on memory, attention, persuasion, and communication into six actionable principles

## Overview

Chip Heath and Dan Heath are brothers who collaborated on [[made-to-stick]] (2007), approaching the question "Why do some ideas stick while others die?" from complementary angles.

**Chip Heath** is a professor at Stanford Graduate School of Business. He has spent over a decade studying why bad ideas succeed in the marketplace of ideas — examining urban legends, conspiracy theories, wartime rumors, and proverbs. He has conducted more than forty experiments with over 1,700 participants on topics including why Nostradamus's prophecies persist, why *Chicken Soup for the Soul* stories are inspirational, and why ineffective folk remedies survive. He teaches a Stanford course called "How to Make Ideas Stick."

**Dan Heath** developed a passion for education and co-founded Thinkwell, a start-up textbook publishing company that used video and technology. Working with some of the most effective professors in the country — a calculus teacher who was also a stand-up comic, a biology teacher named national Teacher of the Year, an economics teacher who was also a chaplain and playwright — Dan discovered that while each teacher had a unique personal style, their instructional *methodologies* were almost identical. The best teachers were systematic, not just charismatic.

In 2004, the brothers realized they had been converging on the same problem. Chip was the researcher and Dan was the practitioner. Together they created the [[success-framework]], identifying six principles (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories) that explain why ideas succeed or fail. The book also identified the [[curse-of-knowledge]] as the primary villain — the cognitive bias that makes it hard for knowledgeable people to communicate clearly.

## Key Ideas

- **[[success-framework]]** — the SUCCESs checklist: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories
- **[[curse-of-knowledge]]** — the villain of communication: once you know something, you can't un-know it, making it hard to communicate with those who don't
- **[[commanders-intent]]** — the Army's method for communicating the essential core, used as the central metaphor for "Simple"
- **[[schema-breaking]]** — deliberately violating expectations to grab attention; the engine of "Unexpected"
- **[[gap-theory-of-curiosity]]** — Loewenstein's theory applied to sustaining attention; the second half of "Unexpected"
- **[[concreteness-principle]]** — concrete sensory language aids memory, understanding, and coordination; the easiest principle to apply
- **Idea Clinics** — before-and-after message redesigns demonstrating the SUCCESs principles in practice (sun exposure, foreign aid, fundraising, oral rehydration therapy)

## Connections

- **Builds on:** George Loewenstein (gap theory of curiosity), Paul Ekman (surprise brow), [[cialdini|Robert Cialdini]] (mystery as teaching tool, influence principles), [[tversky|Amos Tversky]] (decision paralysis research), Malcolm Gladwell (*The Tipping Point* — the concept of "stickiness"), Donald Schon (generative metaphors)
- **Extended by:** The Heaths' later books (*Switch*, *Decisive*, *The Power of Moments*) extend the communication framework to behavior change, decision-making, and experience design
- **Contradicts:** The implicit assumption in much business communication that accuracy and comprehensiveness are sufficient for effectiveness — the Heaths show that accurate but abstract ideas routinely fail
- **Applied in:** The [[success-framework]] is directly applicable to teaching, marketing, organizational strategy, political communication, and public health messaging
