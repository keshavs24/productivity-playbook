# Lee Ross

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 1, 2, 4, 5, 6
**Key Contribution:** Named the fundamental attribution error; pioneered research on belief perseverance, false consensus effect, and the psychology of naively realistic social judgment

## Overview

Lee Ross is a social psychologist at Stanford University and one of the most influential figures in the study of human judgment and social cognition. He is best known for coining the term "fundamental attribution error" (Ross, 1977) to describe the pervasive tendency to overweight dispositional factors and underweight situational factors when explaining others' behavior. This concept has become one of the most cited and widely taught findings in social psychology.

Ross's research program, often conducted with collaborators including Mark Lepper, Craig Anderson, and Richard Nisbett, spans several interconnected domains: attribution theory, belief perseverance, biased assimilation of evidence, the false consensus effect, and the barriers to rational conflict resolution. His work with Nisbett culminated in the influential book *Human Inference: Strategies and Shortcomings of Social Judgment* (1980), which bridges the heuristics-and-biases tradition of Kahneman and Tversky with social psychology.

In the context of this curriculum, Ross provides the critical link between cognitive biases (Phase 1-2) and their social-psychological manifestations in persuasion (Phase 6), negotiation (Phase 5), and communication (Phase 4). His work demonstrates that the same inferential shortcomings documented by Kahneman and Tversky in abstract probability tasks operate powerfully in real social judgment.

## Key Ideas

- [[fundamental-attribution-error]] -- the most important non-motivational attribution bias: overweighting dispositions, underweighting situations (Ross, 1977)
- [[false-consensus-effect]] -- the tendency to see one's own behavioral choices as common and alternatives as deviant and revealing (Ross, Greene, & House, 1977)
- [[belief-perseverance]] -- beliefs survive even total destruction of their evidential bases (Ross, Lepper, & Hubbard, 1975; Anderson, Lepper, & Ross, 1980)
- [[biased-assimilation]] -- exposure to mixed evidence polarizes rather than moderates opposing viewpoints (Lord, Ross, & Lepper, 1979)
- [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]] -- vivid, concrete information overwhelms pallid statistical data in social judgment (Nisbett & Ross, 1980)
