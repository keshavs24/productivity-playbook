# Elliot Aronson

**Type:** Person — Social Psychologist
**Phase(s):** 1
**Key Contribution:** One of the most cited social psychologists in history; co-author of the leading *Social Psychology* textbook and *Age of Propaganda* (with [[pratkanis|Anthony Pratkanis]]); pioneered research on cognitive dissonance, the jigsaw classroom, effort justification, and the gain-loss theory of attraction. The only person to have won all three major APA awards (distinguished writing, teaching, and research).

## Overview

Elliot Aronson is among the most influential social psychologists of the 20th and 21st centuries. His research contributions span cognitive dissonance, self-justification, interpersonal attraction, and prejudice reduction, and his textbook *Social Psychology* (with Wilson and Akert) is one of the most widely used in the field, shaping how generations of students learn about biases, attribution, and social influence.

Aronson is widely regarded as one of the most cited social psychologists alive. His ability to combine rigorous experimental research with practical, real-world applications distinguishes his career. Where many researchers stay in the laboratory, Aronson brought social psychology into schools, courtrooms, and policy discussions — always asking how the science of social behavior can be used to reduce prejudice, improve education, and promote human welfare.

## Key Research

### Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson, Stephan, Sikes, Blaney, & Snapp, 1978)

The jigsaw classroom is Aronson's most celebrated applied intervention, developed in response to the challenges of school desegregation in Austin, Texas in the early 1970s. Aronson observed that the traditional competitive classroom structure was a breeding ground for prejudice — children competed against each other for the teacher's approval, and newly desegregated schools combined this competitive pressure with existing racial hostility. Contact alone was not reducing prejudice; in fact, Stephan (1978) found that desegregation was more likely to *increase* prejudice than decrease it.

**How it works:** Students are placed into small, diverse groups. The lesson material is divided into segments (like pieces of a jigsaw puzzle), and each student is assigned one piece. No student can succeed on the test without learning the material from every other group member. Students must take turns teaching and helping one another. This transforms classmates from competitors into essential collaborators — each person holds information the others need.

**Results (Aronson, Bridgeman, & Geffner, 1978a, 1978b):** Compared to traditional competitive classrooms, the jigsaw classroom produced:
- Significantly more cross-ethnic friendships and less prejudice between racial groups
- Increased minority students' self-esteem, liking for school, and test scores
- White students benefited too — their self-esteem and liking for school increased, and test performance was at least as high as in traditional classes

The jigsaw classroom demonstrated the principle behind Sherif's Robbers Cave findings: mere contact fails to reduce intergroup hostility, but structured cooperation toward shared goals reliably produces liking and friendship across group lines. See [[cooperation-and-liking]] for the broader principle.

### Effort Justification (Aronson & Mills, 1959)

Aronson and Mills demonstrated that the severity of an initiation into a group increases how much people value that group — a direct prediction from [[cognitive-dissonance]] theory. Women who underwent a severe initiation to join a boring discussion group rated the group as significantly more interesting and valuable than those who underwent a mild initiation or no initiation at all. The mechanism is dissonance reduction: having suffered to gain entry, devaluing the group would create intolerable dissonance with the effort already invested. Instead, people inflate their evaluation of the outcome to justify the cost.

This finding underpins the concept of [[effortful-commitment]] and explains why hazing, difficult training programs, and arduous initiation rituals reliably increase group loyalty — not despite the suffering, but because of it.

### Competitive Classrooms as Breeding Grounds for Prejudice

Aronson's analysis of the traditional American classroom is a key contribution to understanding why desegregation alone did not reduce prejudice. He argued that the standard classroom creates a competitive environment where students vie for the teacher's limited approval and favorable evaluations. This structure means that one student's success comes at another's expense. Under these conditions, intergroup contact does not produce the positive associations needed for prejudice reduction — it produces frustration, rivalry, and reinforcement of stereotypes. The jigsaw classroom was designed specifically to break this competitive monopoly by introducing regular cooperative structures alongside traditional methods.
