# Amos Tversky

**Type:** Person — Cognitive Psychologist
**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Key Contribution:** Co-founder (with [[kahneman]]) of the heuristics and biases research program that revolutionized understanding of human judgment under uncertainty.

## Overview

Amos Tversky (1937–1996) was a cognitive and mathematical psychologist who, alongside [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]], fundamentally changed how we understand human decision-making. Their collaboration, beginning at Hebrew University in the late 1960s, produced some of the most cited papers in the history of social science.

Tversky was known for his mathematical rigor and his ability to design elegant experiments that revealed deep flaws in rational-agent models. His work with Kahneman on [[representativeness]], [[availability]], and [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] showed that human judgment relies on heuristics that are useful but systematically biased. Their later work on [[prospect-theory]] (1979) provided an alternative to expected utility theory and became the foundation of behavioral economics.

Tversky spent his later career at Stanford University. He died of metastatic melanoma in 1996, six years before Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in Economics for their joint work — the prize is not awarded posthumously.

## Key Ideas
- [[representativeness]] — Judging probability by similarity (co-developed with Kahneman)
- [[availability]] — Judging frequency by ease of recall (co-developed with Kahneman)
- [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — Estimating by insufficient adjustment from an anchor (co-developed with Kahneman)
- [[prospect-theory]] — Alternative to expected utility theory: loss aversion, reference dependence, decision weights (1979 Econometrica paper)
- [[loss-aversion]] — Losses loom ~2-2.5x larger than equivalent gains
- [[framing-effects]] — Same choice, different presentation, different preferences
- [[value-function]] — S-shaped function mapping gains/losses to subjective value
- [[heuristics-and-biases-program]] — The overarching research program demonstrating systematic deviations from rationality

## Connections
- **Collaborated with:** [[kahneman]] — decades-long partnership producing foundational work
- **Influenced:** Behavioral economics, [[cialdini]], [[bazerman]], [[ariely]], and virtually all subsequent work on cognitive biases
- **Extended by:** [[kahneman]]'s later solo work (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*), Bazerman's managerial decision-making applications
