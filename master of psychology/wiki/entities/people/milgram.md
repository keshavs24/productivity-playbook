# Stanley Milgram

**Type:** Person
**Phase(s):** 1 — Cognitive Foundations
**Key Contribution:** Designed and conducted the most famous experiments in social psychology — the obedience studies — demonstrating that ordinary people will inflict extreme suffering on others when directed by an authority figure

## Overview

Stanley Milgram (1933-1984) was a psychology professor whose obedience experiments, conducted at Yale University beginning in 1963, remain among the most important and disturbing findings in all of social science. His work demonstrated that [[obedience-to-authority]] is not a property of deviant individuals but a near-universal human tendency — one powerful enough to override personal conscience, empathy, and moral judgment in the majority of ordinary people.

Milgram originally designed his experimental paradigm to understand how the German citizenry could have participated in the concentration camp destruction of millions of innocents during the Nazi era. He planned to develop his experimental procedures in the United States and then take them to Germany, a country he assumed would provide enough obedience for a full scientific analysis. The first experiment in New Haven, Connecticut, made the trip unnecessary: "I found so much obedience," he said, "I hardly saw the need of taking the experiment to Germany."

His findings have been replicated across multiple countries (Holland, Germany, Spain, Italy, Australia, Jordan) and across decades, with a recent replication (Burger, in press) finding no significant differences between modern subjects and Milgram's original participants. The basic finding — that roughly two-thirds of ordinary people will administer what they believe are dangerous and painful shocks to a screaming, pleading victim when an authority directs them to — has proven robust across cultures, time periods, and subject demographics.

## Key Ideas

- **The obedience paradigm**: A deceptively simple experimental design — Teacher/Learner/Experimenter roles, escalating shock switches — that revealed the extraordinary power of situational authority pressure over individual moral judgment
- **"It is the extreme willingness of adults to go to almost any lengths on the command of an authority that constitutes the chief finding of the study"** (Milgram, 1974) — the central conclusion
- **Authority is the key variable**: Multiple variations (switched scripts, contradictory researchers, fellow-subject-as-authority) isolated authority pressure as the cause, ruling out aggression, sadism, or ignorance of harm
- **Normal people, extraordinary behavior**: Subjects were a representative cross-section of society — psychologically normal, with no hint of psychosis on personality scales. "They are you and me," as Milgram put it.
- **The prediction gap**: Everyone — psychiatrists, psychologists, lay observers — dramatically underestimated obedience rates, revealing a blind spot about the power of situational authority pressure (paralleling the [[fundamental-attribution-error]])
