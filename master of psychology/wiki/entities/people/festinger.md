# Leon Festinger

**Type:** Person — Social Psychologist
**Phase(s):** 1
**Key Contribution:** Originator of cognitive dissonance theory (1957) and social comparison theory (1954); conducted the landmark *When Prophecy Fails* doomsday cult study demonstrating how disconfirmed beliefs can paradoxically strengthen commitment.

## Overview

Leon Festinger (1919--1989) was one of the most influential social psychologists of the 20th century. His cognitive dissonance theory -- the idea that holding contradictory cognitions produces psychological discomfort that motivates people to reduce the inconsistency -- is among the most generative theories in all of psychology. It provided the theoretical foundation for understanding why people rationalize decisions, justify effort, and cling to beliefs in the face of contradictory evidence.

Festinger's work appears throughout the Phase 1 curriculum. Cialdini identifies the desire for consistency (rooted in Festinger's theory) as a "central motivator of behavior" alongside Fritz Heider's balance theory and Theodore Newcomb's congruity theory. Festinger's social comparison theory -- the idea that people evaluate their own opinions and abilities by comparing them to others, especially similar others -- also provides a theoretical basis for Cialdini's [[social-proof]] principle.

## Key Research

### Cognitive Dissonance Theory (Festinger, 1957)

Festinger proposed that when a person holds two or more cognitions that are psychologically inconsistent, they experience an aversive motivational state -- dissonance -- that drives them to reduce the inconsistency. Reduction strategies include changing one's beliefs, changing one's behavior, or adding new cognitions that reconcile the contradiction. The theory explains phenomena ranging from post-decisional rationalization to effort justification to the persistence of discredited beliefs. See [[cognitive-dissonance]] for the full concept page.

### When Prophecy Fails (Festinger, Riecken, & Schachter, 1964)

Cialdini calls this his "favorite illustration" of [[social-proof]]. Festinger and colleagues infiltrated a Chicago-area doomsday cult led by "Mrs. Marian Keech," who claimed to receive messages from extraterrestrial beings ("the Guardians") predicting a catastrophic flood. The cult, never more than 30 members, prepared for rescue by spacemen.

When the prophesied flood failed to occur, the group initially fell into confusion. Then Mrs. Keech announced a new message: the group's faith had been so strong that God had saved the world. The critical finding was what happened next. Before the disconfirmation, the group had been secretive and publicity-averse. Afterward, they launched an urgent campaign of proselytization -- calling newspapers, granting interviews, seeking converts for the first time. Festinger's interpretation: having committed everything (jobs, money, social ties) to the belief, members could not afford to accept disconfirmation. Instead, they sought social proof -- if they could persuade others to believe, it would reduce their dissonance. "I can't afford to doubt. I have to believe."

The cult ultimately failed -- they gained no new converts and disintegrated within three weeks. But historically, other doomsday groups that successfully recruited after disconfirmation (e.g., the Dutch Anabaptists after 1533) grew and prospered, demonstrating that the strategy can work when social proof is successfully manufactured.

### Social Comparison Theory (Festinger, 1954)

Festinger proposed that people have a fundamental drive to evaluate their own opinions and abilities, and that in the absence of objective, non-social means of evaluation, they compare themselves to other people -- especially similar others. This theory provides a key mechanism behind [[social-proof]]: we follow the behavior of people like us because they serve as the most informative comparison standard for determining correct action. Cialdini cites Festinger (1954) directly when explaining why social proof operates most powerfully when we observe similar others.

## Key Ideas
- [[cognitive-dissonance]] -- Contradictory cognitions produce aversive arousal that motivates consistency-restoring change
- [[commitment-and-consistency]] -- Cialdini's principle built directly on Festinger's theoretical foundation
- [[social-proof]] -- Social comparison theory explains why we use similar others' behavior as evidence of correctness
- [[effortful-commitment]] -- Effort justification (Aronson & Mills, 1959) is a direct derivation from dissonance theory

## Connections
- **Influenced by:** Kurt Lewin (field theory, group dynamics)
- **Influenced:** [[aronson|Elliot Aronson]] (student; extended dissonance theory to effort justification and self-justification), [[cialdini|Robert Cialdini]] (commitment/consistency principle and social proof both build on Festinger's theories)
- **Builds on:** Fritz Heider's balance theory (1946), Theodore Newcomb's congruity theory (1953) -- all three theorists viewed consistency as a central motivator
- **Extended by:** Aronson & Mills (effort justification, 1959); contemporary research on self-affirmation theory (Steele) as an alternative to dissonance reduction
