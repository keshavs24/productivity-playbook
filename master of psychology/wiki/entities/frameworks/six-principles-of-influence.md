# Six Principles of Influence

**Type:** Framework
**Phase(s):** 1 (Cognitive Foundations), 6 (Advanced Persuasion)
**Key Contribution:** A comprehensive taxonomy of social influence showing that virtually all compliance tactics fall into six categories, each governed by a fundamental psychological principle that triggers automatic responding

## Overview

Robert B. Cialdini's Six Principles of Influence is the organizing framework for the entire field of compliance research. Developed through a unique combination of experimental social psychology and nearly three years of participant observation among compliance professionals (sales organizations, fundraising operations, advertising agencies, PR firms), the framework identifies six universal principles that account for the vast majority of successful influence attempts.

The framework's power lies in its parsimony and explanatory reach. Thousands of different compliance tactics used across cultures and contexts reduce to just six underlying mechanisms. Each principle works because it triggers a normally adaptive automatic response — what Cialdini calls [[click-whirr]] responding. These shortcuts are usually reliable guides to correct behavior, which is precisely why they are so effective when exploited.

Cialdini's synthesis chapter (Ch.8) argues that the framework's importance will only increase over time. As information overload intensifies and the pace of modern life accelerates, people will rely more heavily on shortcut decision-making, making the six trigger features ever more potent. This creates both an opportunity (for ethical persuaders) and a danger (from exploiters who fabricate trigger cues).

## The Six Principles

### 1. Reciprocation
**Chapter:** 2 | **Summary:** [[summary-1-04-influence-ch2]] | **Concept page:** [[reciprocation]]

**Core mechanism:** The rule of reciprocation says that we should try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us. The obligation to reciprocate is so powerful that it can overcome dislike, uninvited favors, and unequal exchanges.

**Key tactics:**
- Free samples and gifts that create obligation
- [[rejection-then-retreat]] (door-in-the-face) — start with a large request, retreat to a smaller one; the concession triggers reciprocal concession
- Uninvited debts — giving something unasked creates obligation anyway

**Defense:** Distinguish genuine favors from compliance devices. Accept initial favors in good faith, but redefine exploitative "favors" as tricks — the rule says to repay favors with favors, not tricks with favors.

### 2. Commitment and Consistency
**Chapter:** 3 | **Summary:** [[summary-1-04-influence-ch3]] | **Concept page:** [[commitment-and-consistency]]

**Core mechanism:** Once we make a choice or take a stand, we encounter personal and interpersonal pressures to behave consistently with that commitment. Consistency is valued because it is adaptive, socially attractive, and provides a shortcut through complexity.

**Key tactics:**
- [[foot-in-the-door]] — small initial commitment escalates to larger compliance
- [[lowball-technique]] — favorable terms secure commitment, then terms worsen but commitment persists because it has "grown its own legs"
- [[public-commitment]] — commitments visible to others are more binding
- [[active-commitment]] — writing or speaking a commitment changes self-image more than passive agreement
- [[effortful-commitment]] — greater effort invested increases the commitment's hold
- [[inner-choice]] — commitments perceived as freely chosen (not coerced) produce lasting attitude change

**Defense:** Listen for "stomach signs" — the visceral feeling that you are being manipulated. Ask: "Knowing what I know now, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?" The "heart of hearts" test.

### 3. Social Proof
**Chapter:** 4 | **Summary:** [[summary-1-04-influence-ch4]] | **Concept page:** [[social-proof]]

**Core mechanism:** We determine what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct. The principle applies especially to determining correct behavior, and its power is amplified under uncertainty and when the others are similar to us.

**Key tactics:**
- Canned laughter — artificially manufactured social proof
- Testimonials and "person on the street" interviews
- "Fastest growing" and "best-selling" claims
- Bartender tip jar salting
- Long lines and manufactured scarcity signals

**Key phenomena:**
- [[bystander-effect]] and [[pluralistic-ignorance]] — social proof can kill when each bystander looks to others for guidance and everyone concludes no emergency exists
- [[werther-effect]] — publicized suicides trigger copycat suicides
- [[similarity-and-social-proof]] — we follow most readily the lead of similar others
- [[uncertainty-and-social-proof]] — uncertainty amplifies social proof's power

**Defense:** Be alert to obviously fabricated social proof (canned laughter, staged testimonials). Recognize that the actions of similar others are not a reliable guide when those others are themselves uncertain.

### 4. Liking
**Chapter:** 5 | **Summary:** [[summary-1-04-influence-ch5]] | **Concept page:** [[liking-principle]]

**Core mechanism:** We prefer to say yes to people we know and like. Compliance professionals systematically exploit the factors that produce liking: physical attractiveness, similarity, compliments, contact and cooperation, and conditioning/association.

**Key factors:**
- [[physical-attractiveness-bias]] — the halo effect extends attractiveness to competence, honesty, and trustworthiness
- [[similarity-and-liking]] — we like people who are similar to us in opinions, personality, background, or lifestyle
- Compliments — flattery works even when we know it is insincere
- Contact and cooperation — familiarity breeds liking (the [[mere-exposure-effect]]), especially under conditions of cooperative interaction (the jigsaw classroom)
- Conditioning and association — weather reporters blamed for bad weather; celebrities associated with products; "luncheon technique"

**Defense:** Ask whether you have come to like the practitioner more than you would expect given the circumstances. Separate the requester from the request.

### 5. Authority
**Chapter:** 6 | **Summary:** [[summary-1-04-influence-ch6]] | **Concept page:** [[authority-principle]]

**Core mechanism:** There is a deep-seated sense of duty to authority within us all. Deference to legitimate authorities is adaptive because they usually possess superior knowledge and power. But the obedience response can be triggered by mere symbols of authority without genuine expertise.

**Key findings:**
- [[obedience-to-authority]] — Milgram's experiments showed ~67% of subjects delivered maximum shocks when directed by an authority figure
- [[authority-symbols]] — titles, clothing, and trappings trigger compliance even without genuine expertise
- [[sly-sincerity]] — admitting a small weakness first increases perceived trustworthiness, making subsequent claims more persuasive

**Defense:** Ask two questions: (1) Is this authority truly an expert? (2) How truthful can we expect this expert to be? Be alert to the illegitimate use of authority symbols.

### 6. Scarcity
**Chapter:** 7 | **Summary:** [[summary-1-04-influence-ch7]] | **Concept page:** [[scarcity-principle]]

**Core mechanism:** Opportunities seem more valuable when their availability is limited. Scarcity works through two mechanisms: (1) things difficult to attain are typically better, so availability serves as a quality shortcut; (2) as things become less accessible, we lose freedoms, triggering [[psychological-reactance]].

**Key tactics:**
- [[limited-numbers]] — "Only 5 left!"
- [[deadline-tactic]] — "Offer expires Friday!"
- Competition for scarce resources — auctions, bidding wars, rival suitors

**Key findings:**
- Scarce items are desired more but do not taste, feel, or work any better (the cookie jar study: Worchel, Lee, & Adewole, 1975)
- Newly scarce items are valued more than items that were always scarce
- Social-demand scarcity (competition with others) is the most powerful form
- [[romeo-and-juliet-effect]] — parental interference intensifies romantic desire
- Censored information is desired more and found more persuasive, even if unheard
- [[commodity-theory]] — any commodity is valued more to the extent that it is unavailable

**Defense:** Use the rush of arousal as a signal. Pause and ask: Do I want this item for its function or merely to possess it? "The scarce cookies didn't taste any better."

## Meta-Mechanism: Click, Whirr

All six principles operate through the same meta-mechanism: [[click-whirr]] responding. Each principle provides a single trigger feature that, under normal circumstances, reliably cues the correct response. Like the cheep-cheep sound that triggers a mother turkey's maternal behavior, these single features allow efficient decision-making by bypassing the need for full analysis. The principles work best (and are most exploitable) when the target lacks the cognitive resources for careful deliberation — a condition Cialdini calls [[primitive-automaticity]].

## Defense Framework

Cialdini provides a two-tier defense structure:

1. **Principle-specific defenses** — each chapter identifies the particular signs that a principle is being exploited and offers targeted countermeasures
2. **Meta-defense** — aggressive retaliation against anyone who fabricates, falsifies, or counterfeits trigger cues. The goal is not to stop using shortcuts (that is impossible in modern life) but to keep them reliable by punishing those who corrupt them

## Connections

- **Builds on:** [[heuristics-and-biases-program]] (Tversky/Kahneman — the six principles are applied cognitive shortcuts), [[attribution-theory]] (Heider — we attribute behavior to dispositions, underestimating situational forces including the six principles), [[cognitive-dissonance]] (Festinger — theoretical basis for commitment/consistency)
- **Extended by:** *Pre-Suasion* (Cialdini, 2016) — what happens before the influence attempt to make the principles more effective
- **Complements:** [[prospect-theory]] (decision under risk — loss aversion interacts with scarcity and commitment), [[emotional-intelligence]] (EI's dual-edged nature: these principles can be used prosocially or manipulatively)
- **Tensions with:** Full-rationality models of decision-making — the framework assumes bounded rationality and automatic responding as the norm
- Part of [[phase-1-overview]] — the Six Principles are a core framework in the Cognitive Foundations phase
