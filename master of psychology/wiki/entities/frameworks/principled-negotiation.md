# Principled Negotiation Framework

**Type:** Framework
**Phase(s):** 5 — Negotiation
**Key Contribution:** A four-principle method for negotiation that replaces the soft/hard dichotomy of positional bargaining with an approach that is simultaneously soft on people and hard on problems, producing wise agreements efficiently and amicably

## Overview

The Principled Negotiation Framework, developed by [[fisher-ury|Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton]] at the Harvard Negotiation Project, is the organizing framework for modern interest-based negotiation. First published in [[getting-to-yes|Getting to Yes]] (1981, 2nd ed. 1991), the framework proposes that virtually all negotiation problems can be addressed through four fundamental principles, supplemented by two techniques for resistant counterparts and a concept of negotiating power based on alternatives rather than resources.

The framework's enduring influence comes from its parsimony (four principles cover the entire domain), its universality (applicable to two-party and multi-party, one-issue and many-issue, domestic and international negotiations), and its remarkable property that *knowing the other side uses it makes it work better, not worse*.

## The Four Principles

### 1. Separate the People from the Problem
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch2-people]] | **Concept:** [[separate-people-from-problem]]

Deal with people problems (perception, emotion, communication) independently from substantive issues. Be soft on the people, hard on the problem. Base the relationship on accurate perceptions, clear communication, appropriate emotions, and a forward-looking outlook.

### 2. Focus on Interests, Not Positions
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch3-interests]] | **Concept:** [[positions-vs-interests]]

Look behind stated positions to identify the underlying needs, desires, concerns, and fears that motivate them. For every interest, there usually exist several positions that could satisfy it. Behind opposed positions lie many shared and compatible interests.

### 3. Invent Options for Mutual Gain
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch4-options]] | **Concept:** [[invent-options-for-mutual-gain]]

Before trying to reach agreement, generate a wide range of creative possibilities. Overcome four obstacles: premature judgment, searching for the single answer, the [[fixed-pie-bias|fixed-pie assumption]], and thinking that solving their problem is their problem. Dovetail differing interests — differences create value.

### 4. Insist on Using Objective Criteria
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch5-criteria]] | **Concept:** [[objective-criteria]]

Base outcomes on fair standards (market value, precedent, professional standards) or fair procedures (one cuts/the other chooses, arbitration, mediation). Never yield to pressure, only to principle.

## Supplementary Components

### BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement)
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch6-power]] | **Concept:** [[batna]]

Your [[batna|BATNA]] is the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured. It is the source of your negotiating power. The better your BATNA, the greater your power. Develop it actively; don't just identify it.

### Negotiation Jujitsu
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]] | **Concept:** [[negotiation-jujitsu]]

When they won't play: don't push back. Step aside and deflect their energy onto the problem. Use questions instead of statements. Use silence as a tool. Key stock phrases for each type of attack.

### One-Text Procedure
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]] | **Concept:** [[one-text-procedure]]

When a third party is needed: prepare a single draft, ask each side to criticize it, revise iteratively, present the final text for a binary yes/no decision. Used at Camp David (23 drafts over 13 days).

### Dirty Tricks Defense
**Summary:** [[summary-5-16-gty-ch8-dirty-tricks]]

Apply the four principles to the negotiation *procedure* itself. Recognize the tactic, raise it explicitly, negotiate about the rules of the game. Three categories: deliberate deception, psychological warfare, positional pressure tactics.

## Three Stages of Application

The framework applies across three stages of any negotiation:

| Stage | Activity | Application of Four Principles |
|-------|----------|-------------------------------|
| **Analysis** | Diagnosing the situation | Identify people problems; identify interests; note existing options; note existing criteria |
| **Planning** | Generating ideas and deciding what to do | Plan how to handle people problems; prioritize interests; generate options; identify applicable criteria |
| **Discussion** | Communicating back and forth | Acknowledge perception differences and emotions; explore interests; jointly generate options; reason about criteria |

## Comparison: Soft vs. Hard vs. Principled

| Dimension | Soft | Hard | Principled |
|-----------|------|------|-----------|
| Participants | Friends | Adversaries | **Problem-solvers** |
| Goal | Agreement | Victory | **Wise outcome, efficiently and amicably** |
| Concessions | Make them to cultivate relationship | Demand them as condition of relationship | **Separate people from problem** |
| People & problem | Soft on both | Hard on both | **Soft on people, hard on problem** |
| Trust | Trust others | Distrust others | **Proceed independent of trust** |
| Position | Change easily | Dig in | **Focus on interests, not positions** |
| Bottom line | Disclose it | Mislead about it | **Avoid having one (use BATNA instead)** |
| Losses/gains | Accept one-sided losses | Demand one-sided gains | **Invent options for mutual gain** |
| Search | For answer they will accept | For answer you will accept | **Develop multiple options; decide later** |
| Insist on | Agreement | Your position | **Objective criteria** |
| Pressure | Yield to it | Apply it | **Yield to principle, not pressure** |
