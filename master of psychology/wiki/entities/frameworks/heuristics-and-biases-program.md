# Heuristics and Biases Research Program

**Type:** Framework / Research Program
**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Key Contribution:** Demonstrated that human judgment under uncertainty is governed by cognitive shortcuts (heuristics) that produce systematic, predictable errors (biases) — overturning the assumption of human rationality in economics and decision science.

## Overview

The heuristics and biases program, launched by [[tversky|Amos Tversky]] and [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]] in the early 1970s, is arguably the most influential research program in the history of behavioral science. Its central claim: when people make judgments under uncertainty, they do not compute probabilities. Instead, they rely on a small set of heuristics — mental shortcuts — that are efficient but systematically biased.

The 1974 *Science* paper identified three core heuristics — [[representativeness]], [[availability]], and [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — and cataloged the specific biases each produces. This framework became the foundation for behavioral economics, nudge theory, and modern decision science.

## The Three Heuristics and Their Biases

### [[representativeness]]
Judging probability by similarity to a prototype.
- [[base-rate-neglect]] — Ignoring prior probabilities
- [[law-of-small-numbers]] — Expecting small samples to mirror populations
- [[gamblers-fallacy]] — Expecting random sequences to self-correct
- Insensitivity to predictability
- [[illusion-of-validity]] — Confidence from pattern fit, not evidence quality
- [[regression-to-the-mean]] — Failure to recognize statistical regression

### [[availability]]
Judging frequency by ease of mental retrieval.
- Biases due to retrievability (familiarity, salience, recency)
- Biases due to search set effectiveness (cognitive search structure)
- Biases of imaginability (ease of scenario construction)
- [[illusory-correlation]] — Overestimating co-occurrence of associated events

### [[anchoring-and-adjustment]]
Estimating by insufficient adjustment from an initial value.
- Insufficient adjustment from arbitrary anchors
- Overestimation of conjunctive events (planning fallacy)
- Underestimation of disjunctive events (risk underestimation)
- [[overconfidence-in-calibration]] — Confidence intervals far too narrow

## Core Principles
1. **Heuristics are useful.** They work most of the time. They fail predictably.
2. **Biases are cognitive, not motivational.** They are not caused by wishful thinking, laziness, or ignorance. They persist in experts and in problems with no emotional content.
3. **Biases are systematic.** The errors go in predictable directions. This makes them scientifically tractable and practically addressable.
4. **Understanding biases can improve judgment.** The paper closes with the claim that "a better understanding of these heuristics and of the biases to which they lead could improve judgments and decisions in situations of uncertainty."
