# STEPPS Framework

**Type:** Framework
**Phase(s):** 6 (Advanced Persuasion)
**Key Contribution:** A six-principle, research-based framework for understanding and engineering contagious content — products, ideas, and behaviors that spread through word of mouth and social influence

## Overview

The STEPPS framework was developed by [[berger]] based on more than a decade of research at the Wharton School. It identifies six independent principles that drive word of mouth and social transmission. The name is an acronym: **S**ocial Currency, **T**riggers, **E**motion, **P**ublic, **P**ractical Value, **S**tories. The framework is presented in Berger's 2013 book *[[contagious]]*.

Unlike prior models of virality that focused on the characteristics of *people* who spread content (Gladwell's mavens, connectors, and salesmen), STEPPS focuses on the characteristics of the *content itself*. Berger's central argument is that the message matters more than the messenger — contagious content spreads regardless of who is doing the talking.

The six principles are independent and modular. Not all six are required; any combination can work. Berger describes them as "salad toppings" rather than a recipe — more principles are better, but a product or idea can succeed with just a few. The principles can be built into the product/idea itself or into the messaging around it.

## The Six Principles

### 1. [[social-currency]] — "We share things that make us look good"
People use word of mouth to achieve desired impressions. Three mechanisms: (a) inner remarkability — find what's surprising, novel, or pattern-breaking; (b) game mechanics — leverage points, levels, and leaderboards to create shareable achievements; (c) make people feel like insiders — use scarcity and exclusivity.

### 2. [[environmental-triggers]] — "Top of mind, tip of tongue"
Environmental stimuli remind people to think about products and ideas. Triggers drive ongoing word of mouth (vs. social currency's immediate buzz). Effective triggers are frequent, strongly linked, and proximate to the desired behavior. Growing the "habitat" (set of triggers) expands ongoing discussion.

### 3. [[high-arousal-emotions]] — "When we care, we share"
Physiological arousal — not emotional valence — drives sharing. High-arousal emotions (awe, excitement, anger, anxiety, amusement) increase sharing; low-arousal emotions (sadness, contentment) decrease it. Even physical exercise (a non-emotional source of arousal) increases sharing.

### 4. [[behavioral-residue]] (Public) — "Built to show, built to grow"
Observable behavior is imitable. Making products and ideas more publicly visible — through self-advertising products, behavioral residue (wristbands, stickers, email signatures), and making the private public — increases adoption through [[social-proof]]. Caution: making unwanted behavior visible can normalize it (anti-drug PSA backfire).

### 5. [[practical-value]] — "News you can use"
People share useful information to help others. [[prospect-theory]] explains why some deals seem more shareable (reference points, diminishing sensitivity, the Rule of 100). Packaging matters — short, focused, narrowly targeted content is more shareable. False information can spread as easily as true information.

### 6. [[trojan-horse-stories]] — "Information travels under the guise of idle chatter"
Stories are vessels that carry information inside engaging narratives. The key is *valuable* virality — the brand or message must be integral to the narrative, not extraneous. Critical details persist through retelling while extraneous ones are stripped away (Allport & Postman's rumor sharpening research).
