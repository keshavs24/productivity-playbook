# Attribution Theory

**Type:** Framework
**Phase(s):** 1
**Key Contribution:** The study of how people explain the causes of behavior — the most fundamental question in social perception.

## Overview

Attribution theory, founded by [[heider|Fritz Heider]] (1958), is the study of how people infer the causes of their own and others' behavior. The core question: is behavior caused by something **internal** (disposition, personality, character) or something **external** (situation, context, circumstances)?

This deceptively simple dichotomy generates a rich set of biases and errors that are central to understanding persuasion, communication, negotiation, and leadership.

## Core Components

### The Internal/External Dichotomy ([[heider]], 1958)
- **Internal attribution:** behavior reflects the person's disposition
- **External attribution:** behavior reflects the situation
- Which attribution we make profoundly shapes our impression and response

### The [[covariation-model]] (Kelley, 1967)
Three dimensions for determining attribution:
- **Consensus:** Do others behave this way toward this stimulus?
- **Distinctiveness:** Does this person behave this way toward other stimuli?
- **Consistency:** Does this person behave this way across time?

### The [[fundamental-attribution-error]] (Ross, 1977)
The systematic tendency to overweight internal causes and underweight external causes when explaining others' behavior. Driven by [[perceptual-salience]] and the two-step attribution process.

### The [[self-serving-attribution]]
Credit successes to internal causes; blame failures on external causes. Protects self-esteem.

### The [[just-world-belief]] (Lerner, 1980)
Defensive attribution: people deserve what they get. Leads to victim blaming.

### The [[bias-blind-spot]] (Pronin, 2002)
Meta-bias: believing others are more susceptible to biases than you are.
