# Emotional Intelligence Model (Salovey & Mayer, 1990)

**Type:** Framework
**Phase(s):** 1
**Key Contribution:** The first formal scientific framework for emotional intelligence, defining it as a set of interrelated mental abilities organized into three branches with specific sub-processes.

## Overview

The Salovey and Mayer (1990) Emotional Intelligence Model is the foundational framework that established emotional intelligence as a legitimate area of psychological inquiry. It defines EI as "the subset of social intelligence that involves the ability to monitor one's own and others' feelings and emotions, to discriminate among them and to use this information to guide one's thinking and actions." The model organizes EI into three major branches, each with sub-components targeting processes in the self and in others.

The model draws on the functionalist tradition in emotion theory, which views emotions not as disorganized interruptions of rational thought but as organized, adaptive responses that cross physiological, cognitive, motivational, and experiential subsystems. The framework integrates previously scattered research literatures — alexithymia, nonverbal perception, empathy, mood regulation, affect and cognition — into a coherent theoretical structure.

## The Three-Branch Structure

### Branch 1: Appraisal and Expression of Emotion

**In the Self:**
- **Verbal** — Introspection and clear articulation of emotional experience. Deficits here manifest as [[alexithymia]]. Measured via Beth Israel Psychosomatic Questionnaire, Toronto Alexithymia Scale, State and Trait Meta-Mood Scales.
- **Nonverbal** — Emotional expressiveness through facial expression, gesture, tone. Measured via Affect Expression Rating Scale, Affective Communication Test. Correlates positively with [[empathy]] and inversely with depression.

**In Others:**
- **Nonverbal Perception** — Reading emotions from others' faces, bodies, voices. Measured via Affect Sensitivity Test, CARAT, PONS. Women generally score higher (except for anger recognition). Not a unidimensional construct.
- **[[empathy]]** — Comprehending another's feelings and re-experiencing them. Involves primary circular reactions (innate) and classical empathic conditioning (learned). Both a disposition and a skill. Empathy scales may measure access to one's own feelings as well as responsiveness to others'.

### Branch 2: Regulation of Emotion

**In the Self:**
- Meta-experiences of mood: a regulatory system that monitors, evaluates, and sometimes changes mood states
- Mood regulation through associate selection, self-evaluation maintenance, helping others ("negative state relief"), countervailing evaluations of negative moods
- Mood maintenance (prolonging positive states) and mood repair (terminating negative states) — the earliest evidence for self-regulation of mood came from asymmetry in mood's effects on memory
- Aesthetic experience as emotional practice — seeking sorrow through art builds emotional tolerance

**In Others:**
- Impression management (Goffman): presenting self to guide others' emotional reactions
- Emotional labor (Hochschild): commercialized regulation of others' emotions
- Charisma as emotional regulation of followers (Wasielewski)
- Ingratiation and strategic emotional communication
- **Dual-edged:** Can be used prosocially (motivating charismatically) or antisocially (manipulation, sociopathy)

### Branch 3: Utilization of Emotion

- **Flexible Planning** — Mood swings generate varied estimates of future events, breaking cognitive set and enabling broader scenario generation
- **Creative Thinking** — Positive mood facilitates categorization, unusual associations (remote associates task), discovery of organizing principles (Isen et al.)
- **Redirected Attention** — Strong emotions reprioritize processing to more immediately important problems; affect allocates attentional resources
- **Motivating Emotions** — Anxiety motivates preparation; good moods increase confidence and persistence; positive attitudes construct better interpersonal outcomes

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Thorndike's social intelligence (1920), Gardner's personal intelligences (1983), Wechsler's broad definition of intelligence, functionalist emotion theory (Leeper, 1948), appraisal theories of emotion
- **Extended by:** Salovey and Mayer's own revised four-branch model (1997), Goleman's popularized EI model (1995), Bar-On's EQ-i model
- **Contradicts:** Traditional Western view of emotions as disorganized interruptions; Spearman's "g" (if interpreted as requiring all intelligences to intercorrelate; the authors argue EI need not conform to "g")
- **Applied in:** Clinical assessment, leadership development, educational psychology, organizational behavior
