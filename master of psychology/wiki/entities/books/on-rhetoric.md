# On Rhetoric: A Theory of Civic Discourse

**Type:** Book
**Phase(s):** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Key Contribution:** The foundational treatise on persuasion, identifying the three modes of persuasion (ethos, pathos, logos), three genres of rhetoric, the enthymeme as the primary tool of logical proof, and the first systematic psychology of emotions

## Overview

*On Rhetoric* (*Peri rhetorike*, also known as *Ars Rhetorica* or *Art of Rhetoric*) is [[aristotle]]'s treatise on the art of persuasion, composed over multiple periods (early lectures from the 350s B.C.E., with revisions through the 330s) and translated here by George A. Kennedy (Oxford University Press, 2nd ed., 2007). It is the most systematic and influential work on rhetoric in Western intellectual history.

The treatise is structured in three books:
- **Book 1** defines rhetoric, introduces the [[three-modes-of-persuasion]] and the [[three-genres-of-rhetoric]], and provides specific topics (*idia*) for deliberative, epideictic, and judicial rhetoric
- **Book 2** continues the discussion of *pisteis* by analyzing [[ethos]] (the speaker's character), [[pathos]] (the audience's emotions — the famous emotion chapters), and [[logos]] (logical argument through [[enthymeme]]s, paradigms, maxims, and twenty-eight common [[topoi]])
- **Book 3** (not covered in this ingestion) treats delivery, prose style, and the arrangement of speeches

The work was an esoteric text — lecture notes for students at the Lyceum who had already studied dialectic. It was never formally published in Aristotle's lifetime. Despite this, it influenced Cicero, Quintilian, and virtually every subsequent theorist of communication, argumentation, and persuasion.

## Key Ideas

- **[[Three-modes-of-persuasion]]** — ethos, pathos, logos as the exhaustive set of artistic means of persuasion
- **[[Enthymeme]]** — the rhetorical syllogism, the "body of persuasion," drawn from probable premises with the audience supplying unstated assumptions
- **[[Three-genres-of-rhetoric]]** — deliberative (future, advantageous), judicial (past, just), epideictic (present, honorable)
- **[[Topoi]]** — 28 common lines of argument and 9 fallacious patterns
- **[[Rhetorical-emotions]]** — the earliest systematic psychology of emotions: anger, calmness, friendliness, enmity, fear, confidence, shame, shamelessness, kindliness, pity, indignation, envy, emulation
- **Rhetoric as counterpart of dialectic** — both deal with probabilities and commonly held opinions, but rhetoric addresses a popular audience through continuous speech while dialectic addresses an interlocutor through question and answer
- **Moral neutrality** — rhetoric can be used for good or ill; the art itself is value-neutral, though Aristotle insists truth and justice are naturally stronger than their opposites

## Connections

- **Builds on:** Plato's *Phaedrus* (philosophical rhetoric adapted to practice); Plato's *Gorgias* (responding to the critique that rhetoric is mere flattery); the early rhetorical handbooks (criticized but incorporated); the sophist tradition (systematized)
- **Extended by:** Cicero's *De Oratore* and *On Invention*; Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria*; the entire Western tradition of rhetoric, communication, and argumentation theory
- **Contradicts:** The handbook writers (for narrowness); Isocrates (for emphasis on style over substance)
- **Modern parallels:** The [[six-principles-of-influence]] (Cialdini) can be partially mapped onto Aristotle's framework — [[authority-principle]] ≈ ethos, [[liking-principle]] ≈ ethos + pathos, [[social-proof]] ≈ logos from endoxa
