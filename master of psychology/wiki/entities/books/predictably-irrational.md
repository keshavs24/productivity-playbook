# Predictably Irrational: The Hidden Forces That Shape Our Decisions

**Type:** Book (Revised and Expanded Edition)
**Phase(s):** 2
**Key Contribution:** Accessible, experiment-driven demonstration that human irrationality is not random but systematic and predictable, with direct implications for personal decisions, business, and public policy.

## Overview

*Predictably Irrational* by [[ariely|Dan Ariely]] (HarperCollins, 2008; revised and expanded edition 2010) is a popular science book that translates behavioral economics research into vivid, relatable narratives. Each chapter is built around one or more original experiments conducted by Ariely and colleagues, demonstrating a specific way in which human behavior departs from rational-agent economic models.

The book's core argument challenges the foundational assumption of standard economics -- that humans are rational actors who maximize utility. Ariely shows that our irrational behaviors are **systematic** (not random), **predictable** (following identifiable patterns), and **repeatable** (we make the same mistakes again and again). This predictability opens the door to designing better environments, policies, and personal strategies.

The book is a supplementary source (2-S1) for Phase 2 of the curriculum, complementing the more technical treatment in [[judgment-in-managerial-decision-making|Bazerman & Moore]] and [[choices-values-and-frames|Kahneman & Tversky's Choices, Values, and Frames]].

## Key Ideas

- Humans lack an internal "value meter" and rely on relative comparisons, which can be manipulated ([[decoy-effect]])
- Initial prices are arbitrary but create coherent reference systems ([[arbitrary-coherence]])
- Zero is qualitatively different from any positive price ([[zero-price-effect]])
- Social and market exchange systems are incompatible; mixing them destroys social norms ([[social-norms-vs-market-norms]])
- We cannot predict our own behavior in emotional states ([[hot-cold-empathy-gap]])
- External constraints beat willpower for self-control ([[procrastination-and-precommitment]])
- Ownership -- even virtual ownership -- dramatically inflates perceived value ([[endowment-effect]], [[virtual-ownership]])
- We irrationally chase closing options even when it costs us ([[keeping-doors-open]])
- Expectations reshape actual sensory experience, not just our interpretation of it ([[expectation-assimilation]])
- Price is a placebo -- expensive things genuinely work better because we expect them to ([[placebo-pricing]])
- Trust is a fragile public good eroded by a few bad actors ([[cycle-of-distrust]])
- Most people cheat a little but not a lot; moral reminders eliminate cheating entirely ([[dishonesty-fudge-factor]], [[moral-reminder-effect]])
- Cheating increases dramatically when the medium is one step removed from cash
- Public decisions differ from private ones -- social signaling corrupts personal preference ([[beer-and-free-choice]])
