# Phase 3: Motivation Science

## Purpose

Phase 3 shifts from understanding how people judge and decide (Phase 2) to understanding what drives them to act in the first place. Where Phase 2 documented systematic errors in judgment, Phase 3 asks: what makes people persist, engage, grow, and flourish — or conversely, disengage, burn out, and stagnate? The central question: what are the fundamental psychological needs that energize and direct human behavior, and how do social contexts either support or undermine them?

## Emerging Themes

### 1. Motivation Quality Matters More Than Quantity
SDT's central insight: the type of motivation (autonomous vs. controlled) predicts outcomes better than the amount. This directly challenges simple incentive models and connects to Phase 2's [[undermining-effect]] and [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]].

### 2. Three Universal Psychological Needs
Autonomy, competence, and relatedness operate cross-culturally as psychological "vitamins." Their satisfaction predicts wellness; their frustration predicts pathology. This provides the motivational foundation that was missing from Phase 2's purely cognitive framework.

### 3. The Internalization Continuum
External regulation → introjected → identified → integrated → intrinsic motivation. Not all extrinsic motivation is bad — the key is how far along the internalization continuum a regulation has progressed. Introjected regulation maps onto Phase 2's [[want-should-conflict]].

### 4. Self-Efficacy as Generative Capability
Bandura's self-efficacy is not "do you have the skill?" but "can you orchestrate your skills under varying conditions?" This reframes capability as belief-mediated and argues that optimistic self-appraisal is adaptive — directly challenging Phase 2's treatment of overconfidence as purely a bias.

### 5. SDT and Self-Efficacy Are Complementary
SDT's competence need addresses the experience of effectance; Bandura's self-efficacy addresses the belief that one can perform specific actions. SDT focuses on contextual supports for need satisfaction; self-efficacy theory focuses on the four sources that build beliefs. Together they provide a complete motivation science.

### 6. Direct Productivity Playbook Applications
- Autonomy-supportive management produces 3:1 ROI (Deci et al. 1989)
- Performance-contingent pay undermines intrinsic motivation in field settings
- Intrinsic goal framing enhances learning depth
- Self-efficacy training programs reduce absenteeism and improve job performance
- Collective efficacy predicts team-level performance
- The acquirable-skill framing produces resilient performance under organizational pressure

### 7. Bridges to Later Phases
Phase 3 → Phase 5 (Negotiation): Autonomy-supportive negotiation styles should produce better outcomes
Phase 3 → Phase 6 (Persuasion): Understanding autonomous vs. controlled motivation makes persuasion more ethical and effective
Phase 3 → Phase 7 (Leadership): Autonomy-supportive leadership directly predicts work engagement

## Phase 3 Synthesis

Phase 3 provides two complementary theories of what drives human behavior. Ryan and Deci's Self-Determination Theory is an organismic, needs-based framework: humans naturally tend toward growth, integration, and well-being when their needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied. When these needs are thwarted — by controlling environments, conditional regard, or extrinsic goal pursuit — people disengage, develop compensatory motives, and suffer. SDT's six mini-theories (CET, OIT, COT, BPNT, GCT, RMT) each address a different facet of this dynamic.

Bandura's Self-Efficacy Theory complements SDT by focusing on the belief dimension: it's not enough for needs to be satisfied — people must also believe in their capabilities. The four sources of self-efficacy (mastery experience, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, physiological states) and four mediating processes (cognitive, motivational, affective, selection) provide a practical framework for building the confidence that turns motivation into action.

Together, these theories complete the foundation laid in Phases 1-2. Phase 1 showed how the mind processes social information. Phase 2 showed how judgment and decision-making systematically err. Phase 3 shows what energizes and directs behavior in the first place — and crucially, how social contexts can be designed to support (rather than undermine) human flourishing. This has direct implications for the Productivity Playbook: gamification must support autonomy, build competence, and foster relatedness, not merely add extrinsic incentives.
