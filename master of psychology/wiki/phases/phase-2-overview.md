# Phase 2: Decision Science

## Purpose

Phase 2 deepens the cognitive foundations of Phase 1 into a rigorous decision science. Where Phase 1 introduced heuristics, biases, and influence principles at a foundational level, Phase 2 applies these insights systematically to managerial decision making, investment, negotiation, and ethics — while also grounding them in the cognitive architecture of problem-solving, expertise, reasoning, and decision-making. The central question shifts from "how does the mind work?" to "how do these cognitive mechanisms produce specific, predictable errors in real-world professional contexts — and what can we do about them?"

## Emerging Themes

### 1. From Individual Biases to Systemic Bounds
Phase 2 organizes individual biases into five categories of bounded judgment: bounded rationality, bounded willpower, bounded self-interest, bounded awareness, and bounded ethicality. This taxonomic framework reveals that cognitive errors are not random — they cluster into predictable families with shared mechanisms.

### 2. Ethics Without Intent
Bounded ethicality is perhaps the most practically important concept in Phase 2. Most unethical behavior in organizations is not deliberate corruption but the product of cognitive biases operating below awareness — overclaiming credit, in-group favoritism, implicit bias, and motivated blindness to conflicts of interest.

### 3. Negotiation as Applied Decision Science
Chapters 11-12 of Bazerman bridge decision science and negotiation, showing how the same biases that distort individual decisions (framing, overconfidence, anchoring, self-serving reasoning) systematically prevent negotiators from reaching efficient agreements. This prepares the ground for Phase 5's deep dive into negotiation strategy.

### 4. Debiasing Is Possible but Difficult
Bazerman's final chapter provides cautious optimism: biases can be reduced through structured interventions, but passive experience alone does not eliminate them. True improvement requires deliberate learning, external decision aids, and institutional design.

### 5. The Cognitive Architecture Beneath the Biases
Anderson's chapters reveal *why* heuristics exist: the human cognitive system has finite working memory, learns through chunking and proceduralization, and reasons via mental models rather than formal logic. These architectural constraints make heuristic-based judgment not just a flaw but an inevitability — and they explain why expertise (thousands of hours of deliberate practice building accurate chunks) is the only reliable path to better intuitive judgment.

### 6. Prospect Theory Reaches Maturity
The CVF collection shows prospect theory evolving from a single paper (1979) to a comprehensive framework: cumulative weighting functions, the fourfold pattern, mental accounting, endowment effects, status quo bias, and field evidence from cab drivers. By Phase 2, prospect theory is no longer a laboratory curiosity but a proven model of real-world economic behavior.

### 7. Models Beat Experts
Dawes's "robust beauty" finding — that even crude linear models outperform expert clinical judgment — is one of the most replicated and most resisted findings in all of psychology. It connects directly to Bazerman's debiasing strategies and challenges the common assumption that more experience automatically produces better judgment.

---

### 8. Behavioral Economics Goes Experimental
Ariely demonstrates that the biases documented by Kahneman/Tversky and systematized by Bazerman can be tested with clever, vivid experiments. His work adds several concepts not found in the more theoretical sources: the decoy effect, arbitrary coherence, social vs. market norms, the zero-price effect, and the dishonesty fudge factor. These extend behavioral economics from a descriptive theory of errors to an experimental science of predictable human behavior.

### 9. Fairness and Trust as Economic Constraints
The Kahneman/Knetsch/Thaler fairness research and Ariely's trust/dishonesty chapters reveal that economic behavior is constrained by deeply held fairness norms and trust dynamics. Firms cannot simply maximize profit — they face public backlash for perceived unfairness. And trust, once eroded by market-norm intrusions or dishonesty, is extremely difficult to rebuild. These findings bridge to Phase 5 (negotiation) and Phase 6 (persuasion).

## Phase 2 Synthesis

Phase 2 covers five complementary perspectives on human judgment and decision-making. Bazerman provides the applied managerial lens — how biases play out in organizations, investments, negotiations, and ethics. The Kahneman/Tversky collections provide the theoretical deepening — cumulative prospect theory, mental accounting, simulation heuristics, field evidence, and fairness norms. Anderson provides the cognitive science substrate — how problems are represented and solved, how expertise develops, how reasoning works (and fails). Ariely provides the experimental behavioral economics perspective — vivid demonstrations of irrationality in everyday consumer and social contexts. Together they paint a complete picture: the cognitive architecture (Anderson) produces systematic biases (Kahneman/Tversky) that play out in predictable ways in professional contexts (Bazerman) and everyday life (Ariely) — and can be partially corrected through structured intervention.

The phase introduces over 120 concepts and deepens many carried forward from Phase 1 (prospect theory, loss aversion, framing effects, and anchoring all receive substantial confidence boosts from multiple-source confirmation). The bridge to Phase 3 (Motivation Science) is already visible in Anderson's expertise research and Ariely's procrastination/self-control chapters — deliberate practice requires sustained motivation, and understanding the cognitive and emotional forces that drive procrastination is essential to designing effective self-regulation strategies.
