# Framing Effects

**Phase(s):** 1, 2, 4, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.95

## Definition

Framing effects occur when **the same objective information, presented differently, leads to different choices or judgments**. The way a problem is framed — what is presented as the reference point, what is labeled a gain vs. a loss, what is highlighted vs. backgrounded — systematically changes preferences. This violates the invariance principle (rational agents should have the same preference regardless of how options are described).

## Key Findings

- Problems 11 & 12: Identical final wealth states, framed as gains vs. losses, produced opposite risk preferences ([[summary-1-02-prospect-theory]])
- The [[isolation-effect]] is the cognitive mechanism: people cancel shared components and evaluate only what's distinctive — but what counts as "shared" depends on the frame
- Framing determines the reference point, which determines what counts as a gain vs. a loss, which (via [[loss-aversion]] and the [[reflection-effect]]) determines risk preferences

## Mechanisms

Framing works through [[reference-dependence]] and the editing phase of [[prospect-theory]]. The frame sets the reference point. The reference point determines the coding of outcomes as gains or losses. [[loss-aversion]] and the [[reflection-effect]] then produce different preferences depending on the coding. The entire chain is triggered by something as simple as word choice or presentation order.

## Applications

- **Communication (Phase 4):** How you frame a message — "90% survival rate" vs. "10% mortality rate" — determines its persuasive impact. Same data, different framing, different reactions.
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Framing proposals as "what you gain" vs. "what you avoid losing" shifts the counterpart's risk preferences. A negotiator who frames the other side's choice as a loss creates risk-seeking behavior (they'll gamble to avoid the loss).
- **Persuasion (Phase 6):** Framing is one of the most powerful tools of influence. Pre-suasion (Cialdini, Phase 6) is essentially the science of setting the frame before the message.
- **Pricing:** "$5 discount for cash" vs. "$5 surcharge for credit" — same price difference, different frames, different consumer reactions
- **Medical decisions:** Patients choose differently when told "90% survive surgery" (gain frame) vs. "10% die in surgery" (loss frame) — even doctors are affected
- **Policy:** "Lives saved" vs. "lives lost" framing changes public support for the same policy (Tversky & Kahneman's Asian Disease Problem, 1981)

## Bazerman & Moore Extensions (Chapters 5-6)

Bazerman and Moore provide extensive managerial applications and new evidence for framing effects ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch5]], [[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch6]]):

### Portfolio Irrationality from Sequential Framing
Tversky & Kahneman (1981, n=150): When two decisions are framed separately (one involving gains, one losses), 73% of people chose a dominated portfolio. Only 3% chose the dominant combination. This means sequential framing in organizations (sales framing as gains, credit framing as losses) produces systematically inferior aggregate decisions.

### Pseudocertainty
[[pseudocertainty]] -- the perception of certainty regarding a subset of uncertainties -- is a powerful extension of the [[certainty-effect]]. Slovic et al. (1982) vaccination study: pseudocertainty framing increased vaccine uptake by 43% with identical objective risk reduction.

### Insurance Framing
The word "insurance" triggers social norms that override rational analysis. Slovic et al. (1982): describing a sure loss as an "insurance premium" vs. a "certain loss" dramatically increased acceptance. Extended warranties are mostly dealer profit ($555 of $795 in one Nissan case).

### Rebate/Bonus Framing
Epley et al. (2006): Identical $25 payments framed as "bonus" produced 4.6x more spending ($11.16) than "rebate" framing ($2.43). "Bonus" creates surplus cash imagery; "rebate" conveys status quo restoration.

### Transactional Utility
[[transactional-utility]] (Thaler, 1985): People evaluate transactions through deal quality relative to expectations. Same beer costs $2.65 at a resort hotel vs. $1.50 at a grocery store because the expected "fair price" differs.

### Joint vs. Separate Evaluation
[[preference-reversal]] between separate and joint evaluation: the "want" self (System 1) dominates separate evaluation while the "should" self (System 2) dominates joint evaluation. Hard-to-evaluate attributes are underweighted in separate evaluation.

### Real-World Generalization
Framing effects now documented in: taxi driver labor supply (Camerer et al., 1997), telephone line insurance (Thaler & Ziemba, 1988), professional golfers' putting (Pope & Schweitzer, 2011), football betting (Simmons et al., 2011), investment disposition effect (Odean, 1998), and capuchin monkey behavior (Chen et al., 2006).

### Evolutionary Explanation
Rayo and Becker (2007): Evolution programmed reference-dependent framing because the subjective utility scale has limited sensitivity (declining marginal utility). The reference point must readjust to maintain motivation, creating the hedonic treadmill (Brickman & Campbell, 1971).

## Anderson's Neuroscience of Framing (Ch.11)

Anderson ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch11]]) adds the cognitive neuroscience perspective on framing:

- **Trolley problem neuroscience** (Greene, Sommerville, Nystrom, Darley, & Cohen, 2001): "impersonal" moral dilemmas (pulling a switch) activate parietal regions associated with cold calculation; "personal" dilemmas (pushing someone off a bridge) activate ventromedial prefrontal regions associated with emotion -- different framings of the same moral calculus engage fundamentally different brain systems
- **Justification as mechanism**: Shafir (1993) proposed people make decisions based on which choice is easiest to **justify** rather than which is objectively best. In custody decisions, Parent B (with extreme positive AND negative qualities) was both awarded and denied custody more often than average Parent A -- the same parent was chosen whether the question was "award to" or "deny from" because extreme qualities provide justification for either action
- **Default effects as framing**: Organ donation, 401(k) enrollment, flu vaccination rates are dramatically higher when the default is opt-out rather than opt-in (Johnson, Steffel, & Goldstein, 2005; Choi, Laibson, Madrian, & Metrick, 2003) -- people avoid the decision itself to avoid having to justify an active choice
- **Choice architecture effects**: When a third option is added, the similarity, attraction, and compromise effects all operate through the mechanism of justification (Oppenheimer & Kelso, 2015; Mercier & Sperber, 2011) -- see [[summary-2-09-anderson-ch11]] for details

## Tversky & Kahneman's Formal Analysis (Ch. 12, CVF)

Tversky and Kahneman ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch12-rational-choice-framing]]) provide the most systematic treatment of framing effects within the context of rational choice theory:

### Framing Violates the Most Basic Axiom of Rationality
The four axioms of expected utility theory can be ranked by normative appeal: cancellation (weakest), transitivity, dominance, and **invariance/[[extensionality]]** (strongest). Framing effects violate invariance -- the most compelling axiom, so basic it is typically assumed rather than stated. This means framing effects are not a minor anomaly but a **fundamental challenge to rational choice theory**.

### Transparency Determines Compliance
Normative principles are obeyed when problems are "transparent" (the logical structure is obvious) and violated when "nontransparent." Framing determines transparency. Example: 74% chose the safe option in a sequential (transparent) gamble, but 58% chose the risky option in a formally identical single-stage (nontransparent) version -- a [[dominance-violation]].

### No Theory Can Be Both Normatively Adequate and Descriptively Accurate
Because invariance is normatively indispensable but descriptively invalid, "the dream of constructing a theory that is acceptable both descriptively and normatively appears unrealizable." [[prospect-theory]] is the only model that accommodates violations of both invariance and dominance, precisely because it makes no normative claims.

### Bolstering Assumptions Fail
Common defenses of rationality -- incentives, learning, and market forces -- have weak empirical support. Incentives prevent errors from inattention but not from misperception. Learning requires conditions (immediate feedback, repeatability) that rarely hold for important decisions. Markets fail to correct some errors even in high-incentive, high-expertise settings (racetrack place-and-show markets).

## Ariely's Perceptual Framing Evidence (Predictably Irrational, Chs. 10-11)

Ariely ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]], [[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]]) extends framing effects from decision-making into perceptual experience:

- **Price as frame:** A $2.50 painkiller (vitamin C) reduced pain for almost all participants; the same pill at $0.10 reduced pain for only half. Price frames quality expectations, and those expectations alter the actual physiological experience.
- **Container framing:** Identical coffee condiments in fancy glass containers vs. Styrofoam cups — the container framed the coffee experience, producing higher ratings, willingness to pay, and recommendations for the "fancy container" condition.
- **Timing as frame:** Telling participants about vinegar in beer *before* vs. *after* tasting produced radically different experiences from identical stimuli. The frame must be set before the experience; post-experience framing has no effect.
- **Context framing:** Joshua Bell playing in a Metro station vs. a concert hall — the performance frame (street performer vs. virtuoso) determined whether people perceived genius-level musicianship.
- **Key extension:** Classic framing effects change *decisions* between options. Ariely shows framing also changes *experiences* of the chosen option — the frame doesn't just change what you pick, it changes what you feel.

## Open Questions
- Are there "natural" or default frames that people gravitate toward? Can you predict which frame a naive decision-maker will adopt?
- How does expertise interact with framing? Do experts resist framing effects, or are they equally susceptible?
- What happens when the framer's intent is transparent -- does awareness of framing reduce its effect? (Evidence suggests: only partially)
- How do framing effects interact with [[bounded-awareness]]? Does bounded awareness determine which frame people naturally adopt?
- Can deliberate reflection about framing break the perceptual framing effect? Ariely's SoBe experiment suggests that consumers who reflect on the price-quality relationship are less susceptible — but the effect is automatic by default.
