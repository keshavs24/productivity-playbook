# Practical Value

**Phase(s):** 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Practical value is the principle that people share useful information to help others. While [[social-currency]] is about how sharing makes the *sender* look, practical value is about how sharing helps the *receiver*. People pass along tips, advice, deals, and useful knowledge because helping others feels good, strengthens social bonds, and is a modern form of prosocial behavior. Practical value is the fifth principle of [[berger]]'s [[stepps-framework]]: "News you can use."

## Key Findings

- **Ken Craig's corn-shucking video** went viral (5+ million views) despite being made by an 86-year-old with no social media presence, no remarkability, and no emotional hook — pure practical value drove sharing.
- **Hikers talked about vacuum cleaners** instead of the beautiful mountain scenery because practical product recommendations were more useful to share.
- **[[prospect-theory]] explains deal perception.** People evaluate prices relative to reference points, not in absolute terms. A grill at $250 (marked down from $350) seems like a better deal than the same grill at $240 (marked down from $255), even though $240 is cheaper.
- **The word "sale" alone increases purchases by 50%** even when prices are identical to non-sale versions (Anderson & Simester research).
- **The Rule of 100:** Under $100, percentage discounts seem larger; over $100, absolute dollar discounts seem larger.
- **Quantity limits increase sales by 50%+.** "Limit three per customer" signals the deal is so good the store might run out.
- **Narrower content can be more viral** than broad content because it reminds people of a specific person who would benefit, creating a stronger urge to share.
- **False information spreads as easily as true information** because the drive to share helpful things doesn't include a verification step (vaccine-autism hoax).

## Mechanisms

### The Psychology of Deals
[[prospect-theory]] (Kahneman & Tversky) provides the framework:
- **Reference points:** People don't evaluate prices in absolute terms. They compare to an expected price. The same $10 feels different depending on the reference point.
- **Diminishing sensitivity:** The same change has less impact farther from the reference point. Saving $10 on a $35 item (29%) motivates action; saving $10 on a $650 item (1.5%) doesn't.
- **Loss aversion and reference point manipulation:** Infomercials set artificially high reference points ("you might expect to pay $200") to make the actual price feel like a steal.
- **The Rule of 100:** A framing heuristic — for products under $100, frame discounts as percentages (larger number); for products over $100, frame them in dollars (larger number).

### Factors That Make Deals Seem More Valuable
1. **Exceed expectations** — the deal should be surprising relative to the reference point
2. **Scarcity and restrictions** — time limits, quantity limits, and exclusive access make deals seem more valuable (counterintuitively, restrictions *increase* rather than decrease demand)
3. **Visibility** — the easier it is for others to see the savings, the more likely they are to share

### Beyond Deals — Useful Information
Practical value extends to any useful information: health tips, recipes, advice, how-to knowledge, expert recommendations. Vanguard's *MoneyWhys* newsletter packages financial expertise into shareable emails. *New York Times* health and education articles are among the most shared because they provide actionable advice.

**Packaging matters:** Short, focused lists (5 ways to lose weight, 10 dating tips) are more shareable than long, rambling articles. Information should be easy to scan and forward.

**Audience targeting:** Content that is obviously relevant to a specific person may be more viral than broadly relevant content, because it triggers the thought "I know exactly who needs to see this."

## Applications

- **Pricing strategy:** Use the Rule of 100 to frame discounts maximally. Set reference points strategically. Consider quantity limits to increase perceived value.
- **Content marketing:** Package expertise into short, useful, focused content that people can easily forward.
- **Product positioning:** Emphasize how the product saves people time, money, or effort — the practical value that makes recipients want to pass it along.
- **Audience segmentation:** Narrowly relevant content for specific audiences may spread more than broadly relevant content for everyone.
- **Truth verification:** Be aware that false but "helpful" information spreads easily. Verify claims before sharing.
