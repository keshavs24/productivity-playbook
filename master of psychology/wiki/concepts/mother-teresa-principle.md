# Mother Teresa Principle

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Mother Teresa Principle captures the psychological finding that people respond with greater empathy, generosity, and action when confronted with a single, identifiable individual than when presented with statistical descriptions of mass suffering. Named for Mother Teresa's statement, "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will," the principle reveals a fundamental asymmetry in human emotional processing: we feel for individuals but go numb when faced with large numbers. The concept is closely related to the "identifiable victim effect" in behavioral economics and is presented by [[heath-brothers]] in [[made-to-stick]] as a core principle of making ideas emotionally sticky.

## Key Findings

- **Carnegie Mellon Save the Children study (2004):** Participants who read about a single child (Rokia, a seven-year-old girl from Mali) donated an average of $2.38, while those who read statistics about millions of suffering African children donated only $1.14 -- less than half
- **The combination effect is devastating:** When participants received BOTH the Rokia story AND the statistics, they donated $1.43 -- almost a dollar less than Rokia alone. The statistics contaminated the emotional response. Somehow, evidence of massive human suffering actually made people less charitable
- **The analytical priming study:** Researchers primed some participants with analytical questions ("If an object travels at five feet per minute, how far will it travel in 360 seconds?") and others with emotional questions ("Write down one word to describe how you feel when you hear the word 'baby'"). Both groups then received the Rokia letter. Analytically primed people gave $1.26; emotionally primed people gave $2.34. The mere act of calculation reduced charitable giving
- **Key insight:** Once we put on our "Analytical Hat," we react to emotional appeals differently. We hinder our ability to feel. The act of thinking analytically suppresses the emotional circuitry that drives charitable action
- Charities have long exploited this principle: you sponsor a specific child, not "African poverty." Farm Sanctuary lets donors "adopt a chicken" ($10/month), a goat ($25), or a cow ($50). No one wants to donate to the "General Administrative Fund"

## Mechanisms

1. **Emotional specificity:** Individual stories activate [[empathy]] circuits; statistics activate analytical processing, which suppresses emotional response
2. **The "drop in the bucket" effect:** When the scale of a problem is presented statistically, potential helpers may feel their small contribution is meaningless against the enormity of the problem -- leading to inaction
3. **Dual-process interference:** Analytical thinking and emotional feeling operate through partially competing cognitive systems. Activating the analytical system dampens emotional responsiveness (consistent with [[emotional-intelligence]] research on the interplay between cognition and emotion)
4. **Concreteness and imagery:** A single individual is concrete and imageable; millions of people are abstract and unimaginable. This connects to the broader principle that [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]] differ in their persuasive power

## Applications

- **Charity and fundraising:** Always lead with an individual story, never with statistics. If you must include statistics, present them only after the emotional connection has been established, and frame them in human-scale terms
- **Public health communication:** Present the impact on a single identifiable person rather than aggregate mortality data. A story about one patient is more motivating than a chart showing 10,000 deaths
- **Business presentations:** When making a case for change, lead with a single customer story or employee story before presenting the data. The IDEO hospital video (showing the patient experience from the patient's eyes) was more motivating than any satisfaction survey
- **Policy advocacy:** The identifiable victim effect explains why one trapped miner generates more rescue spending than statistical evidence about mine safety. Advocates should put a face on the issue
- **Avoiding the trap:** Be aware that presenting statistics alongside emotional appeals may actually reduce their effectiveness. If data is necessary, present it separately or frame it in terms of a single person's experience
- **Teaching and training:** Present one detailed case study rather than surveying many. The [[story-as-simulation]] approach leverages the same principle -- we simulate the experience of one person, not a statistical aggregate

## Open Questions

- Can the Mother Teresa Principle be reconciled with effective altruism's emphasis on maximizing aggregate impact? Is there a way to harness both emotional engagement and rational resource allocation?
- Does the analytical suppression effect vary across cultures, or is it a universal feature of human cognition?
- How does repeated exposure to individual stories affect the principle -- does compassion fatigue eventually set in?
