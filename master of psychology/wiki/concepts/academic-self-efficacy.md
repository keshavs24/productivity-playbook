# Academic Self-Efficacy

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Academic self-efficacy refers to students' beliefs in their capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to master academic subjects and regulate their own learning activities (Bandura, 1997, Ch.6). It operates at multiple levels: efficacy for specific academic tasks (e.g., mathematical self-efficacy), efficacy for self-regulated learning, and efficacy for academic achievement in general. It is distinct from actual cognitive ability -- children of equal ability but differing efficacy beliefs show markedly different academic outcomes.

## Key Findings

- Collins (1982): At every ability level (low, medium, high), children with higher mathematical self-efficacy discarded faulty strategies faster, solved more problems, reworked more failures, and performed more accurately than equal-ability peers with lower efficacy (Fig. 6.1) ([[summary-3-11-se-ch6-cognitive-functioning]])
- Perceived self-efficacy is a better predictor of intellectual performance than skills alone (Bandura, 1997, p.216)
- Path analyses consistently show efficacy beliefs influence academic performance both directly and through effects on quality of analytic thinking, persistence, and personal goals (Schunk, 1984a, Fig. 6.2)
- The unique contribution of efficacy beliefs to academic performance is highly replicable across diverse determinants (Hackett, 1985; Pajares & Kranzler, 1995; Pajares & Miller, 1994a; Randhawa, Beamer, & Lundberg, 1993)
- Proximal subgoals build academic efficacy far more effectively than distal goals (Bandura & Schunk, 1981): children with proximal subgoals made rapid progress and developed strong mathematical efficacy; distal goals had no demonstrable effects
- Self-efficacy cultivates [[intrinsic-motivation]]: moderate threshold efficacy predicts high intrinsic interest; supreme self-assurance may render activities unchallenging
- Academic self-efficacy predicts career choice and development: it predicts the scope of career options seriously considered, occupational interests, enrollment in relevant courses, perseverance, and academic success ([[summary-3-11-se-ch10-organizational-functioning]])
- When perceived self-efficacy is removed from analyses, the relation between academic anxiety and performance disappears or is markedly diminished (Pajares & Johnson, 1994)
- Self-regulatory efficacy (ability to mobilize, direct, and sustain one's learning activities) is a distinct and critical facet that predicts academic achievement through its effects on academic goals (Zimmerman, Bandura, & Martinez-Pons, 1984a, Fig. 6.3)

## Mechanisms

Academic self-efficacy operates through all four [[self-efficacy-mediating-processes]]:

1. **Cognitive:** High-efficacy students visualize success scenarios, use more effective cognitive strategies, employ metacognitive self-regulation, and engage in verbal self-guidance during problem solving
2. **Motivational:** Efficacy beliefs determine goal level, commitment, and effort mobilization. Self-set goals and learning goals (vs. performance goals) are particularly effective for building efficacy
3. **Affective:** Low academic efficacy generates anxiety that disrupts performance through ruminative self-focus; high efficacy helps students manage academic stress through cognitive coping strategies
4. **Selection:** Academic efficacy influences course selection, field of study choice, career paths, and whether students pursue self-directed learning beyond school settings

Key developmental influences:
- **[[mastery-experience]]**: Self-directed mastery with progressive challenges is the most powerful source
- **Peer modeling**: Coping peer models boost efficacy more than mastery models (Schunk & Hanson, 1985)
- **[[verbal-persuasion]]**: Feedback crediting progress to ability (acquirable skill framing) enhances efficacy more than effort attribution alone
- **Parental and [[teacher-efficacy]]**: Parents' academic aspirations and teachers' instructional efficacy indirectly shape children's academic self-efficacy

## Applications

- **Goal-setting for learning:** Use proximal subgoals linked to distal aspirations; learning goals (strategy mastery) more effective than performance goals for building efficacy and proficiency
- **Self-regulated learning:** Teach students to self-monitor, set goals, structure learning environments, use cognitive strategies, and self-evaluate -- connects to [[deliberate-practice]] and [[chunking]]
- **Anxiety management:** Build cognitive capabilities and self-regulatory skills rather than treating anxiety directly
- **Intrinsic interest cultivation:** Structure activities for progressive challenge at threshold efficacy levels; rewards tied to competence levels enhance rather than undermine interest
- **Writing development:** Path from writing instruction to proficiency runs through self-regulatory efficacy, academic efficacy, self-evaluative standards, and writing goals (Zimmerman & Bandura, 1994, Fig. 6.4)
