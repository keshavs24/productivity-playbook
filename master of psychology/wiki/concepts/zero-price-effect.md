# Zero-Price Effect

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The zero-price effect is the disproportionately strong preference for **free** (zero-cost) items compared to items at any positive price, even when the price difference is trivially small. Zero is not just another price point on the continuum -- it is qualitatively different, triggering emotional reactions that overwhelm rational cost-benefit analysis.

## Key Findings

- **Lindt truffle vs. Hershey's Kiss experiment ([[summary-2-S1-ariely-ch3]]):** At 15c/1c, 73% chose the truffle (rational). At 14c/0c (same 1-cent reduction for both), 69% chose the FREE Kiss. The price gap was unchanged; only the presence of zero changed behavior
- **Amazon France shipping:** Free shipping boosted sales across all countries. France charged 1 franc (~20 cents) instead of free and saw no increase. Switching to genuinely free shipping produced the same sales boost as everywhere else
- **Gift certificate experiment:** People preferred a FREE $10 Amazon certificate over a $20 certificate for $7 (which yields $13 profit) -- forgoing $3 of value because of the FREE label
- **Halloween candy experiment:** ~70% of children took a free small Snickers bar over a rationally superior trade (1 Kiss for a large Snickers). Replicated with MIT students
- **AOL unlimited access:** Switching from pay-per-hour to $19.95/month unlimited caused an overnight increase from 140,000 to 236,000 simultaneous users. The perception of "free" per-hour usage overwhelmed rational capacity planning
- **Price reductions above zero show no special effect:** Reducing Kiss from 2c to 1c, or truffle from 27c to 26c, produced no preference shift. Only the crossing to zero triggers the effect

## Mechanisms

The zero-price effect is driven by **[[loss-aversion]]**:

1. Most transactions involve an upside (getting the item) and a downside (paying the price). The downside creates anxiety about making a bad choice
2. When an item is FREE, the downside **disappears entirely**. There is no visible possibility of loss
3. This emotional relief makes free items feel immensely more valuable than their objective worth
4. At any positive price (even 1 cent), there remains a possibility of loss, maintaining rational evaluation. Zero eliminates this and shifts evaluation to a purely emotional register

Additionally, free pricing activates **[[social-norms-vs-market-norms]]**: when items are free, people shift from selfish market-norm behavior to considerate social-norm behavior, often taking less than they would if the item cost even a penny.

## Applications

- **Business:** Free shipping, free trials, buy-one-get-one-free all exploit the zero-price effect. A 20-cent shipping charge and free shipping are worlds apart in customer response
- **Public policy:** To increase uptake of preventive healthcare (colonoscopies, mammograms), don't lower the co-pay -- **eliminate it entirely**. The difference between $1 and $0 is more powerful than the difference between $50 and $1
- **Product design:** "Zero calories," "zero trans fat" may trigger the same emotional response as zero cost
- **Choice architecture:** Including a free option in a choice set will disproportionately attract attention, even when the paid option is objectively better value
- **Trap awareness:** Free credit cards (no annual fee but higher APR), free gifts with purchase, and free trials are all designed to exploit the zero-price effect

## Open Questions
- Does the zero-price effect vary across cultures? (The Amazon France example suggests cultural factors may modulate it)
- How does the zero-price effect interact with perceived quality? (Are free items also perceived as lower quality?)
- Can the zero-price effect be weakened by making the hidden costs of "free" more salient?
