# Psychological Reactance

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Psychological reactance is the motivational state aroused whenever free choice is limited or threatened, driving individuals to reassert the threatened freedoms. Developed by psychologist **Jack W. Brehm** (1966; Burgoon et al., 2002), the theory posits that when a freedom we believe we possess is restricted, we experience reactance — a motivational pressure to want and pursue that freedom (and the goods and services associated with it) more than before the restriction.

## Key Findings

- **Terrible twos — developmental emergence**: Reactance first appears around age 2, when children develop a concept of themselves as autonomous individuals (Howe, 2003). Two-year-old boys in a Virginia study (S. S. Brehm & Weintraub, 1977) went directly to a toy blocked by a 2-foot Plexiglas barrier, contacting it **3x faster** than the unobstructed toy. When the barrier was only 1 foot (no real obstacle), no preference was shown. Two-year-old girls showed reactance primarily against restrictions from *people* rather than physical barriers (S. S. Brehm, 1981).
- **Teenage resurgence**: Adolescents experience a second peak of reactance as they transition from child to adult. Traditional parental authority imposed during this period is often counterproductive — teenagers sneak, scheme, and fight.
- **[[romeo-and-juliet-effect]]**: Parental interference in teenage romance intensifies rather than reduces romantic feelings (Driscoll, Davis, & Lipetz, 1972). 140 Colorado teenage couples showed greater love and desire for marriage as parental interference increased; when interference weakened, feelings cooled.
- **Kennesaw gun law**: A law requiring gun ownership produced massive noncompliance among residents — but outsiders, whose freedom was unrestricted, flocked to buy guns (Ruback & Juieng, 1997).
- **Dade County phosphate ban**: Miami residents rated banned phosphate detergents as gentler, more effective, better whitening, and even easier to pour compared to Tampa residents unaffected by the ban (Mazis, 1975; Mazis, Settle, & Leslie, 1973).
- **Censorship effects**: Banned information is desired more and believed more, even when never received. UNC students became more sympathetic to a banned speech they never heard (Worchel, Arnold, & Baker, 1975). Purdue students wanted a restricted book more and expected to like it more (Zellinger, Fromkin, Speller, & Kohn, 1974).
- **Courtroom inadmissibility**: Jurors may weight inadmissible evidence *more* when they feel it's their right to consider all evidence (Lieberman & Arndt, 2000).
- **P&G coupon revolt**: Eliminating coupons (even while lowering prices equivalently) produced boycotts and protests because coupons had become an established right.

## Mechanisms

Reactance operates through a desire-merit confusion:
1. A freedom is restricted or threatened
2. We experience heightened desire for the restricted item/freedom
3. We rarely recognize reactance as the cause of our desire
4. Instead, we attribute positive qualities to the restricted item to justify our wanting — assuming desire reflects genuine merit
5. The item appears better, more desirable, more valuable *because* it is restricted

**Critical moderator — newness of restriction**: Reactance is most intense when freedoms that were *previously enjoyed* are revoked, rather than freedoms that never existed. This explains why:
- The 10-to-2 cookie reduction was more powerful than always having 2
- Inconsistent parenting (sometimes allowing, sometimes prohibiting) produces more rebellion than consistent strictness (Lytton, 1979; O'Leary, 1995)
- Revolutions follow periods of progress-then-reversal, not chronic deprivation (Davies, 1962, 1969)
- "It is more dangerous to have given for a while than never to have given at all"

**Evolutionary basis**: Reactance may be adaptive. If established freedoms represent resources critical to survival, fighting to preserve them is functionally important.

## Applications

- **Parenting**: Consistent rule enforcement avoids creating-then-revoking freedoms. Heavy-handed restrictions on teen romance backfire via the Romeo and Juliet effect; preference and persuasion work better than prohibition.
- **Policy design**: Legislators should anticipate reactance when restricting established freedoms. The Dade County phosphate ban and Kennesaw gun law both produced results opposite to their intentions among the restricted population.
- **Persuasion and censorship**: Banning information or speech risks making it more attractive and credible. The First Amendment may serve a psychological function beyond civil liberty — preventing reactance-driven support for fringe ideas.
- **Sales defense**: Recognize the internal arousal triggered by scarcity as a reactance signal. Pause and ask whether you want the item for its function or merely for the possession of something restricted.

## Open Questions

- What is the relationship between psychological reactance and self-determination theory's concept of [[autonomy]]? Both concern the human need for freedom and control, but they emerge from different research traditions.
- Are there individual differences in reactance susceptibility, and do they correlate with personality traits like need for control or trait rebelliousness?
- How does reactance interact with authority and conformity pressures? When does obedience to authority override reactance, and vice versa?
