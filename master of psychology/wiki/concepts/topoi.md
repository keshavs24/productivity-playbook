# Topoi (Common Topics)

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Topoi (singular: *topos*; literally "places") are common lines or strategies of argument that can be used to construct [[enthymeme]]s on any subject. [[Aristotle]] distinguishes them in [[on-rhetoric]] from *idia* (specific topics belonging to particular disciplines like politics or ethics) — topoi are formal argument patterns applicable across all three [[three-genres-of-rhetoric|genres of rhetoric]] (1.2.21-22).

The word *topos* originally referred to a "place" in a handbook where a speaker could look for arguments. Aristotle gives it a distinctive meaning: a strategy of argument, a logical pattern into which different subject matter can be inserted. They are the **formal structure** of enthymemes, while the **material content** comes from the specific topics of politics, ethics, and other disciplines.

## Key Findings

### The Twenty-Eight Common Topoi (2.23)

Aristotle lists twenty-eight topoi for constructing demonstrative and refutative enthymemes:

**Logical Relationships:**
1. **From opposites** — if the opposite predicate is true/false of the opposite subject, the argument follows
2. **From grammatical inflection** — if "justly" is true, then "just" is true
3. **From correlatives** — if one correlate holds, the other holds ("if it is honorable to experience, it is honorable to do")
4. **From the more and the less** (*a fortiori*) — if the less likely is true, the more likely must be; if the more likely is not true, the less likely is not

**Temporal and Causal:**
5. **From looking at the time** — contrasting past expectations with present outcomes
6. **From turning the argument on the opponent** — using the opponent's own words/actions against them
17. **From results to causes** — same result implies same cause
18. **From contrasted choices** — different choices before and after an event reveal true preferences
19. **From identifying purpose with cause** — the purpose for which something might exist is treated as its cause
20. **From reasons for and against** — factors that impel and deter action

**Definitional and Analytical:**
7. **From definition** — unpacking what something essentially is to draw conclusions
8. **From varied meanings** — exploiting ambiguity in a word's meaning
9. **From division** — eliminating possibilities to prove the remainder
12. **From subordinate parts** — arguing from species to genus
28. **From the meaning of a name** — drawing conclusions from etymology or figurative meaning

**Evidential:**
10. **From induction** — building a general rule from specific cases
11. **From authority** — citing judgments of the wise, famous, or those with legal authority
21. **From the implausible** — things that seem implausible are more credible precisely because no one would fabricate them
22. **From contradictions** — showing the opponent's statements are internally inconsistent

**Strategic:**
13. **From the consequence** — good and bad follow from the same cause, so argue either direction
14. **From contrasting opposites** — a dilemma where both alternatives lead to the same conclusion
15. **From hypocritical deception** — showing the opponent's private actions contradict their public position
16. **From consequences by analogy** — if a similar case has a certain consequence, so does this one
23. **From the cause of a false impression** — explaining why something appeared true but was not
24. **From cause and effect** — if the cause exists, the effect follows; no cause, no effect
25. **From a better plan** — a better alternative existed but was not taken
26. **From comparison of contraries** — examining what is contrary to what was done
27. **From what would have been a mistake** — the alternative outcome would have been worse

### Four Common Propositions (*Koina*) (1.3.7-9; 2.19)
In addition to the topoi, Aristotle identifies four subjects common to all speeches:
1. **Possible and impossible** — can this happen or not?
2. **Past fact** — did this happen or not?
3. **Future fact** — will this happen or not?
4. **Degree of magnitude** — is this greater or lesser? (amplification/diminution)

### Nine Fallacious Topoi (2.24)
Apparent enthymemes that lack logical validity:
1. From verbal style (mimicking logical form without substance)
2. From combination/division and omission
3. From exaggeration (amplifying without proving)
4. From a non-necessary sign (treating a fallible sign as conclusive)
5. From accidental result (confusing coincidence with causation)
6. From affirming the consequent (invalid logical inference)
7. From *post hoc, ergo propter hoc* (temporal sequence ≠ causation)
8. From omission of when and how (ignoring qualifying circumstances)
9. From confusing the particular with the general

## Mechanisms

Topoi work as **argument templates** — reusable logical patterns that a speaker fills with context-specific content. They function like heuristics for argument construction: when facing a new subject, the speaker mentally cycles through available topoi to find which ones apply.

The power of topoi comes from their universality: an argument from opposites works whether the subject is justice, health, war, or personal relationships. This is why rhetoric is not a specialized discipline — its formal structures apply to any content domain.

## Applications

- **Argument invention:** When preparing a speech, systematically review the 28 topoi to identify which apply to your subject — this is a generative checklist for building arguments
- **Argument analysis:** When evaluating an opponent's argument, identify which topos is being used — this reveals the logical structure and potential weaknesses
- **Fallacy detection:** Knowledge of the nine fallacious topoi enables recognition of specious arguments — *post hoc* reasoning, false signs, verbal trickery
- **Cross-disciplinary:** The topoi apply to writing, negotiation, debate, legal argumentation, policy analysis, and any context requiring persuasive reasoning

## Open Questions

- How do Aristotle's topoi relate to modern argumentation schemes (Walton, Reed, and Macagno's taxonomy)? The modern lists are more detailed but follow a similar logic of reusable argument patterns.
- Aristotle notes that the more specifically one draws premises from a particular subject, the more one departs from rhetoric into that discipline (1.2.21). Where is the boundary between rhetorical argument and domain expertise?
