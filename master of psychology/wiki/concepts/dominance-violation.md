# Dominance Violation

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

A dominance violation occurs when a decision-maker **chooses an option that is objectively inferior** to an available alternative -- one that is at least as good in every state and strictly better in at least one state. Dominance (including stochastic dominance) is considered "the cornerstone of the normative theory of choice" (Tversky & Kahneman, 1986) and is perhaps the most intuitively compelling axiom of rational choice.

Dominance violations occur not because people deliberately choose inferior options, but because **problem framing obscures the dominance relation**. When dominance is transparent, virtually everyone obeys it. When it is hidden behind nontransparent framing, most people violate it.

## Key Findings

- **Problem 1 vs. Problem 2** (Tversky & Kahneman 1986): A two-stage gamble reformulated as a single-stage gamble produced opposite preferences despite identical probability structures. 74% chose the safe option in sequential framing; 58% chose the risky option in the equivalent single-stage framing. ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch12-rational-choice-framing]])
- **Concurrent decisions:** 73% of participants chose a combination that was stochastically dominated by an alternative combination; only 3% chose the dominant portfolio
- **Medical decisions:** Physicians violated dominance through pseudocertainty framing in tumor treatment scenarios
- [[prospect-theory]] is the **only** alternative decision model that can accommodate observed dominance violations, because it is purely descriptive and does not impose dominance as a constraint

## Mechanisms

Dominance violations arise through two interacting mechanisms:
1. **[[pseudocertainty]]** and the **[[certainty-effect]]**: Sequential or conditional framing creates the illusion that an outcome is certain within a sub-problem, even though the overall probability is not certain. People overweight this illusory certainty.
2. **Isolated evaluation**: People evaluate each decision problem or stage separately rather than computing the overall probability distribution. This is a manifestation of [[mental-accounting]] and narrow bracketing.

The key insight is that dominance is a property of the overall probability distributions, but people evaluate individual frames/stages.

## Applications

- **Portfolio construction:** Investors who evaluate individual positions separately (narrow bracketing) may construct portfolios that are stochastically dominated by simpler alternatives
- **Organizational decision-making:** Sequential departmental decisions (marketing evaluates gains, finance evaluates losses) can produce dominated aggregate outcomes
- **Product design:** Presenting options in sequential "feature bundles" can lead consumers to choose dominated packages
- **Insurance design:** Bundling and unbundling risks can obscure or reveal dominance relations
