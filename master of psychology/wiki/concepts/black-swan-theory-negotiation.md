# Black Swan Theory (Negotiation)

**Phase(s):** 5 — Negotiation
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

In negotiation, Black Swans are **unknown unknowns** — hidden and unexpected pieces of information whose discovery has game-changing effects on the negotiation dynamic. The term was adapted from Nassim Nicholas Taleb's broader concept (from *Fooled by Randomness*, 2001, and *The Black Swan*, 2007) by [[chris-voss]], who hypothesizes that in every negotiation, each side possesses at least three Black Swans. Voss considers this concept so central to negotiation that he named his company "The Black Swan Group."

## Key Findings

- **Three categories of information** in any negotiation: (1) known knowns — the counterpart's name, their offer, past experience; (2) known unknowns — things you're certain exist but don't know who has them (poker wild cards); (3) unknown unknowns — things you don't even know you don't know, which would be game-changing if uncovered ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
- **At least three Black Swans per side** — Voss's working hypothesis, consistently validated by experience ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
- **Black Swans are leverage multipliers** — they give you the upper hand by revealing the three types of leverage: positive, negative, and normative ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
- **Conventional research methods fail** — standard questioning and research techniques are designed to confirm known knowns and reduce uncertainty, not to dig into the unknown ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
- **"They're crazy!" is usually wrong** — when a counterpart seems irrational, they are most likely ill-informed, constrained, or pursuing hidden interests (Malhotra & Bazerman, *Negotiation Genius*) ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])

## Mechanisms

Black Swans transform negotiations through three leverage types:

1. **Positive leverage**: Discovering what the counterpart truly wants — their "I want..." reveals. You can provide it, withhold it, or use their desire to create competition.
2. **Negative leverage**: Discovering what the counterpart fears losing — rooted in [[loss-aversion]]. Label your negative leverage rather than wielding it directly: "It seems like you strongly value the fact that you've always paid on time."
3. **Normative leverage**: Discovering the counterpart's own norms and standards, then showing inconsistencies between their beliefs and actions. No one likes to look like a hypocrite.

### Techniques for Finding Black Swans

- **Know their "religion"** — understand the counterpart's worldview, their reason for being. Position your demands within their worldview to show respect and gain influence. (Watson standoff: the "Dawn of the Third Day" reference to his Christian beliefs)
- **The Similarity Principle** — people trust in-group members. Trigger shared identity to gain influence. (Ohio CEO: "stewardship" as a Christian concept)
- **Get face time** — Black Swans are incredibly hard to uncover remotely. Email gives too much time to compose and hide. The [[seven-thirty-eight-fifty-five-rule]] means you miss 93% of the message without face-to-face interaction.
- **Observe unguarded moments** — the first and last minutes of meetings, interruptions, and breaks reveal more than formal sessions
- **Use labels to trigger correction** — deliberately mislabel the situation to get the counterpart to correct you and reveal hidden information (Charleston real estate case: "It seems like the seller must have doubts about future market fundamentals")
- **Listen for what doesn't make sense** — the moment something seems crazy, push forward. That's where the Black Swan lives.

## Applications

- **William Griffin bank siege (1981)**: Three missed Black Swans — the double homicide connection, the absence of monetary demands, and the suicide-by-cop intention hidden in a rambling note — led to a hostage's death ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
- **Dwight Watson tractor standoff (2003)**: The Black Swan was Watson's devout Christianity, uncovered through deep listening by FBI agent Winnie Miller. "Dawn of the Third Day" resolved a 48-hour standoff ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
- **Charleston real estate deal**: A mislabel about "seller doubts" triggered the broker to reveal the seller's constraint (underwater properties in Atlanta and Savannah), enabling a $700,000 discount ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
- **Coca-Cola client pursuit**: A nonresponding contact wasn't being difficult — his division was in chaos and he had lost all influence. The constraint was the Black Swan ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])
