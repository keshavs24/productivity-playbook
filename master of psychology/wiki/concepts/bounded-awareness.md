# Bounded Awareness

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Bounded awareness (Bazerman & Chugh, 2005) is the systematic failure to **notice or focus on obvious, observable, and relevant data** during decision making. Our minds constantly filter information -- unconsciously, automatically, and inefficiently -- causing us to ignore useful information while paying attention to irrelevant information. Unlike the [[availability]] heuristic (a general cognitive tendency), bounded awareness identifies **specific groups of variables that are predictably out of focus** in particular domains.

## Key Findings

- People have bounded awareness regarding their own cognition ([[inattentional-blindness]], [[change-blindness]]), their environments ([[focalism]]), group information sharing, and strategic settings ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch4]])
- The Madoff Ponzi scheme ($64.8 billion) illustrates bounded awareness at scale: feeder fund managers, professional investors, regulators, and investment bankers failed to notice readily available evidence that returns were impossible
- The nine-dot problem demonstrates how self-imposed psychological boundaries prevent creative solutions -- people assume they must stay within the dots even though no such constraint exists
- Groups are bounded in their awareness of unshared information: Stasser and Titus (1985) showed groups spend disproportionate time discussing shared information rather than unique information held by individual members -- only 18% of groups with distributed information chose the correct candidate vs. 83% of fully informed groups
- In strategic settings, people systematically exclude information about opposing parties' decisions and the rules of the game: the "Acquiring a Company" problem shows only 9 of 123 MBA students made the rational offer ($0), and replications with CEOs and investment bankers produced similar results
- The winner's curse in auctions: winning bidders overlook the implication that their bid was the highest -- suggesting they overestimated value (Bazerman & Samuelson, 1983)
- Reference group neglect: entrepreneurs focus on their own strengths while ignoring the competition (Camerer & Lovallo, 1999; Moore, Oesch, & Zietsma, 2007)
- Choice overload: too many options can paralyze decision making (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Schwartz, 2005; Vohs et al., 2008)

## Mechanisms

Bounded awareness arises from the fundamental human need to simplify the information environment. William James (1890) described the world of a newborn as "one great buzzing, blooming confusion." Learning to navigate requires figuring out what to attend to and what to ignore. These attention filters are essential but imperfect -- they create systematic blind spots. The misalignment between the information needed for good decisions and the information included in awareness produces "focusing failures."

Unlike availability (which explains cross-context tendencies to overweight vivid/recent information), bounded awareness is domain-specific: in negotiations, people predictably miss the rules of the game and the cognitions of the opposing party; in groups, people predictably miss unshared information; in ethical contexts, gradual degradation escapes notice.

## Applications

- **Negotiation:** Attend to the rules of the game, the decisions of the other party, and how their acceptance/rejection changes your expected value
- **Group decision making:** Structure discussions to surface unique/unshared information; forewarn members about unique knowledge; identify expertise before discussion begins (Stasser, Vaughn, & Stewart, 2000)
- **Ethics:** Watch for the "slippery slope" -- small ethical lapses are harder to notice than large ones (Gino & Bazerman, 2009)
- **Competition:** Systematically analyze your reference group and competitors, not just your own strengths
- **Innovation:** Ask "why not?" to break psychological bounds on the problem space (Nalebuff & Ayres, 2003)
