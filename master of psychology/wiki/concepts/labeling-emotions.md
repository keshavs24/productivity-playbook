# Labeling Emotions

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Labeling is a [[tactical-empathy]] technique in which the negotiator identifies and verbalizes the other person's emotional state, using neutral, third-person phrasing. It is a way of validating someone's emotion by acknowledging it -- giving an emotion a name shows you identify with how that person feels, creating a shortcut to intimacy without asking about external factors.

Labels are phrased as statements or questions beginning with:
- "It seems like..."
- "It sounds like..."
- "It looks like..."

**Critical rule:** Never use "I" statements ("I'm hearing that..."). The word "I" centers you instead of them, raises their guard, and takes personal responsibility for the words that follow.

**After delivering a label:** Go silent. Let the label do its work. Resist the urge to elaborate or follow up with a specific question. A label's power is that it invites the other person to reveal themselves.

## Key Findings

- **Lieberman fMRI research (UCLA):** When people are shown photos of faces expressing strong emotion, the brain shows greater activity in the amygdala (fear/threat center). When they are asked to **label the emotion** (apply rational words to it), activity moves to areas governing rational thinking. Labeling an emotion disrupts its raw intensity -- it is a neurological interrupt of the fear response.
- **Harlem high-rise (1998):** Voss labeled through an apartment door for six hours: "It looks like you don't want to come out... It seems like you worry that if you open the door, we'll come in with guns blazing... It looks like you don't want to go back to jail." Three armed fugitives surrendered peacefully.
- **Washington Redskins ticket collection:** TJ rewrote an aggressive collection script using labels ("In these difficult times, we understand our fans have been hit hard"). Every delinquent ticket holder called back and set up payment plans.
- **Girl Scout fundraising:** A fundraiser's label -- "It seems that you are really passionate about this gift and want to find the right project reflecting the opportunities and life-changing experiences the Girl Scouts gave you" -- caused a "difficult" donor to sign a blank check: "You understand me."
- **Anna's $1 million negotiation:** An [[accusation-audit]] combined with ongoing labeling turned a hostile subcontractor meeting into a $1 million win for the prime contractor.
- **Ryan's airport upgrade:** A sequence of labels and mirrors -- acknowledging gate agent Wendy's difficult day -- produced a boarding pass and Economy Plus upgrade in under two minutes on a sold-out flight.

## Mechanisms

1. **Amygdala disruption:** Labeling moves brain processing from the emotional amygdala to rational thinking areas, reducing the raw intensity of fear and anger. "Bathe the fears in sunlight, bleaching them of their power."
2. **Presenting vs. underlying emotion:** Emotions have two levels. The "presenting" behavior is the visible surface (e.g., a grumpy grandfather being cranky). The "underlying" feeling is the driver (e.g., loneliness from feeling left out). Good negotiators label the underlying emotion.
3. **Negative diffusion / positive reinforcement:** Labeling negatives diffuses (or defuses, in extreme cases) their power. Labeling positives reinforces them. The fastest way to establish a working relationship is to acknowledge the negative and diffuse it.
4. **Invitation to elaborate:** When phrased as a neutral statement of understanding, a label encourages longer, more substantive responses than "yes" or "no." If the counterpart disagrees, you can always retreat: "I didn't say that was what it was. I just said it seems like that."

## Applications

**How to label:**
1. Detect the other person's emotional state -- through words, tone, and body language (the trinity of "words, music, and dance")
2. Label it aloud using "It seems like..." / "It sounds like..." / "It looks like..."
3. Go silent -- let the label work
4. If they elaborate, listen and label again if needed
5. Layer labels: the first label uncovers the presenting dynamic, the second uncovers the underlying dynamic

**Strategic applications:**
- **Defusing anger:** Label the anger rather than responding to it -- it forces acknowledgment of feelings rather than continued acting out
- **Clearing obstacles before presenting solutions:** "Clear the road before advertising the destination" -- label fears first, then present proposals
- **Following hostile interactions:** After an angry customer leaves, label the service agent's experience to build instant rapport ("It seems like they were pretty upset")
- **Correcting mistakes:** Open with "Bless me, Father, for I have sinned" energy -- acknowledge the negative dynamic immediately rather than denying it
