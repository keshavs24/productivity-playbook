# Creeping Determinism

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Creeping determinism is Fischhoff's term for the unconscious tendency to view reported outcomes as having been relatively inevitable -- as opposed to **philosophical determinism**, which is the conscious belief that whatever happens must happen. The "creeping" qualifier captures the insidious, below-awareness nature of the process: outcome knowledge seeps into our interpretation of the past without our noticing, making events seem foreordained even when we do not hold a deterministic worldview.

## Key Findings

- The term was introduced by Fischhoff (1975, 1982) as the mechanism underlying [[hindsight-bias]] ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch23-hindsight-bias]])
- Creeping determinism is distinct from deliberate belief in inevitability; it is an automatic byproduct of integrating outcome knowledge into existing understanding
- It produces several corollary effects: telescoping the rate of historical change, remembering people as more like their current selves, and believing that critical experiments were recognized as critical at the time
- Historian Georges Florovsky (1969): "The tendency toward determinism is somehow implied in the method of retrospection itself"
- Even deliberate attempts to resist creeping determinism (by being warned about it) are largely ineffective; active counter-arguing is required

## Mechanisms

When we learn an outcome, we immediately assimilate it into our mental model by reinterpreting the pre-outcome situation. This reinterpretation makes the outcome seem like a natural, almost inevitable consequence. The reinterpretation is automatic -- we are typically unaware that our understanding has shifted. The result is that the path from initial conditions to the outcome now appears deterministic, even though it was genuinely uncertain at the time.

The process is closely related to [[anchoring-and-adjustment]]: the known outcome serves as an anchor from which we insufficiently adjust when trying to reconstruct our foresightful state of mind.

## Applications

- **Historical analysis:** Creeping determinism explains why historians "drag into prominence the forces which have triumphed and thrust into the background those which they have swallowed up" (Tawney, 1961)
- **Performance evaluation:** Evaluators judge decisions by their outcomes rather than by the quality of the decision process, because creeping determinism makes bad outcomes look foreseeable
- **Risk management:** If past failures seem inevitable in retrospect, organizations conclude the decision makers were foolish rather than that the system is inherently uncertain -- missing the lesson that "we must accept the fact of uncertainty and learn to live with it" (Wohlstetter, 1962)
