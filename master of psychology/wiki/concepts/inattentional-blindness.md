# Inattentional Blindness

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Inattentional blindness is the failure to **notice clearly visible but unexpected objects or events when attention is focused on another task**. People have a strong tendency not to see what they are not looking for, even when they are looking directly at it (Mack & Rock, 1998; Simons & Levin, 2003).

## Key Findings

- Neisser (1979): participants watching two superimposed groups passing basketballs and counting passes for one group failed to notice a woman with an umbrella who clearly walked through the scene -- only 21% noticed ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch4]])
- Simons and Chabris (1999, 2010) replicated with a person in a gorilla costume who walked through the basketball game, thumped its chest, and was visible for over 5 seconds -- about half of participants missed it entirely
- Mack (2003): inattentional blindness could cause airplane pilots to overlook another aircraft on their runway
- Cell phone use while driving creates inattentional blindness -- drivers focusing on conversations miss hazards they are looking directly at (Levy, Pashler, & Boer, 2006)
- Neural correlates: C.M. Moore and Egeth (1997) connected inattentional blindness to specific brain regions
- The phenomenon extends beyond vision to any information domain: spouses report telling us things we have absolutely no recollection of

## Mechanisms

Attention is a limited resource. When focused on a demanding primary task (counting passes), there is insufficient attentional capacity remaining to process unexpected stimuli, even when those stimuli are conspicuous. The key independent variables: task difficulty, stimulus expectedness, and perceptual load (Mack, 2003). Inattentional blindness violates our common assumption of complete visual awareness -- we believe we see everything in our visual field, but we do not.

## Applications

- **Managerial decision making:** Analogous to the visual phenomenon -- when focused on one aspect of a decision, managers can miss critical information that is readily available
- **Driving safety:** Strong evidence against cell phone use while driving
- **Aviation safety:** Pilots must be trained to scan for unexpected hazards
- **Organizational attention:** Teams focused on specific metrics may miss emergent threats or opportunities
- **Ethics:** We may fail to notice ethical violations occurring in plain sight when our attention is focused elsewhere
