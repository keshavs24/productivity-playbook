# Fundamental Attribution Error

**Phase(s):** 1, 4, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

The fundamental attribution error (FAE), also called correspondence bias, is the tendency to **overestimate the extent to which other people's behavior is due to internal, dispositional factors** (personality, character, attitudes) **and to underestimate the role of situational factors** (context, circumstances, social pressure). Named by Lee Ross (1977).

## Key Findings

- **Jones & Harris (1967):** Participants who knew an essay writer was *assigned* a pro-Castro position still believed the essay reflected the writer's true attitudes. Situational knowledge didn't eliminate the dispositional inference. ([[summary-1-03-social-psychology-ch4]])
- **[[perceptual-salience]]:** People are visually prominent; situations are invisible. Taylor & Fiske (1975) showed that the actor observers could see better was judged as more causally influential — same conversation, different attributions based on viewing angle.
- **Two-step process (Gilbert, 1989):** Step 1 (automatic): make internal attribution. Step 2 (effortful): adjust for situation. Step 2 is often skipped when distracted or cognitively loaded. This parallels [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — the dispositional inference is the anchor, situational adjustment is insufficient.
- **Bierbrauer (1973):** Participants witnessed a verbatim reenactment of one subject's "obedience" in the Milgram (1963) situation. Regardless of whether they played the role or merely observed, they consistently underestimated the degree to which subjects in general would yield to situational forces, attributing the behavior to personal dispositions. ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch9-attribution-shortcomings]])
- **Ross, Amabile, & Steinmetz (1977) quiz game study:** Randomly assigned "questioners" composed questions from their own esoteric knowledge. Both contestants and uninvolved observers rated questioners as far superior in general knowledge despite the blatant arbitrariness of role assignment. Questioners themselves were not misled. This demonstrates how social roles create biased "data samples" that drive dispositional inferences. ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch9-attribution-shortcomings]])
- **Camera angle affects confessions:** Lassiter et al. (2007) showed judges and police rated confessions as more voluntary when the camera focused on the suspect alone. New Zealand changed its interrogation recording rules in response.
- **Culturally moderated:** The FAE is stronger in Western individualistic cultures (analytic thinking) and weaker in East Asian collectivistic cultures (holistic thinking). Miller (1984) showed this is learned, not inborn — children from US and India were indistinguishable.
- **Salience/availability mechanism:** The FAE may fundamentally reflect the fact that actors are more perceptually salient than situational features. Taylor et al. (1976) showed that actors made visually salient (by unique race, sex, or appearance) are assigned disproportionate responsibility. Manipulating observers' visual perspective (Storms, 1973; Taylor & Fiske, 1975) correspondingly shifts attributions between situational and dispositional. ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch9-attribution-shortcomings]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Perceptual salience:** We focus on people because they are visually prominent; the situation is background and largely invisible
2. **Anchoring:** The dispositional inference comes first (automatic); situational adjustment comes second (effortful) and is typically insufficient
3. **Cultural schemas:** Western cultures provide a "personality psychologist" schema that encourages seeing behavior as reflecting stable traits

## Applications

- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** When a counterpart makes a tough demand, the natural tendency is to attribute it to their personality ("they're aggressive") rather than their situation ("their boss is pressuring them"). Recognizing this error opens empathy and better deal-making.
- **Communication (Phase 4):** Receivers of messages attribute the message to the speaker's character, not their constraints. A salesperson who follows a script is judged by the script's content as if it reflects their beliefs.
- **Leadership:** Leaders who attribute subordinate failures to character rather than circumstance make worse decisions, create less psychological safety, and miss systemic problems.
- **Sales/persuasion (Phase 6):** Understanding that people will attribute your behavior to your character (not your sales strategy) means you can leverage genuine warmth and competence cues.
- **Relationships:** Happy couples make external attributions for partner's negative behavior; unhappy couples make internal attributions. The FAE predicts relationship deterioration.
