# Placebo Effect

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The placebo effect is the phenomenon whereby an inert treatment (a sugar pill, sham surgery, or other intervention with no active therapeutic ingredient) produces genuine therapeutic effects — both subjective (feeling better) and objective (measurable physiological changes). The effect is driven by the patient's expectations and beliefs about the treatment, and it extends far beyond medicine into consumer products, performance enhancement, and everyday experience. The term derives from the Latin "I shall please" and was used in the 14th century for sham mourners hired to sob at funerals.

## Key Findings

### Medical Placebos
- **Internal mammary artery ligation (Cobb, 1955):** Sham surgery (incisions only) produced identical angina relief to the real procedure. Both lasted about 3 months; electrocardiograms showed no difference between groups. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])
- **Arthroscopic knee surgery (Moseley, 2002):** 180 patients in 3 groups (full surgery, partial surgery, simulated surgery with only incisions). All 3 groups showed **identical** improvements in pain and walking for 2 years post-surgery. Published as lead article in *NEJM*. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])
- **Antidepressants:** 75% of the effect of six leading antidepressants was duplicated in placebo controls ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])
- **Parkinson's brain surgery:** Sham surgery (drilling holes in skulls without completing the procedure) produced the same outcomes as real surgery ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])
- **Scar tissue surgery:** For years surgeons cut abdominal scar tissue believing it caused chronic pain — faked procedures produced equal relief ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])
- **Antibiotics for viral colds:** Study found 1/3 of patients receiving antibiotics for sore throats actually had viral infections — doctors prescribe placebos routinely because patients expect prescriptions ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])
- **Gerbi (1794):** Italian physician treated toothache with worm secretions — 68% of patients reported year-long pain relief ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])

### Price-Modulated Placebos (see [[placebo-pricing]])
- $2.50 placebo painkiller relieved nearly all participants; $0.10 version relieved only half ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])
- Full-price SoBe improved anagram performance 28% over discounted SoBe ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])

### Historical Placebos
- Egyptian mummy powder, wing of bat, mercury, mineral water, cocaine — all used as medical treatments
- The "royal touch" — from AD 800, European monarchs healed by laying hands on the sick; Charles II (1660-1685) touched ~100,000 people ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch11-power-of-price]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Belief and confidence:** Faith in the treatment, practitioner, or procedure activates internal healing processes. A doctor's enthusiasm directly influences outcomes.
2. **Conditioning:** Repeated experiences (taking pills that work, seeing doctors who help) build conditioned associations. The body prepares for relief before the treatment takes effect — Ariely's personal example of endorphin release at the sight of a nurse approaching with painkiller in the burn ward.
3. **Neurochemical release:** Expectation of pain relief triggers endorphins and opiates. Expectation of improvement triggers dopamine and other reward neurotransmitters.
4. **Price and branding as placebo cues:** Price, packaging, and brand signals set expectations that amplify or dampen the placebo response (see [[placebo-pricing]])

## Applications

- **Healthcare ethics:** Should doctors prescribe placebos? They already do (antibiotics for viral infections). Ariely raises but does not resolve the ethical tension between honesty and therapeutic benefit.
- **Healthcare economics:** If expensive drugs are genuinely more effective due to placebo pricing, this has major implications for cost-containment policies and generic medication adoption.
- **Clinical trial design:** The power of placebo makes rigorous controlled testing essential — many established medical procedures (arthroscopic surgery, scar tissue removal) have never been tested against placebos.
- **Consumer products:** Branding, packaging, and pricing create real subjective value through placebo mechanisms, blurring the line between marketing deception and genuine value creation.

## Open Questions
- Are there individual differences in placebo susceptibility? (Ariely notes the effect was stronger for people with more pain medication experience)
- What is the ethical framework for deliberately deploying placebos? Is there a difference between a doctor prescribing one and a marketer selling one?
- Few surgical procedures have been tested against placebo controls. How many currently common procedures would survive such testing?
- Can placebos be made to work even when people know they are placebos? (Some research suggests "open-label placebos" can still be effective)
