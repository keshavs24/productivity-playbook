# Separate the People from the Problem

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

"Separate the people from the problem" is the first principle of [[principled-negotiation]]. It means disentangling relationship issues (perception, emotion, communication) from substantive issues (terms, conditions, numbers) and dealing with each on its own merits. Rather than making substantive concessions to preserve the relationship, or damaging the relationship to win on substance, negotiators should handle each dimension directly and independently.

The core prescription: **Be soft on the people, hard on the problem.**

## Key Findings

- **Every negotiator has two kinds of interests: substance and relationship.** Usually, the ongoing relationship is more important than any single transaction. Positional bargaining forces a trade-off between the two. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch2-people]])
- **People problems fall into three categories** — each requiring distinct techniques ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch2-people]]):
  - **Perception:** Put yourself in their shoes. Don't deduce intentions from fears. Don't blame them. Discuss perceptions openly. Act inconsistently with their expectations (as Sadat did by visiting Jerusalem). Give them a stake through participation. Make proposals consistent with their values (face-saving).
  - **Emotion:** Recognize and acknowledge emotions. Allow letting off steam. Don't react to outbursts. Use symbolic gestures (apologies, small courtesies).
  - **Communication:** Listen actively and acknowledge what is being said. Speak to be understood (not to impress third parties). Speak about yourself, not about them ("I feel" not "You did"). Speak for a purpose.
- **Prevention is more effective than cure.** Build relationships before the negotiation begins. Benjamin Franklin's technique: ask an adversary for a small favor (borrowing a book). ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch2-people]])
- **Sit side by side, facing the problem.** The physical arrangement reinforces the psychological orientation. Shipwrecked sailors in a lifeboat naturally cooperate once they see their problems as shared. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch2-people]])

## Mechanisms

The people-problem separation works by addressing cognitive biases that undermine negotiation:

1. **Countering [[self-serving-bias-in-negotiation]]:** Perspective-taking ("put yourself in their shoes") directly combats the tendency to see only the merits of your own case.
2. **Countering [[reactive-devaluation]]:** When proposals come from a collaborative frame rather than an adversarial one, they are less likely to be automatically devalued.
3. **Countering [[framing-effects]]:** Reframing from "us vs. them" to "us vs. the problem" transforms the negotiation from a competitive to a collaborative frame.
4. **Addressing [[loss-aversion]] through face-saving:** People resist agreements that make them look like they've lost. Making proposals consistent with their values and self-image removes this barrier.
5. **Reducing emotional escalation:** The techniques for managing emotion (acknowledging, allowing steam, not reacting) prevent the amygdala hijack that derails rational negotiation.

## Applications

- **The insurance commissioner case:** The insurance company lawyer attacked the regulation. The commissioner responded defensively: "Are you saying I'm unfair?" Result: breakdown. Better approach: separate the person (the commissioner's competence and integrity) from the problem (the regulation's unintended consequences).
- **The Jones Realty case:** Frank Turnbull consistently praised Mrs. Jones personally ("We appreciate what you've done for us") while challenging the substantive unfairness of the rent. This combination allowed her to agree to a refund without feeling attacked. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]])
- **The steel industry rule:** Only one person can get angry at a time. This structural intervention prevents emotional escalation while legitimizing expression.

## Open Questions
- Is it always possible to separate people from problems? Fisher and Ury acknowledge (in the "Ten Questions" section) that sometimes the people *are* the problem — personality clashes, irrational behavior, or bad faith negotiators may require different strategies.
- How does this principle interact with cultural contexts where personal relationships and business are deeply intertwined (e.g., many East Asian and Middle Eastern cultures)?
