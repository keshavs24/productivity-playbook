# Perceptual Salience

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Perceptual salience is the principle that **whatever we focus our attention on seems more causally important**. In social perception, people are perceptually prominent (we can see them) while situations are often invisible — leading us to overattribute behavior to the person.

## Key Findings

- Taylor & Fiske (1975): Observers who had a better view of one actor in a conversation rated that actor as more influential — same conversation, different causal attributions based purely on viewing angle ([[summary-1-03-social-psychology-ch4]])
- Lassiter et al. (2007): Camera angle on confession videos changed perceived voluntariness — even for judges and police officers
- This is a core mechanism behind the [[fundamental-attribution-error]]

## Applications

- **Communication/Persuasion:** What you draw attention to appears more important and more causal. Controlling salience is controlling attribution — a direct connection to [[cialdini]]'s concept of pre-suasion (Phase 6).
- **Presentations:** The speaker is perceptually salient — audiences will attribute the message's quality to the speaker's character/competence, not to the quality of their preparation or their team's contributions.
