# Sunk-Cost Fallacy

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The sunk-cost fallacy is the tendency to continue investing in a course of action because of previously invested resources (time, money, effort) rather than evaluating only the future costs and benefits. Sunk costs are historical, irrecoverable, and rationally irrelevant to future decisions. Yet people intuitively weight them heavily, feeling that abandoning an investment "wastes" what was already spent.

## Key Findings

- Accountants and economists uniformly prescribe ignoring sunk costs: "the reference point for action should be our current state, and we should consider all alternative courses of action by evaluating only the future costs and benefits" ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch8]])
- Textbook training in sunk costs "seldom translates to wise solutions to real-world problems" because typical education lacks a clear description of WHY we intuitively include sunk costs
- The sunk-cost fallacy underlies [[escalation-of-commitment]]: prior investment creates a reference point from which quitting feels like accepting a loss, triggering risk-seeking behavior via [[loss-aversion]]
- Staw (1976) demonstrated that personal responsibility for the sunk cost magnifies the fallacy through self-justification
- NBA draft position (Staw & Hoang, 1995): Teams gave more playing time to higher draft picks regardless of actual performance -- the sunk cost of draft capital influenced roster decisions

## Mechanisms

The sunk-cost fallacy operates through [[prospect-theory]] and [[loss-aversion]]:
1. Previous investments establish a reference point
2. Quitting means accepting a sure loss (the sunk investment is "wasted")
3. Continuing means a gamble with a chance of recovering the investment
4. Risk seeking in the loss domain biases toward continuation
5. Self-justification (from [[cognitive-dissonance]]) adds further pressure -- quitting implies the original decision was wrong

## Applications

- **Career decisions:** "I've been in this job for 5 years" is irrelevant to whether to stay. Only future prospects matter.
- **Project management:** "We've already spent $2M" is irrelevant. Only the incremental investment vs. expected incremental return matters.
- **Relationships:** "We've been together for years" is a sunk cost; only future happiness should determine whether to continue.
- **Education:** Whether to finish a degree should depend on the value of completion vs. the cost of remaining time, not on time already spent.

## Open Questions
- Does awareness of the sunk-cost fallacy reduce its effect in personal decisions? (Evidence suggests it helps for financial decisions but less so for emotional ones)
- Is there an adaptive value to the sunk-cost fallacy in some contexts? (Some argue it serves as a commitment device that signals resolve to others)
