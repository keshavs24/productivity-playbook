# Concreteness Principle

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The concreteness principle holds that ideas expressed in concrete, sensory language — in terms of specific human actions, physical objects, and tangible images — are more understandable, more memorable, and easier to coordinate around than ideas expressed in abstract language. The [[heath-brothers]] identify concreteness as the third principle of the [[success-framework]] and argue it is the *easiest* of the six principles to apply — the barrier is not difficulty but forgetfulness, because expertise naturally drives people toward abstraction.

Something is "concrete" if you can examine it with your senses. A V8 engine is concrete. "High performance" is abstract. A Nordie ironing a customer's shirt is concrete. "World-class customer service" is abstract. See [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]].

## Key Findings

- **Concreteness serves three distinct functions:**
  1. **Understanding:** Concrete language helps novices grasp new concepts by building on existing schemas. Asian math teachers use tiles, stick figures, and shopping scenarios to teach subtraction — "Conceptual Knowledge" questions appeared in 37% of Japanese math lessons but only 2% of American ones.
  2. **Memory:** The Velcro theory of memory — memory is not a single filing cabinet but a tangle of hooks and loops. Concrete ideas snag many hooks (visual, auditory, emotional, kinesthetic); abstract ideas snag few. Aesop's fables survived 2,500 years through concrete images; the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* lost their abstractions but kept their concrete actions.
  3. **Coordination:** Concrete goals align groups. Boeing's 727 goal ("seat 131 passengers, fly nonstop Miami to NYC, land on Runway 4-22 at LaGuardia") coordinated thousands of experts. "The best passenger plane in the world" would not have.

- **Abstraction is the luxury of the expert.** Novices see concrete details as concrete details. Experts see them as symbols of patterns and principles. Because experts *can* think abstractly, they naturally *want* to — and this is where the [[curse-of-knowledge]] inserts itself. The engineers who responded to manufacturing problems by making their drawings more elaborate were speaking "English more slowly and loudly."

- **Concrete props transform communication.** James Grant's packet of salt and sugar (ORT ingredients), Jerry Kaplan's maroon portfolio (future of computing), Jeff Hawkins's wooden Palm Pilot block — all converted abstract concepts into tangible objects that focused attention and invited collaboration. "You can't see it and *not* start brainstorming."

- **Concrete personas guide decisions.** Saddleback Church's "Saddleback Sam" and General Mills' "Fingertips" program both used concrete representations of target audiences to drive better decisions — Sam's profile immediately killed a telemarketing proposal because "Sam hates telemarketers."

## Mechanisms

- **Dual coding:** Concrete words activate both verbal and imagery systems in memory, creating more retrieval paths than abstract words (Paivio's dual-coding theory). This enriches [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]].
- **Schema activation:** Concrete language activates existing schemas (the grapefruit schema for a pomelo), which provides a rich network of associations and inferences for free.
- **Shared understanding:** Abstract language is ambiguous — two programmers may interpret "the next great search engine" as meaning completeness or speed. Concrete language eliminates ambiguity and creates shared turf for collaboration.
- **Experiential hooks:** Jane Elliott's brown eyes/blue eyes simulation put so many sensory, emotional, and social hooks into the idea of prejudice that students couldn't forget it twenty years later. The more hooks, the more loops snagged.

## Applications

- **Fight abstractspeak:** Before communicating, ask: "Can my audience examine this with their senses?" If not, find a concrete way to express it. The engineers should go to the factory floor; the nonprofit should carry a prop.
- **Use concrete examples to teach abstract concepts:** Asian math classrooms demonstrate that concrete foundations (tiles, yen, playing ball) accelerate abstract understanding. "Trying to teach an abstract principle without concrete foundations is like trying to start a house by building a roof in the air."
- **Create concrete goals:** Boeing's 727 goal, Kennedy's moon mission, Ibuka's pocketable radio — concrete goals coordinate diverse experts better than abstract mission statements.
- **Name your landscapes:** TNC's decision to name "the Mount Hamilton Wilderness" converted an abstract conservation goal into a tangible, evocative entity that other organizations spontaneously adopted.

## Open Questions

- What is the relationship between concreteness and the [[representativeness]] heuristic? Concrete, vivid examples may be more "representative" of a category in people's minds, even if they are statistically atypical. Does concreteness enhance or distort judgment?
- At what point does concreteness become counterproductive? Over-specificity can limit generalization — the Heaths don't discuss this tension directly.
- How does concreteness interact with cultural context? The "supersized grapefruit" schema works only if the audience has a grapefruit schema. What happens when concrete references are culturally unfamiliar?
