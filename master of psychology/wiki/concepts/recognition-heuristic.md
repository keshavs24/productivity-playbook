# Recognition Heuristic

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The recognition heuristic is a decision strategy where, when choosing between two items and one is recognized while the other is not, people infer that the recognized item scores higher on the criterion being judged. Goldstein and Gigerenzer (1999, 2002) showed this heuristic is often **adaptive** rather than biased -- in many domains, recognition correlates strongly with the criterion of interest because both are driven by the same underlying factor (e.g., media coverage correlates with city size).

## Key Findings

- American students judging German city sizes: when they recognized one city but not the other, they almost always picked the recognized city -- and were more accurate than German students who recognized both cities and had to use other (less reliable) cues ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch11]])
- The heuristic works via a two-step ecological correlation: (1) city size correlates with media mention frequency (.72/.70 for American/German cities), and (2) media mention frequency correlates with recognition probability (.86/.79)
- Result: recognition correlates with city size (.66/.60) -- a reliable cue
- People can adaptively override the recognition heuristic when they have contradicting knowledge: Richter and Spath (2006) found people correctly chose mottled umbers (a common moth) over giant pandas for population size, because they knew pandas are endangered despite being more recognizable
- Gigerenzer, Todd, and ABC Research Group (1999) argued in *Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart* that such cases of heuristic failure are the exception, not the rule

## Mechanisms

The recognition heuristic exploits a reliable ecological correlation: important, large, or consequential things tend to be mentioned more frequently in media and conversation, which makes them more likely to be stored in memory. Recognition serves as a proxy for these underlying properties. The heuristic is "fast and frugal" -- it requires only a single binary cue (recognized or not) and no further information processing. People can also integrate recognition with other knowledge (rather than blindly following recognition) when domain-specific knowledge provides contradicting information.

## Applications

- **Judgment under ignorance:** When you have very limited information, recognition alone can be a surprisingly accurate guide -- sometimes more accurate than extensive but unreliable knowledge
- **Marketing and branding:** The recognition heuristic explains brand premium: recognized brands are chosen over unrecognized ones even without evidence of superior quality
- **Ecological rationality:** Designing environments where the recognition heuristic leads to good outcomes (e.g., making important information more visible/memorable)
