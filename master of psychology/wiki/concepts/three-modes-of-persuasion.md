# Three Modes of Persuasion

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The three modes of persuasion (*pisteis*) are [[aristotle]]'s classification of the means by which a speaker persuades an audience. Stated in [[on-rhetoric]] 1.2.3-6, they are:

1. **[[Ethos]]** — persuasion through the speaker's character as projected in the speech. The audience believes the speaker because they seem trustworthy, wise, and well-intentioned. "Almost the most authoritative form of persuasion" (1.2.4).
2. **[[Pathos]]** — persuasion through the emotions of the audience. The speech puts the hearers into a certain emotional state that disposes them toward the desired judgment. "We do not give the same judgment when grieved and rejoicing" (1.2.5).
3. **[[Logos]]** — persuasion through logical argument. The speech demonstrates truth or apparent truth through [[enthymeme]]s (rhetorical syllogisms) and paradigms (examples). This includes the formal structures of proof (1.2.6).

These three modes are the "artistic" (*entechnic*) proofs — those the speaker invents and controls. They are contrasted with "non-artistic" (*atechnic*) proofs — pre-existing evidence like laws, witnesses, contracts, torture testimony, and oaths — which the speaker uses but does not create (1.2.2).

## Key Findings

- The three modes are exhaustive — Aristotle states there are three "and only three" artistic means of persuasion (Kennedy intro, p. 20)
- They map onto the three elements of any speech situation: **ethos** corresponds to the speaker, **pathos** to the audience, and **logos** to the speech/argument itself (cf. 1.3.1)
- To be a competent rhetorician, one must be able to "reason logically and be observant about characters and virtues and, third, about emotions" (1.2.7)
- Rhetoric is therefore described as an offshoot (*paraphues*) of dialectic (logic) and of ethics/politics (character and values) — a hybrid discipline
- The three modes interact: strong logos enhances ethos; appropriate pathos makes logos more persuasive; ethos predisposes the audience to emotional receptivity

## Mechanisms

The framework works because persuasion is not a single-channel process. Audiences evaluate speeches along multiple dimensions simultaneously:

| Mode | Question the Audience Asks | Cognitive Process |
|------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| [[Ethos]] | "Can I trust this speaker?" | Character assessment (heuristic) |
| [[Pathos]] | "How does this make me feel?" | Emotional appraisal (affective framing) |
| [[Logos]] | "Does this argument make sense?" | Logical evaluation (analytical) |

When all three align — a trustworthy speaker making an emotionally resonant, logically sound argument — persuasion is maximized. When they conflict (e.g., a logical argument from a distrusted speaker), the effect is weakened or negated.

## Applications

- **Speech design:** A persuasive speech should attend to all three modes — establish credibility, connect emotionally, and argue logically
- **Audience analysis:** Different audiences weight the modes differently. Popular audiences respond more to pathos and ethos; educated audiences respond more to logos (cf. 2.22.3)
- **Persuasion diagnosis:** When persuasion fails, diagnose which mode is deficient — the argument may be sound (logos) but the speaker untrusted (ethos) or the emotional framing wrong (pathos)
- **Defense against manipulation:** Understanding the three modes allows one to identify which channel a speaker is exploiting and evaluate it critically

## Open Questions

- The relationship between Aristotle's three modes and the Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo): ethos and pathos seem to correspond to "peripheral route" processing, while logos corresponds to the "central route." But Aristotle would resist this mapping — he treats ethos and pathos as substantive, not peripheral.
- How do the three modes map onto Cialdini's [[six-principles-of-influence]]? Authority ≈ ethos; liking ≈ ethos + pathos; social proof ≈ a form of logos (what most people believe); reciprocity, consistency, scarcity have no clear Aristotelian parallel.
