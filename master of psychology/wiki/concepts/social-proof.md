# Social Proof

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The principle of social proof states that we determine what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct (Lun et al., 2007). It applies especially to the way we decide what constitutes correct behavior: *we view a behavior as correct in a given situation to the degree that we see others performing it.* Social proof is the third of [[cialdini]]'s six principles of influence and functions as one of many [[click-whirr]] shortcuts that are normally adaptive but can be exploited.

## Key Findings

- Canned laughter causes audiences to laugh longer, more often, and to rate comedy material as funnier (Provine, 2000), with the effect strongest for poor-quality jokes (No-sanchuk & Lightstone, 1974)
- A group of people staring at an empty spot in the sky caused 80% of passersby to look up (Milgram, Bickman, & Berkowitz, 1969)
- Social proof-based film therapy eliminated dog phobias in children within four days, with effects lasting at least one month (Bandura, Grusec, & Menlove, 1967); film clips of *multiple* children were most effective (Bandura & Menlove, 1968)
- A 23-minute social proof film transformed the most severely withdrawn preschoolers into social leaders within six weeks (O'Connor, 1972)
- Pain perception itself is altered by social proof — subjects felt less pain when paired with a stoic companion (Craig & Prkrachin, 1978)
- Front-page suicide stories produce an average of 58 excess suicides within two months (Phillips, 1974) — the [[werther-effect]]
- Citizens of collectivistic cultures respond more strongly to social proof than those from individualistic cultures (Bond & Smith, 1996; Cialdini et al., 1999)

## Mechanisms

Social proof works through the same basic mechanism as all [[click-whirr]] responses: it provides a mental shortcut for determining correct behavior without requiring detailed analysis. The underlying logic — "if a lot of people are doing it, it must be the right thing to do" — is usually correct, which is why the shortcut persists.

The principle is amplified by two key conditions:
1. **[[uncertainty-and-social-proof]]** — When we are unsure, the situation is ambiguous, or we lack familiarity, we become especially dependent on others' behavior as a guide
2. **[[similarity-and-social-proof]]** — We follow most readily the lead of people who are similar to us in relevant ways (age, gender, social role, circumstances)

The principle can produce dangerous cascading effects through [[pluralistic-ignorance]], where everyone in a group looks to everyone else for guidance, nobody acts, and the collective inaction itself becomes the social proof that nothing is wrong.

## Applications

- **Marketing/Sales:** Testimonials, "fastest-growing" claims, visible queues, seeded tip jars, salted collection baskets, listed prior donors
- **Therapy:** Film-based modeling to eliminate phobias and social withdrawal in children (Bandura)
- **Emergency response:** Overcoming [[bystander-effect]] by singling out one specific individual and assigning them a clear role
- **Leadership:** The most influential leaders arrange group conditions to let social proof work in their favor (Watts & Dodd, 2007) — "simply get some members moving in the desired direction"
- **Persuasion defense:** Recognize counterfeit social evidence (manufactured laughter, fake testimonials); periodically check crowd behavior against objective facts
