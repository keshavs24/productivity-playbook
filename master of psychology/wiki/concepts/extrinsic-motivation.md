# Extrinsic Motivation

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Extrinsic motivation refers to behaviors that are instrumental for some separable consequence -- such as an external reward, social approval, avoidance of punishment, or the attainment of a valued outcome. Unlike [[intrinsic-motivation]], the activity is not done for its own sake but as a means to an end. SDT's critical contribution is demonstrating that extrinsic motivation is not a single category but varies widely in its degree of autonomy, from fully controlled to nearly autonomous.

## Key Findings

- Extrinsic motivation varies along the [[controlled-vs-autonomous-motivation|autonomy-control continuum]] through four regulatory styles:
  - **External regulation** -- behavior controlled by external contingencies (rewards, punishments). Most controlled. Highly dependent on ambient contingencies.
  - **Introjected regulation** -- partially internalized; motivated by guilt, shame, contingent self-esteem, ego involvement, fear of disapproval. Internally controlling; associated with pressure, tension, and conflict. Can drive behavior even absent external contingencies.
  - **Identified regulation** -- the person identifies with and accepts the value of the behavior. More autonomous. The person consciously values the activity's outcome.
  - **Integrated regulation** -- identifications have been fully integrated with one's other values and beliefs. Most autonomous form of extrinsic motivation. Experienced as volitional. Distinguished from intrinsic motivation only in that the activity is still done for its outcomes rather than inherent enjoyment.
- The degree of internalization reflects the degree to which behavioral regulation is relatively autonomous versus controlled (Ryan & Connell, 1989)
- More autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation yield higher quality persistence, performance, and well-being than controlled forms
- The internalization of extrinsic regulations is fueled by all three [[basic-psychological-needs]], with [[relatedness-need|relatedness]] playing a particularly salient role -- people tend to internalize the values and goals of those with whom they feel connected
- Introjected regulation connects directly to [[want-should-conflict]] -- the person has taken in the "should" but does not fully endorse it, creating internal tension

## Mechanisms

The process by which extrinsic motivation becomes more autonomous is called *internalization* -- the active process of taking in and transforming external regulations into self-regulations. When internalization proceeds optimally, it results in *integration* -- the regulation becomes fully assimilated into one's coherent sense of self.

Internalization is facilitated by social contexts that are:
1. **Autonomy-supportive** -- providing choice and encouraging self-regulation
2. **Competence-supporting** -- providing structure and positive informational feedback
3. **Relationally supportive** -- providing caring involvement and a sense of belonging

Controlling, rejecting, or overchallenging environments disrupt internalization, producing alienation, interpersonal and intrapersonal conflict, and less full engagement.

## Applications

- **Education:** Students who identify with and integrate the value of learning (identified/integrated regulation) show deeper engagement than those motivated by grades alone (external regulation) or fear of parental disapproval (introjected regulation)
- **Workplace:** Employees who internalize organizational values show greater commitment and performance than those controlled by incentive systems. This explains why [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]] shift matters -- monetary incentives can prevent internalization.
- **Health behavior:** Patients who autonomously internalize the value of health behaviors (diet, exercise, medication adherence) maintain them long-term; those who comply only under external pressure relapse
- **Parenting:** Supporting children's autonomy while providing rationales for rules facilitates internalization of social values
