# Bystander Effect

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The bystander effect is the paradoxical finding that the more witnesses there are to an emergency, the *less* likely any individual witness is to help. Contrary to the common-sense notion of "safety in numbers," a victim may actually have a better chance of receiving aid from a single bystander than from a crowd (Latane & Darley, 1968b; for a review, see Latane & Nida, 1981).

## Key Findings

- **Catherine Genovese case (March 1964):** 38 neighbors watched from their apartment windows as a woman was attacked and murdered over 35 minutes. Not one called the police. The case catalyzed the entire research program.
- **Epileptic seizure experiment** (Darley & Latane, 1968): Help for a student having a seizure dropped from 85% (one bystander) to 31% (five bystanders)
- **Smoke experiment** (Latane & Darley, 1968a): Reporting of smoke under a door dropped from 75% (alone) to 38% (three-person group) to 10% (with two coached passive bystanders)
- **Toronto study** (A. S. Ross, 1971): Emergency aid dropped from 90% (alone) to 16% (two passive bystanders present)
- **Unambiguous emergencies** (Clark & Word, 1972, 1974): When the emergency was *clear* (visibly injured maintenance man), help occurred 90-100% of the time regardless of group size. The bystander effect requires ambiguity.
- **Lee Alexis Wilson case (Chicago):** A university coed was beaten and strangled in daylight near the Art Institute; thousands passed; one man who heard a scream at 2 PM did not investigate "because no one else seemed to be paying attention"

## Mechanisms

Two mechanisms drive the bystander effect:

1. **Diffusion of responsibility** — With multiple potential helpers present, the personal responsibility felt by each individual is reduced. "Perhaps someone else will give or call for aid, perhaps someone else already has."
2. **[[pluralistic-ignorance]]** — In ambiguous situations, each bystander looks to others for cues about whether an emergency exists. Since everyone is trying to appear composed, everyone interprets others' calm as evidence that no emergency is occurring.

Three features of urban environments naturally amplify both mechanisms:
1. Cities are clamorous and distracting, making events harder to interpret
2. Cities are populous, meaning more co-witnesses
3. City dwellers are surrounded by strangers, among whom [[pluralistic-ignorance]] is strongest (Latane & Rodin, 1969)

## Applications

- **Victim strategy:** To overcome the bystander effect, explicitly label the emergency ("I need help — I'm having a stroke"), single out one specific individual ("You, sir, in the blue jacket"), and give them a specific task ("Call an ambulance"). This eliminates all three barriers: ambiguity, diffusion, and uncertainty about what to do.
- **Training/education:** Simply knowing about the bystander effect can inoculate people against it (demonstrated by Reader's Reports from people who overcame their passive impulses after reading Cialdini)
- **Organizational design:** In teams, assign explicit responsibility for safety monitoring and intervention to reduce diffusion
