# Low-Ball Technique

**Phase(s):** 1, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The low-ball technique is a compliance strategy in which an initial favorable offer or deal is made to secure a person's commitment to a decision, after which the terms are changed to be less favorable — yet the person typically maintains their commitment. The technique works because commitments "grow their own legs": once a decision is made, people generate new reasons to support it, so the decision stands even when the original reason (the favorable deal) is removed.

## Key Findings

- **Cialdini, Cacioppo, Bassett, & Miller (1978)**: Students asked to participate in a study — 56% agreed when asked generally. When then told it started at 7:00 AM, *none* backed out, and **95%** actually showed up. Control group told about 7:00 AM upfront: only **24%** agreed.
- **Car dealership practice**: Dealers offer prices $400+ below competitors to get a purchase decision, then remove the advantage through "calculation errors" or reduced trade-in values. The customer stays because new self-generated reasons support the choice (Brockner & Rubin, 1985; Teger, 1980).
- **Replicated across contexts**: Brownstein & Katzev (1985); Burger & Petty (1981); Joule (1987).
- **Works in both directions**: The tactic works whether a positive aspect of the deal is removed *or* a negative aspect is added — as long as the initial commitment has been secured.
- **Iowa energy conservation (Pallak, Cook, & Sullivan, 1980)**: Homeowners promised newspaper publicity saved 422 cubic feet of gas per month. When publicity was cancelled, they *increased* savings to 15.5% — the commitment had grown its own legs.

## Mechanisms

1. **Commitment as anchor**: The initial decision creates a psychological anchor. Once a person has decided, they begin to see themselves as someone who made this choice.
2. **Self-generated justification**: After deciding, people generate new reasons to support the decision — lifestyle fit, aesthetic appeal, convenience, etc. These reasons are independent of the original inducement.
3. **Leg multiplication**: The new reasons create multiple "legs" supporting the decision. When the original leg (the favorable deal) is pulled away, the remaining legs keep the decision standing.
4. **Self-image protection**: Reversing the decision would mean admitting to having been fooled, which threatens self-image. It's psychologically easier to accept the worse terms than to confront the manipulation.

## Applications

- **Car sales**: The canonical application — offer a great price, build commitment through paperwork and test drives, then "discover" an error requiring a price increase.
- **Personal relationships**: Cialdini identifies his neighbor Tim as "a true low-ball aficionado" — offering promises of marriage and sobriety to win Sara back, then reneging after she committed.
- **Pro-social behavior**: The Iowa energy conservation study shows the technique can be used ethically — initial incentives can spark lasting behavior change that persists after incentives are removed.
- **Recruitment**: Offering attractive initial conditions for a job or program, then revealing less attractive aspects after the person has committed.

## Open Questions

- Is there a threshold of term-change magnitude beyond which the technique fails? (At what point does the cost increase break the commitment?)
- How does the technique interact with [[loss-aversion]]? The sunk effort and self-generated reasons may function as "losses" that make backing out feel costly.
- Can awareness of the technique inoculate against it? Cialdini suggests the "heart-of-hearts" test as a defense.
- What are the ethical boundaries of using low-ball for pro-social purposes (as in the Iowa study)?
