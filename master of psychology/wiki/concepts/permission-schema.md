# Permission Schema

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The permission schema is a mental framework for reasoning about social rules of the form "If you want to take action A, you must first satisfy condition B." Cheng and Holyoak (1985) proposed that people have evolved or learned general schemas for permission rules, and that these schemas enable much better logical reasoning than abstract conditional tasks. The permission schema explains why people who fail the abstract [[wason-selection-task]] succeed dramatically when the same logical structure is framed as a social rule to be enforced.

## Key Findings

- Griggs and Cox (1982): 74% of participants selected both logically correct cards when the Wason task was framed as enforcing a drinking-age law (vs. ~10% with abstract content) ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch10]])
- Cheng and Holyoak (1985): even with unfamiliar content (cholera vaccination for entering a country), providing a permission rationale dramatically improved performance -- ruling out mere familiarity as the explanation
- Cosmides (1989) and Gigerenzer and Hug (1992) extended the idea with **social contract theory**: humans evolved specialized cognitive mechanisms for detecting cheaters -- those who take benefits without meeting obligations
- Gigerenzer and Hug's key finding: identical logical structure, but cheating condition (school board detecting illegal attendance) = 80% correct vs. non-cheating condition (visiting official checking whether a rule exists) = only 45% correct

## Mechanisms

The permission schema activates a cheater-detection algorithm: when reasoning about a social rule, people naturally check (1) whether someone took the benefit (turn over the "action taken" card) and (2) whether someone failed to meet the precondition (turn over the "precondition not met" card). This naturally implements modus tollens, which is otherwise difficult. The schema is domain-specific -- it applies to deontic (ought/permission) reasoning but not to abstract indicative reasoning.

## Applications

- **Rule enforcement:** Design compliance checks and audit procedures to leverage people's natural cheater-detection abilities rather than requiring abstract logical analysis
- **Communication:** Frame arguments and policies as social contracts rather than abstract logical propositions to make them easier for audiences to evaluate
- **Education:** Teaching conditional logic through permission/obligation examples is more effective than abstract formal instruction

## Open Questions
- Is the permission schema innate (evolutionary adaptation) or learned (cultural acquisition)? The debate between Cosmides (nativist) and Cheng & Holyoak (empiricist) remains unresolved.
- Does the cheater-detection mechanism extend to altruist-detection (detecting those who give benefits without receiving obligations)?
