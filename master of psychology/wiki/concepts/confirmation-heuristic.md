# Confirmation Heuristic

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The confirmation heuristic is the tendency to **intuitively use selective data when testing hypotheses**, searching for instances where the variable of interest is present rather than absent. People naturally seek information that confirms their existing beliefs and hypotheses rather than information that could disconfirm them. Bazerman and Moore elevate this to a fourth general cognitive heuristic alongside [[availability]], [[representativeness]], and [[anchoring-and-adjustment]].

## Key Findings

- People assume statements are true in the absence of contrary evidence (Gilbert, 1991; Trabasso, Rollins, & Shaughnessy, 1971) ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch1]])
- The confirmation heuristic leads to the confirmation bias: searching for and interpreting evidence in a way that supports conclusions we favored at the outset (Nickerson, 1998) ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch3]])
- It can explain [[anchoring-and-adjustment]]: an irrelevant initial hypothesis holds undue sway because we test it by seeking confirming evidence
- Positive hypothesis testing triggers hindsight bias: we too quickly dismiss the possibility that things could have turned out differently
- The marijuana/delinquency example: people search for confirming cases (marijuana users who are delinquents) while neglecting the full 2x2 table (including users who are not delinquent, non-users who are delinquent, and non-users who are not delinquent) (Klayman & Ha, 1987; Fernbach, Darlow, & Sloman, 2011)
- Wason's (1960) 2-4-6 task: only 6 of 29 participants found the rule on first guess because they sought confirming rather than disconfirming sequences
- Gilovich (1991): for confirming evidence we ask "May I believe it?" but for disconfirming evidence we ask "Must I believe it?"
- Lord, Ross, and Lepper (1979): death penalty supporters and opponents both found the same mixed evidence to confirm their pre-existing views
- Fischer, Greitemeyer, and Frey (2008): tired individuals show even stronger preference for viewpoint-consistent information

## Mechanisms

Two primary drivers: (1) **Memory retrieval bias** -- the mere consideration of hypotheses makes consistent information selectively accessible in memory (Gilbert, 1991); Loftus (1975) showed leading questions can implant false memories. (2) **Search strategy bias** -- we search selectively, giving special credence to information that supports desired conclusions (Kunda, 1990); political partisans selectively consume media that affirms their views. These are both System 1 processes that operate automatically; overcoming the confirmation heuristic requires deliberate System 2 effort.

## Applications

- **Hiring:** After tentatively deciding to hire someone, we search for confirming positive references rather than disconfirming criminal records or negative information
- **Product launches:** After deciding on a new product line, managers seek data supporting the decision rather than searching for reasons it might fail
- **Thaler's consulting firms:** "Yes Person" (confirms client ideas) vs. "Devil's Advocate" (top 10 reasons not to pursue) -- Devil's Advocate would be more useful, but clients prefer Yes Person
- **Scientific method:** The scientific emphasis on falsification is an explicit attempt to counteract the confirmation heuristic
- **Debiasing:** Consider the opposite; seek out perspectives different from your own; design decision processes that require identifying disconfirming evidence

## Anderson's Scientific Discovery Evidence (Ch.10)

Anderson ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch10]]) adds evidence from scientific reasoning contexts:

- **Dunbar (1993) gene regulation study**: Undergraduates recreating Monod and Jacob's Nobel Prize-winning research on gene regulation uniformly began by testing activation hypotheses (their favored theory) rather than inhibition mechanisms (the actual answer). Only a minority eventually searched for alternative hypotheses.
- **fMRI evidence** (Fugelsang & Dunbar, 2005): When participants viewed evidence inconsistent with their hypothesis, the **anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** showed greater activation -- the same conflict-detection region that activates during Stroop tasks. This suggests confirmation bias engages basic cognitive control mechanisms, not a unique bias module.
- **Science as institutional correction**: Individual scientists show strong confirmation bias, but science as a social institution corrects for it through adversarial peer review. Competitive scientists are motivated to find flaws in each other's work (Nickerson, 1998).
- **Collaboration as debiasing**: Okada and Simon (1997) found pairs of participants were much more successful at finding the correct inhibitory mechanism than individuals, because collaboration forces explicit articulation and prompts requests for explanation.
- **Pasteur example**: Even Louis Pasteur published only the 10% of results that confirmed his theory about spontaneous generation, dismissing the other 90% as experimental error (Geison, 1995) -- yet he turned out to be right, illustrating that confirmation bias is not always maladaptive.

## Ariely's Expectation-Confirmation Evidence (Predictably Irrational, Ch. 10)

Ariely ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]]) provides behavioral evidence that expectations function as a form of confirmation bias operating at the perceptual level:

- **Beer taste experiments:** When participants were told beer contained vinegar *before* tasting, they "confirmed" the expectation of bad taste and disliked it. Those told *after* tasting liked it just as much as uninformed participants — demonstrating that confirmation operates during experience, not retroactively.
- **Football fan perception:** Two friends watching the same game — one an Eagles fan, one a Giants fan — see the same pass differently. Each "confirms" their team's narrative. Ariely notes this maps to political, religious, and ethnic conflicts worldwide.
- **Brand attribution:** When obviously true statements ("the sun is yellow") were attributed to brands or political parties, people began questioning them — their distrust-driven expectations led them to seek disconfirming evidence for truths they would normally accept without question.
- **Neural evidence (McClure et al. Coke/Pepsi fMRI):** Brand expectations activated dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), which modulated the hedonic experience — expectations literally changed the neural processing of taste, not just the cognitive evaluation.
- **Key extension:** While classic confirmation bias describes biased *information search and interpretation*, Ariely's [[expectation-assimilation]] shows that confirmation operates at the level of *perception itself* — expectations don't just bias how we evaluate experience, they alter the experience.

## Open Questions
- Nelson, McKenzie, Cottrell, and Sejnowski (2010) found that given multiple pieces of information, people do a good job choosing the most useful -- suggesting the confirmation tendency can be overcome. Under what conditions?
- How does the confirmation heuristic interact with [[social-proof]]? (Do we confirm by looking at what others believe?)
- How does the ACC-based conflict detection mechanism relate to the broader Type 1 / Type 2 dual-process framework? (Anderson's neuroscience evidence suggests confirmation bias is a Type 1 default that Type 2 must actively override.)
- Can Ariely's "blind" condition approach (presenting arguments without attribution to strip away expectation-driven confirmation) be systematically applied to conflict resolution?
