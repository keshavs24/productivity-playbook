# Fixed-Pie Bias

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The fixed-pie bias (also called the "mythical fixed pie") is the assumption that one's own interests are diametrically opposed to the other party's in a negotiation. Under this assumption, the negotiation is purely zero-sum -- any gain for one side is necessarily a loss for the other. This belief prevents negotiators from searching for mutually beneficial tradeoffs that could expand the total value available.

## Key Findings

- **Thompson (2001):** Even when two sides want the exact same outcome, negotiators often settle on a different outcome or reach impasse. Thompson calls this the "incompatibility bias" -- assuming one's interests are at odds with the other party's when they may actually be aligned. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch12]])
- **Cold War SALT example (Rep. Floyd Spence):** "If it is in their interests, it can't be in our best interest" -- a textbook illustration of fixed-pie thinking. In reality, arms reduction treaties benefited both sides by reducing defense spending and nuclear risk.
- **Curhan, Neale, and Ross (2004):** Negotiators value a possible outcome more after they propose it and less after the other side proposes it, demonstrating that the fixed-pie assumption distorts evaluation of identical proposals based solely on their source.
- The fixed-pie assumption is resistant to correction because it becomes self-reinforcing: when negotiators expect zero-sum dynamics, they behave competitively, which elicits competitive responses, confirming the assumption.

## Mechanisms

The fixed-pie bias arises from:
1. **Overgeneralization from win-lose contexts:** Many real-world competitions are genuinely zero-sum (sports, admissions, market share battles). People transfer these expectations to mixed-motive situations where value creation is possible.
2. **Salience of the claiming dimension:** In any negotiation involving both value creation and value claiming, the competitive claiming element tends to dominate attention.
3. **[[reactive-devaluation]]:** Concessions offered by the other party are devalued simply because the other party proposed them, reinforcing the perception of opposition.
4. **Lack of information about the other party's interests:** Without knowing what the other side cares about, the default assumption is that they want exactly the opposite of what you want.

## Applications

- **Negotiation preparation (Phase 5):** Before any negotiation, explicitly challenge the assumption that interests are opposed. Ask: "What does the other side actually care about? Where might our priorities differ?"
- **Interest-based negotiation:** The antidote to the fixed-pie bias is the shift from positions to interests (Fisher, Ury, & Patton, 1981). When positions conflict, interests may still be compatible. See the Camp David Accords example in [[value-creation-in-negotiation]].
- **Conflict resolution:** In organizational and interpersonal conflicts, explicitly identifying where interests are compatible can break deadlocks that the fixed-pie assumption creates.
- **First offers:** Avoid making claims about the other side's interests before verifying them. The fixed-pie assumption makes negotiators believe they already know what the other side wants.

## Negotiation Genius Extensions

[[malhotra|Malhotra]] and [[bazerman|Bazerman]] in [[negotiation-genius|Negotiation Genius]] provide practical antidotes to the fixed-pie bias through the entire Part I toolkit:

- **The Chris exclusivity story is a textbook fixed-pie case.** The U.S. negotiation team assumed exclusivity was a zero-sum issue (one side wins, the other loses). By asking "why," Chris discovered that the supplier's refusal protected a trivial commitment — an exception of 250 lbs/year to a cousin. The "pie" was not fixed; there was room for both sides to get exactly what they wanted. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch3-investigative]])
- **[[investigative-negotiation]] is the primary antidote.** The seven principles systematically combat the fixed-pie assumption by uncovering hidden interests, reconciling apparently incompatible demands, and reframing demands as opportunities. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch3-investigative]])
- **Value creation strategies explicitly overcome fixed-pie thinking:** logrolling, adding issues, and contingency contracts all assume that the pie is *not* fixed and can be expanded. The Moms.com case demonstrates that moving from compromise (fixed-pie thinking) to value-maximizing logrolling doubles the total value created. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch2-creating-value]])
- **The fixed-pie bias leads to [[reactive-devaluation]].** Malhotra and Bazerman cite the Ross arms-reduction study: when told a proposal came from Gorbachev, 56% of Americans thought it favored the USSR; when told it came from Reagan, only 27% thought so. The identical proposal was evaluated entirely based on its source. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch1-claiming-value]])

## Open Questions
- Can the fixed-pie bias be reduced through training, or does it recur even after debiasing interventions? (Malhotra and Bazerman's evidence suggests that MBA simulation experience helps, but the bias persists)
- How does culture affect the strength of the fixed-pie assumption? Do collectivistic cultures show different patterns?
- Is the fixed-pie bias stronger when negotiators have no prior relationship vs. when they have an ongoing relationship?
