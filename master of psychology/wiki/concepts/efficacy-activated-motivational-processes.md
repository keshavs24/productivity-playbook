# Efficacy-Activated Motivational Processes

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Self-efficacy beliefs regulate motivation through three forms of cognitive motivators: causal attributions ([[attribution-theory]]), outcome expectancies (expectancy-value theory), and cognized goals (goal theory) (Bandura, 1997, pp.122-137, Figure 4.5). Most human motivation is cognitively generated -- future states are converted into current motivators through forethought that embodies cognized goals. Efficacy beliefs play a central role in the cognitive regulation of all three forms.

## Key Findings

### Causal Attributions (pp.123-125)
- People who credit successes to personal capabilities and failures to insufficient effort undertake difficult tasks and persist; those who ascribe failures to deficiencies in ability display low strivings and give up readily
- **Self-efficacy mediates the effect of causal attributions on performance almost entirely** (Relich, Debus, & Walker, 1986; Schunk & Gunn, 1986; Schunk & Rice, 1986) -- the types of factors singled out by attribution theory serve as conveyors of efficacy-relevant information that influence performance mainly by altering people's beliefs in their efficacy
- Self-efficacious individuals attribute failures to insufficient effort or situational impediments; those with low efficacy attribute failures to lack of ability -- this pattern is reproducible across academic, interpersonal, physical, and health domains. Connects to [[self-serving-attribution]].
- Attribution retraining alone has limited success; sociocognitive programs fostering personal enablement produce uniformly beneficial changes
- Causal attributions serve as supplementary motivators but not as a primary mode of generating motivation

### Expectancy-Value Theory (pp.125-127)
- A low sense of efficacy can nullify the motivating potential of alluring outcome expectations; firm efficacy can sustain efforts over prolonged periods despite uncertain or negative outcomes
- When efficacy beliefs are controlled statistically, outcome expectations contribute little to prediction of behavior
- Perceived self-efficacy is a broader construct than effort expectancy -- people judge capabilities in terms of knowledge, skills, and strategies, not solely exertion
- When perceived self-efficacy is added to expectancy-value models (including Ajzen's perceived behavioral control), perceived control makes no independent contribution (Dzewaltowski, 1989)
- Self-evaluative outcomes from fulfilling personal standards may be valued more than tangible payoffs; self-evaluative outcomes often override tangible rewards (Bandura, 1986a)

### Goal Theory (pp.128-137)
- Explicit, challenging goals enhance motivation across diverse domains (Locke & Latham, 1990)
- Self-efficacy is one of the important self-influences through which personal standards create powerful motivational effects
- **Three self-reactive influences** mediate goal motivation (Figure 4.6, Bandura & Cervone, 1986): (1) affective self-evaluative reactions, (2) perceived self-efficacy for goal attainment, (3) adjustment of personal standards
- The more self-reactive influences brought to bear, the greater the effort exerted
- **Proximal subgoals** are far more effective than distal goals alone: proximal subgoals produce rising efficacy beliefs, achievement gains, and intrinsic interest; distal goals alone have no demonstrable effects (Figure 4.7, Bandura & Schunk, 1981)
- **Goal specificity:** Specific, attainable goals produce higher performance than general intentions (Locke & Latham, 1990; Bandura & Cervone, 1983)
- **Goal challenge:** Higher goals (if accepted and committed to) produce harder work and better performance, but unrealistically high goals produce repeated failure that takes its toll on efficacy
- **Hierarchical goal systems:** Proximal subgoals invest activities with personal significance by linking to higher-level goals. Self-motivation as recurrent proximal self-challenge and evaluative reward.
- **Dual self-regulation:** Human self-motivation requires both discrepancy production (proactive: setting challenging standards) AND discrepancy reduction (reactive: adjusting effort to match standards). Bandura critiques purely reactive negative feedback control systems (Carver & Scheier; Powers) as insufficient.

## Mechanisms

The three motivational subsystems operate through efficacy beliefs:

1. **Attribution pathway:** Performance outcome --> Causal attribution --> Efficacy belief change --> Altered motivation and future performance. Self-efficacy mediates the attribution-performance link almost entirely.
2. **Expectancy pathway:** Outcome expectations set the ceiling of motivation, but efficacy beliefs determine whether people act on those expectations. Low efficacy nullifies even attractive outcome expectations.
3. **Goal pathway:** Efficacy beliefs --> Goal level set --> Commitment strength --> Strategy selection --> Effort mobilization --> Effort intensification when falling short. Proximal subgoals create self-motivating cycles of challenge, self-evaluation, and satisfaction.

## Applications

- **Goal architecture:** Design hierarchical goal systems with challenging but achievable proximal subgoals linked to meaningful distal aspirations
- **Attribution coaching:** Help people attribute failures to controllable factors (effort, strategy) rather than uncontrollable ones (ability) -- but do this through building actual competencies, not just relabeling
- **Self-monitoring systems:** Create feedback mechanisms that make progress visible against proximal standards, framed as gains
- **Proactive standard-setting:** Encourage people to set their own challenging standards rather than simply react to assigned goals -- self-set goals produce greater commitment
