# Relatedness (Basic Psychological Need)

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Relatedness concerns feeling socially connected (Bowlby, 1979; Baumeister & Leary, 1995; Ryan, 1995). It is about belonging and feeling significant among others. Critically, relatedness is not just about receiving care but also about giving -- experiencing oneself as contributing to others and to social organizations beyond oneself, what Angyal (1941) described as *homonomy*. People experience relatedness through both feeling connected to close others and being a significant member of social groups.

Relatedness is experienced both in being cared about *and* in caring. The need is satisfied when others show concern toward the individual *and* when the individual has opportunities to be benevolent toward others, as both directions of caring enhance a sense of connectedness (Deci, La Guardia, Moller, Scheiner, & Ryan, 2006; Deci & Ryan, 2014a; Weinstein & Ryan, 2010).

## Key Findings

- Relatedness plays a particularly salient role in the *internalization* of [[extrinsic-motivation]]. People naturally tend to internalize the values and goals of those with whom they are or wish to be connected or affiliated (Ryan & Lynch, 1989)
- Feelings of relational security are necessary for curiosity and intrinsic exploration in children (Anderson, Manoogian, & Reznick, 1976; Bernier, Matte-Gagne, Belanger, & Whipple, 2014; Frodi, Bridges, & Grolnick, 1985)
- Nonautonomous connections do not satisfy the relatedness need, except in degraded forms
- People must perceive that others care for them unconditionally rather than conditionally for true relatedness satisfaction (see Relationships Motivation Theory, Chapter 12)
- Parents' use of conditional regard creates a conflict between the autonomy and relatedness needs, producing psychological disturbances (Assor, Roth, & Deci, 2004)

## Mechanisms

Relatedness operates as both a direct need and as a facilitator of other psychological processes:

1. **Direct need:** Feeling connected, belonging, and significant is essential for wellness in its own right
2. **Internalization catalyst:** The desire to connect with relevant groups (family, peers, society) drives internalization of social values, norms, and practices. This is why alienation from parents leads teenagers to reject parental values but readily adopt peer standards.
3. **Secure base for exploration:** In development, relational security provides the foundation from which children can explore -- paralleling attachment theory (Bowlby, 1979)

While [[competence-need|competence]] and [[autonomy-need|autonomy]] are invariably proximally implicated in maintaining [[intrinsic-motivation]] for a particular activity, relatedness provides an important developmental support. Some intrinsically motivated activities can be pursued solitarily, but even these are enabled by a background of relational security.

## Applications

- **Relational support** includes the caring involvement of others
- In education: conveying relatedness, inclusion, and a sense of community fosters personal well-being and connection to school
- In workplaces: caring, responsive organizational cultures support relatedness
- In health care: therapeutic alliance and practitioner warmth satisfy relatedness needs
- In parenting: unconditional positive regard (not contingent on performance) satisfies relatedness while supporting autonomy

## BPNT Findings on Relatedness (Ch. 10)

- **Within-person fluctuations:** Daily variations in relatedness satisfaction independently predict daily well-being, including on weekends when people spend more time with those to whom they feel close (Reis et al., 2000; Ryan, Bernstein, & Brown, 2010)
- **Vitality through relatedness:** Falling in love, helping others, and connecting with close others enhances subjective vitality. Weinstein & Ryan (2010) showed that helping for autonomous reasons enhanced vitality through all three needs, including relatedness. Deci et al. (2006) showed giving autonomy support to best friends enhanced vitality for the giver.
- **Relatedness frustration and materialism:** Loneliness (relatedness frustration) is a strong predictor of materialism, and materialism predicts greater loneliness -- a vicious cycle (Pieters, 2013). [[Need-frustration]] in the relatedness domain pushes individuals toward [[extrinsic-aspirations]] as compensatory substitutes.
- **Relatedness and autonomy are positively correlated** at aggregate levels (BPNT Proposition IV), contradicting claims by Markus et al. (1996) and early feminist perspectives that they are in opposition. SDT clearly distinguishes autonomy from independence/separateness.
- **Nature exposure** enhances feelings of relatedness (connectedness to nature), which partially mediates nature's prosocial effects (Weinstein, Przybylski, & Ryan, 2009)
- **Cross-cultural universality:** Relatedness satisfaction predicted unique variance in well-being across Belgium, China, U.S., and Peru (Chen et al., 2015)

## RMT Findings on Relatedness (Ch. 12)

[[Relationships-motivation-theory|Relationships Motivation Theory (RMT)]] is the sixth and final SDT mini-theory, focused specifically on the role of relatedness (and its interplay with [[autonomy-need|autonomy]]) in close relationships. Key findings:

- **Relatedness is intrinsic, not instrumental.** RMT distinguishes *ultimate causes* (evolutionary advantages of social bonds) from *proximal intrinsic motivations* (people are drawn to connect because caring and love are intrinsically satisfying, not because of calculated extrinsic benefits).
- **Not all social interactions satisfy relatedness.** Transactional, role-bound, or agenda-driven interactions do not yield relatedness satisfaction, even within close relationships. What matters is perceived autonomous and authentic caring between self and other.
- **Autonomy and relatedness are complementary.** This is a fundamental tenet of SDT and the central thesis of RMT. Satisfying one need facilitates the other; [[conditional-regard]] is harmful precisely because it forces a trade-off between the two (see [[autonomy-in-relationships]]).
- **Need satisfaction within relationships mediates the link between social support and wellness.** It is the quality of [[need-satisfaction-in-relationships|need satisfaction within relationships]], not sheer quantity of contact, that predicts attachment security, relationship satisfaction, and well-being (La Guardia, Ryan, Couchman, & Deci, 2000; Patrick et al., 2007).
- **Attachment security varies within persons across relationships** as a function of relationship-specific need satisfaction. About 35% of variance is between-person (working model), but more than half is within-person and predicted by need satisfaction with specific partners (La Guardia et al., 2000).
- **Giving autonomy support to others satisfies the giver's relatedness need.** Deci, La Guardia, et al. (2006) showed that *giving* [[autonomy-support]] had a stronger relation to the person's own well-being than *receiving* it.
- **Ostracism triggers "social pain"** involving neural activation patterns shared with physical pain (Eisenberger, Lieberman, & Williams, 2003). The distress from social exclusion (Cyberball paradigm) is mediated by lower relatedness need satisfaction (Legate et al., 2013).

## Open Questions

- How does digital connectedness (social media, online communities) relate to genuine relatedness need satisfaction?
- What is the relationship between relatedness and Baumeister & Leary's (1995) "need to belong"? They are closely related but not identical.
- How does the vicious cycle between loneliness and materialism (Pieters, 2013) operate mechanistically? What breaks the cycle?
- How does the projection/selection mechanism for [[conditional-regard]] operate -- do people raised with conditional regard primarily *select* conditionally regarding partners, or do they defensively *project* conditional regard onto neutral partners? Evidence (Moller et al., 2016) suggests both processes occur.
