# Emotion Regulation

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Emotion regulation is the second branch of the [[emotional-intelligence-model]]. It encompasses the ability to monitor, evaluate, and modify emotional reactions in oneself and in others to promote adaptive functioning. In the self, this involves meta-experiences of mood — a regulatory system that monitors and evaluates one's own emotional states and sometimes acts to change them. In others, it involves managing impressions, influencing others' emotional states, and (at its most sophisticated) charismatic leadership or emotional labor.

Emotion regulation is not simply suppression of emotion. The emotionally intelligent person does not avoid negative feelings but rather engages in sophisticated management: maintaining positive moods, repairing negative moods, tolerating painful emotions when they serve growth, and even seeking emotional experiences (including negative ones through aesthetic engagement) to build emotional range and resilience.

## Key Findings

- **Meta-experiences of mood** function as a regulatory system. People have experiences *about* their moods — evaluating which moods are acceptable, understandable, and controllable. These meta-experiences are conscious and measurable via the State and Trait Meta-Mood Scales (SMMS, TMMS) developed by Salovey and Mayer.
- **Mood maintenance vs. mood repair asymmetry:** People are generally more motivated to maintain positive moods and terminate negative ones. The impact of mood on memory encoding and recall is "generally stronger for positive than negative mood states" (Isen, 1984). This asymmetry echoes [[loss-aversion]] — negative emotional states are experienced as more urgent to resolve.
- **Countervailing evaluation:** A negative mood evaluated as "under control and soon-to-change" is far less destructive than one seen as "unacceptable and long-lasting." The evaluation frame around the mood is itself regulable.
- **Associate selection:** Mood can be regulated by strategically choosing social contacts. Associating with non-threatening successful others boosts positive affect; associating with people whose successes are in domains important to one's self-concept can trigger envy (Tesser's self-evaluation maintenance).
- **Negative state relief:** Acting helpfully toward others can terminate negative moods — altruistic behavior partly functions as emotion regulation (Cialdini & Kenrick, 1976).
- **Aesthetic emotional practice:** People seek out emotional experiences of all kinds through art, fiction, tragedy, and music. This may function as practice for experiencing negative affect with little real-world consequence, building emotional tolerance and range.
- **Regulation of others' emotions** is dual-edged: it can serve prosocial ends (charismatic motivation, empathic support) or antisocial ones (manipulation, sociopathic exploitation, emotional labor that commodifies feeling).

## Mechanisms

**In the Self:**
1. **Monitoring:** Meta-mood system continuously tracks current emotional state
2. **Evaluation:** Assessing whether the mood is appropriate, understandable, and within normal range
3. **Regulation strategies:**
   - Behavioral: changing activities, choosing associates, helping others
   - Cognitive: reappraising the meaning of the mood, seeking countervailing information, using conscious mechanisms to override automatic negative associations
   - Motivational: leveraging the asymmetry between maintenance and repair drives
   - Experiential: seeking emotional range through aesthetic engagement

**In Others:**
1. **Impression management** (Goffman): controlling self-presentation to evoke desired emotional responses
2. **Emotional labor** (Hochschild): commercially managed regulation of others' emotional experiences
3. **Charismatic influence** (Wasielewski): leaders who regulate followers' emotions to motivate action
4. **Strategic communication:** Knowing when to suppress (MUM effect — suppressing negative communications to maintain relationships) and when to be honest

## Applications

- **Therapy:** Teaching meta-mood awareness and regulation strategies to clients with mood disorders
- **Leadership:** Understanding charisma as the ability to regulate followers' emotional states; training leaders in emotional impression management
- **Negotiation:** Managing one's own emotional reactions during high-stakes interactions; strategically influencing the counterpart's emotional state
- **Health:** Emotional regulation deficits linked to psychosomatic complaints, depression, and anxiety; interventions targeting regulation skills may improve health outcomes
- **Persuasion:** Connects to [[cialdini]]'s principles — effective persuaders regulate the emotional context of influence attempts

## Open Questions

- **What distinguishes healthy regulation from suppression?** The paper implies regulation is broader than suppression, but the boundary is not precisely drawn.
- **Is emotion regulation more trait-like or state-like?** The SMMS measures momentary regulation; the TMMS measures longer-term style. How stable are these individual differences?
- **How do regulation strategies interact?** Can countervailing evaluation be combined with associate selection? Is there an optimal regulation portfolio?
- **Cultural variation in regulation norms:** Different cultures likely have different norms for which emotions should be regulated, how, and in what contexts. The paper does not address this.
