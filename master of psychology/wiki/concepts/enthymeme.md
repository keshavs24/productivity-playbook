# Enthymeme

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The enthymeme is a rhetorical syllogism — a deductive argument drawn from probable premises rather than certain ones. [[Aristotle]] calls it the "body" (*soma*) of persuasion (1.1.3) and considers it the most important element of [[logos]], one of the [[three-modes-of-persuasion]].

Unlike a formal syllogism in dialectic (which requires all premises to be stated and necessarily true), the enthymeme:
- Is drawn from **fewer premises** — typically omitting one premise that the audience can supply from common knowledge (1.2.13)
- Deals with **probabilities** (*eikota*) and **signs** (*semeia*), not logical certainties (1.2.14)
- Is addressed to a **popular audience** that would find a chain of formal syllogisms tedious and hard to follow (2.22.3)

**Example:** "Socrates is virtuous; for he is wise." The unstated major premise ("All wise people are virtuous") is supplied by the audience from commonly held opinion (*endoxa*). This makes the argument feel natural and conversational rather than pedantic.

## Key Findings

- **Two species of enthymeme** (2.22.14-15):
  - **Demonstrative** (*deiktikon*): shows that something is or is not the case, drawing conclusions from what is agreed
  - **Refutative** (*elentikon*): draws the opposite conclusion from the opponent's premises; refutative enthymemes are more popular with audiences because they bring opposites together in brief form (2.23.30)

- **Four sources** of enthymemes (2.25.8):
  1. **Probability** (*eikos*) — what happens for the most part
  2. **Paradigm** (example) — parallel cases supporting the conclusion
  3. **Tekmerion** (necessary sign) — irrefutable evidence
  4. **Semeion** (fallible sign) — refutable evidence

- **The materials (premises) come from other disciplines** — politics, ethics, and the specific subject at hand. The **formal strategies come from [[topoi]]** — twenty-eight common lines of argument (2.23) and nine fallacious patterns (2.24).

- **Maxims** (*gnomae*) are the conclusions or premises of enthymemes with the syllogistic framework removed. "A maxim is a general statement about actions... enthymemes are rather like syllogisms about such things" (2.21.2). Adding the cause ("for...") transforms a maxim into an enthymeme.

- **Practical guidelines:**
  - Do not draw conclusions from too far back — this makes the argument unclear
  - Do not include all steps — this makes it tedious; "the hearer supplies it" (1.2.13)
  - The uneducated are more persuasive before crowds because they argue from particular instances near the audience's experience (2.22.3)
  - The more premises are drawn from the specific subject, the more the argument shifts from rhetoric into that discipline (1.2.21)

## Mechanisms

The enthymeme works through three reinforcing psychological mechanisms:

1. **Audience co-creation:** By leaving a premise unstated, the speaker invites the audience to complete the argument internally. This creates a sense of shared reasoning and intellectual participation.
2. **Cognitive ease:** Shorter, less formal arguments are easier to process. A popular audience will grow impatient with rigorous chains of deduction.
3. **Social anchoring:** Drawing from *endoxa* (commonly held opinions) means the enthymeme starts from what the audience already believes, making the conclusion feel natural rather than imposed.

## Applications

- **Constructing enthymemes:** Start from what the audience believes (their *endoxa*), select the appropriate topos (line of argument), and express the conclusion with one premise — let the audience supply the other
- **Maxim as enthymeme seed:** State a provocative general principle, then add "for..." with a supporting reason to create an enthymeme (2.21.2)
- **Refutation:** Attack opponent's enthymemes by showing the probability is not general, the sign is not necessary, or the example has counter-cases (2.25)
- **Identifying fallacious enthymemes:** Watch for the nine fallacious patterns (2.24) — verbal tricks, post hoc reasoning, false signs, confusing particular with general

## Open Questions

- Modern scholars debate whether the defining feature of the enthymeme is the "missing premise" or the use of probability. Aristotle himself emphasizes the latter — the real difference from a syllogism is context (popular audience vs. philosophical discussion) and subject matter (probabilities vs. certainties).
- How does the enthymeme relate to modern argumentation theory and informal logic? Toulmin's model (claim, data, warrant) resembles the enthymeme structure (conclusion, stated premise, unstated premise).
