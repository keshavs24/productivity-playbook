# Uncertainty and Social Proof

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Uncertainty is the first of two key conditions that maximize the power of [[social-proof]]. When people are unsure of themselves, when the situation is unclear or ambiguous, when they lack familiarity with the context — they are most likely to look to the actions of others and accept those actions as correct (Sechrist & Stangor, 2007; Wooten & Reed, 1998; Zitek & Hebl, 2007). Uncertainty is, as Cialdini puts it, "the right-hand man of the principle of social proof."

## Key Findings

- The doomsday cult studied by Festinger, Riecken, and Schachter (1964) became desperate for social proof (proselytization) precisely when their shaken confidence made them most uncertain
- Sylvan Goldman's shopping cart (1934) was adopted only after he hired paid shoppers to model its use — customers were unfamiliar with the device and uncertain about how to behave (Dauten, 2004)
- The [[bystander-effect]] is driven by uncertainty — when an emergency is ambiguous, bystanders look to others; when it is unambiguous (Clark & Word, 1972, 1974), help occurs at 90-100% regardless of group size
- Jonestown's relocation from San Francisco to the Guyanese jungle maximized uncertainty by placing members in a completely alien environment
- The Singapore bank run was triggered by uncertainty: passersby couldn't tell whether a crowd at a bus stop was waiting for a bus or fleeing a failing bank
- Racetrack bettors who know little about horses look to the tote board for social proof of which horse to back; high rollers exploit this by placing decoy bets

## Mechanisms

Uncertainty creates the conditions for social proof by:
1. **Reducing internal confidence** — When we don't know what to do, we are less able to rely on our own judgment
2. **Increasing external information-seeking** — The vacuum of internal certainty is filled by looking outward to others' behavior
3. **Lowering the threshold for imitation** — Actions we might question under normal conditions seem appropriate when modeled by others during periods of uncertainty
4. **Creating vulnerability to cascades** — Under uncertainty, if the first few people in a group act (or don't act), their behavior becomes disproportionately influential, potentially triggering [[pluralistic-ignorance]] or herd behavior

## Applications

- **Sales/Marketing:** Introduce social proof cues especially when the product or context is unfamiliar to the prospect (new technology, novel service category)
- **Emergency response:** Reduce uncertainty explicitly — state the nature of the emergency, specify the help needed, assign responsibility to a specific person
- **Leadership:** In times of organizational uncertainty (mergers, crises, major change), employees will be especially susceptible to social proof — leaders should be deliberate about what behavioral models are most visible
- **Defense:** Recognize that your susceptibility to social proof peaks when you are uncertain; in those moments, cross-check the crowd's behavior against objective data
