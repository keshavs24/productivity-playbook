# Autonomy Support in Education

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Autonomy support in education refers to the interpersonal orientation and set of specific teaching behaviors through which educators understand and relate to students from the students' own perspective, provide meaningful choices, offer rationale for requests and learning activities, minimize controlling language, and acknowledge students' feelings. It is the application of general [[autonomy-support]] principles to the specific domain of schooling, from elementary through post-secondary education.

Autonomy-supportive teaching contrasts with controlling teaching, in which educators pressure students to think, feel, or behave in specific ways, relying on external contingencies (grades, threats, rewards, directives) and relating from the teacher's perspective rather than the student's. Following Dewey (1938), autonomy-supportive teachers treat education as **development from within**, whereas controlling teachers treat education as **formation from without**.

Autonomy support is not permissiveness or lack of structure. Research consistently shows that the combination of high autonomy support and clear structure produces the best educational outcomes (Jang, Reeve, & Deci, 2010; Vansteenkiste, Sierens, et al., 2012).

## Key Findings

### Empirically Validated Teacher Behaviors

**Reeve & Jang (2006)** identified specific teacher behaviors empirically confirmed as autonomy-supportive or controlling through correlations with students' autonomous motivation (Table 14.1):

**Autonomy-Supportive Behaviors:**
1. Listening to students
2. Making time for students' independent work
3. Giving students an opportunity to talk
4. Acknowledging signs of improvement and mastery
5. Encouraging students' effort
6. Offering progress-enabling hints when students seem stuck
7. Being responsive to students' comments and questions
8. Acknowledging students' experiences and perspectives

**Controlling Behaviors:**
1. Monopolizing the learning materials
2. Providing too little time for independent problem-solving
3. Telling students answers without letting them formulate their own
4. Making demands and directives
5. Using controlling words ("should," "have to")
6. Using directed questions to control conversation flow

### Effects on Students

- **Deci, Schwartz, Sheinman, & Ryan (1981):** Students of autonomy-supportive teachers showed higher [[intrinsic-motivation]], perceived competence, and self-esteem within 2 months, persisting at 8 months.
- **Ryan & Grolnick (1986):** Students' perceptions of classroom climate as autonomy-supportive predicted intrinsic motivation, self-worth, and perceived cognitive competence.
- **Tsai, Kunter, Ludtke, Trautwein, & Ryan (2008):** Even lesson-to-lesson fluctuations in perceived teacher autonomy support covaried with students' interest, demonstrating real-time sensitivity to the motivational climate.
- **Black & Deci (2000):** College students in organic chemistry who perceived instructors as more autonomy-supportive showed increases in autonomous self-regulation, perceived competence, decreased anxiety, and earned higher grades even after controlling for ability.
- **Hardre & Reeve (2003):** In rural American high schools, perceived teacher autonomy support predicted autonomous motivation and perceived competence, which predicted school performance and intention to stay in school.

### Autonomy Support and Structure Are Complementary

- **Jang et al. (2010):** Autonomy support was a very strong predictor of student engagement; structure contributed additional variance, especially for behavioral engagement.
- **Vansteenkiste, Sierens, et al. (2012):** High autonomy support + clear structure predicted autonomous motivation, deep learning, time management, concentration, persistence, and low anxiety. Low autonomy support + vague structure predicted maladaptive strategies.

### Training Teachers to Be Autonomy-Supportive

- **Su & Reeve (2011):** Meta-analysis of 19 training studies. Large effect size (d=0.63) for intervention groups becoming more autonomy-supportive.
- **Reeve, Jang, Carrell, Barch, & Jeon (2004):** Trained teachers displayed significantly more autonomy-supportive behaviors; their students were more engaged.
- Training effects transfer beyond classrooms: **Hardre & Reeve (2009)** showed trained supervisors became more autonomy-supportive, and their supervisees showed more autonomous motivation.

### Special Populations

- **Students with disabilities:** Grolnick & Ryan (1990) and Deci et al. (1992) showed that autonomy support benefits students with LD, ED, and ASD just as it does other students. SDT argues these students have the same basic psychological needs.
- **Gifted students:** Vallerand et al. (1994) and Miserandino (1996) showed that even gifted students' engagement depends on autonomy and competence support, not competence alone.

### When Autonomy Support Is Undermined

Teachers become more controlling under:
- **Accountability pressures:** Deci et al. (1982) showed performance-standards pressure led teachers to talk five times as much, make more directives, and give solutions rather than allowing independent work.
- **High-stakes testing mandates:** Flink et al. (1990) showed teachers pressured to raise scores became more controlling; their students performed worse.
- **Student disengagement:** Pelletier et al. (2002) showed pressures from below (disengaged students) also negatively predicted teachers' autonomous motivation and autonomy support.
- **Burnout:** Bartholomew et al. (2014) showed more job pressures led to more teacher burnout through frustration of teachers' own basic psychological needs.

## Mechanisms

1. **Perspective taking:** Autonomy-supportive teachers begin by understanding and relating to the student's internal frame of reference, which naturally reveals when students need relational, competence, or autonomy supports.
2. **Need satisfaction cascade:** Autonomy support satisfies students' need for [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], which facilitates [[intrinsic-motivation]] and [[internalization-in-education|internalization]] of academic values.
3. **Autonomy-support co-occurrence:** When teachers are autonomy-supportive, they are typically also supportive of competence and relatedness needs, because perspective-taking reveals all needs simultaneously.
4. **Teacher well-being link:** Teachers' own autonomous motivation for teaching predicts their autonomy-supportive behavior (Roth et al., 2006), creating a motivational chain from administrators through teachers to students.

## Applications

- **Classroom practice:** Listen to students, provide choice, explain rationale, minimize controlling language, acknowledge improvement, offer hints rather than solutions
- **Curriculum design:** Frame activities in terms of intrinsic value and personal relevance; use criterion-referenced rather than normative assessments
- **School policy:** Provide teachers with professional autonomy; focus accountability on classroom process rather than narrow test outcomes
- **Professional development:** Train teachers in autonomy-supportive techniques (evidence shows large, reliable effects)
- **The umbrella effect:** Even in controlling policy environments, individual teachers can create need-supportive micro-environments in their own classrooms
