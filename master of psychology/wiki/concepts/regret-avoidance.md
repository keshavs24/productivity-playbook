# Regret Avoidance

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Regret avoidance is the tendency to make decisions that minimize the anticipated pain of regret -- sometimes at the cost of making objectively better choices. The intensity of regret is driven by counterfactual thinking: how easily one can imagine having done something differently. Near-misses generate more regret than distant misses, even when outcomes are identical.

## Key Findings

- **Missed flight problem:** Both scenarios result in the same outcome (missed flight, overnight stay). But seeing the plane depart 2 minutes late (scenario b) is far more upsetting than learning it left on time -- because the counterfactual "I almost made it" is vivid. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch7]])
- **Medvec, Madey, & Gilovich (1995):** Olympic silver medalists appear less happy than bronze medalists. Silver = upward counterfactual ("almost won gold"). Bronze = downward counterfactual ("almost didn't medal"). The counterfactual comparison determines emotional response, not the objective outcome.
- **Larrick (1993):** Regret avoidance distorts decisions in two ways:
  1. Choosing options that shield from feedback on foregone alternatives (avoiding information about what you could have had)
  2. When feedback is inevitable, choosing options likely to compare favorably to unchosen alternatives
- **Gilovich, Medvec, & Chen (1995):** Reluctance to switch doors in the Monty Hall problem partly reflects regret avoidance -- the sting of switching to an empty door (having "given up" the prize) feels worse than never having had it
- **Ku (2008):** Imagining future regret from escalation BEFORE entering an auction reduces subsequent [[escalation-of-commitment]]

## Mechanisms

Regret is driven by counterfactual thinking -- the ease of imagining an alternative outcome. Factors that increase counterfactual availability increase regret:
- **Proximity to success:** Near-misses generate stronger counterfactuals
- **Personal responsibility:** Actions of commission ("I switched and lost") generate more regret than omissions ("I stayed and lost")
- **Recency:** Recent alternatives are more vivid than distant ones

Anticipated regret then feeds back into decision-making, creating a bias toward inaction (omission bias) and toward maintaining the status quo.

## Applications

- **Investment decisions:** Regret avoidance contributes to the disposition effect (selling winners too early, holding losers) -- selling a winner that subsequently rises more is painful
- **Medical decisions:** Patients may avoid treatments where negative outcomes from action feel worse than identical outcomes from inaction
- **Negotiation:** The "should" self should consider how the two selves will compare various outcomes, negotiating over the relative value of better results vs. the pain of regret
