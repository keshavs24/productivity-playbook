# Political Efficacy

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Political efficacy involves people's beliefs that they can influence the political system through political action (Bandura, 1997, Ch.11, pp.482-497). It has two aspects: (1) the level of personal efficacy -- producing results by enlistment of effort and proficient use of capabilities -- and (2) the amenability of the social system to change by individual and collective influence (system responsiveness). These two aspects are neither fixed properties of the person or system nor independent of each other -- they operate in dynamic interplay. Perceived political efficacy should be distinguished from means efficacy (beliefs about whether a certain political tactic will work) and from political knowledge or level of participation.

## Key Findings

- Perceived political efficacy is more predictive of political action than is general life efficacy; particularized beliefs about specific political domains yield few significant relationships, while the widely used general measures vary from weakly predictive to nonpredictive (Gootnick, 1974; Fox & Schofield, 1989; Wollman & Stouder, 1991) ([[summary-3-11-se-ch11-collective-efficacy]])
- Locus of control is nonpredictive of political action; only perceived self-efficacy consistently predicts political participation
- Four types of political actors based on efficacy x system trust (Gamson, 1968; Wolfsfeld, 1986): (1) trustful efficacious -- conventional participation, (2) distrustful efficacious -- confrontational tactics, (3) trustful inefficacious -- political passivity, (4) distrustful inefficacious -- political alienation
- Politically efficacious individuals are more apt to take action regardless of whether they hold the system in high or low regard (Hawkins, Marando, & Taylor, 1971)
- From elementary to high school, children's beliefs in political efficacy increase but so does cynicism about government. African-American youths show lower sense of political efficacy and higher cynicism (Lyons, 1970; Rodgers, 1974), though these differences largely disappear at higher SES levels
- Collective efficacy in political action overcomes the free-rider problem ([[commons-dilemma]]): people participate not just for expected shared benefits but because of continuing costs of inaction (Kerr, 1996)
- Belief in collective efficacy sustains participation: social reforms are typically the product of an efficacious and highly committed minority (p.489)
- Self-evaluative incentives (self-respect from challenging unjust practices) serve as powerful motivators beyond material benefits
- The rapid toppling of Communist regimes in Eastern Europe illustrates a modeling enablement process -- widespread social discontent combined with modeled successes of collective action spread the same strategy rapidly across countries with different structural conditions (Braithwaite, 1994)

## Mechanisms

Political efficacy operates through the same mediating processes as other forms of efficacy, extended to the collective level:

1. **Cognitive:** People's beliefs about how much they can influence a given social system affect whether they see opportunities for change or regard the situation as futile
2. **Motivational:** Political efficacy determines goal level (scope of desired changes), persistence in the face of setbacks and opposition, and effort invested in political activities
3. **Affective:** Low political efficacy generates feelings of powerlessness, alienation, and cynicism; high efficacy sustains engagement even after political defeats
4. **Selection:** Political efficacy influences which forms of political action people choose (conventional vs. confrontational), whether they join organizations, and whether they view political processes as worth engaging with

Key dynamics specific to political efficacy:
- **Participatory dilemmas:** The larger the collectivity, the more insignificant any individual contribution appears, creating incentives for free-riding ([[commons-dilemma]]). This is overcome by continuing costs of inaction, self-evaluative incentives, and belief that a critical mass (not universal participation) is sufficient
- **Modeling enablement:** Televised modeling of successful collective action can rapidly diffuse strategies across populations and nations, accelerating social change
- **Reciprocal influence:** Successful political action raises personal efficacy but not necessarily system trust; failed action can leave personal efficacy intact while instilling even lower opinion of system responsiveness

## Applications

- **Civic engagement:** Build particularized political efficacy beliefs tied to specific domains and modes of influence rather than global measures of political competence
- **Community organizing:** Follow the Alinsky enablement model -- develop people's capabilities, forge common goals through self-interest, demonstrate that collective action produces tangible results (pp.500-504)
- **Media advocacy:** Use broadcast media to model efficacious collective action, build policy coalitions, and dramatize issues to move the public to action (Wallack et al., 1993)
- **Social change:** Employ the Sabido method of televised dramatic serials to model desired behavioral changes and build collective efficacy for social change (pp.505-510)
