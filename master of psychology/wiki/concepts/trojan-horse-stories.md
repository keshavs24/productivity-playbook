# Trojan Horse Stories

**Phase(s):** 6
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

Trojan horse stories are narratives that function as vessels for information, carrying lessons, morals, brand messages, and product information inside an engaging surface plot. Just as the original Trojan Horse carried Greek soldiers inside a wooden statue, stories carry embedded messages inside entertaining narratives. People think in terms of stories, not information — while they focus on the plot, information comes along for the ride. This is the sixth principle of [[berger]]'s [[stepps-framework]]: "Information travels under the guise of what seems like idle chatter."

The critical innovation in Berger's treatment is the distinction between **virality** (generating lots of attention) and **valuable virality** (generating lots of attention that actually benefits the brand, message, or cause). Many things go viral. Very few viral things carry their intended message through retelling. The Trojan horse concept addresses this specific problem: how to build stories that go viral *and* carry the message along for the ride.

## Why Stories Work: The Psychological Foundations

### 1. Narrative Transportation

Green and Brock's (2000) narrative transportation theory explains why stories are such effective message carriers. When people become absorbed in a story, they enter a state of **transportation** — a convergent mental process where attention, imagery, and feelings are focused on the narrative events. In this state:

- **Counterarguing decreases.** The analytical faculties that normally scrutinize claims are redirected toward following the plot. The message enters without resistance.
- **Attitudes shift.** Transported readers and listeners adopt attitudes consistent with the story's implications, even when those attitudes contradict their prior beliefs.
- **Peripheral details are accepted uncritically.** Information that would normally be questioned (brand claims, product attributes, behavioral recommendations) is accepted as part of the narrative fabric.

This is why stories are the most powerful persuasion format: they bypass the defensive cognitive processing that direct arguments trigger. A consumer told "Our product helps you lose weight" activates skepticism. A consumer who hears "Jared lost 245 pounds eating Subway sandwiches" is transported into a story and absorbs the product claims as narrative detail.

### 2. Proof by Analogy

Stories function as evidence. "That happened to my friend" or "I read about this guy who..." carries persuasive weight that advertising cannot match. This is because:

- Personal stories feel like first-hand evidence, even when they are second- or third-hand
- The specificity and detail of a good story create an illusion of evidentiary weight
- Stories about named individuals (Jared, not "a customer") are harder to dismiss than anonymous claims
- The emotional engagement with the protagonist creates identification, making the story's outcome feel relevant to the listener's own life

### 3. Reduced Counterarguing

When absorbed in a narrative, people lack the cognitive resources to simultaneously question the embedded message. They are too caught up in the drama of what happened next to evaluate whether the factual claims are accurate. This is the same mechanism that makes product placement in movies effective — viewers absorbed in the film do not consciously evaluate the brand.

### 4. Cultural Learning

Stories are how societies transmit values, norms, and practical knowledge. Fairy tales teach children about danger (Little Red Riding Hood), hubris (Icarus), and kindness (Cinderella). Religious parables convey moral principles through narrative. Urban legends carry warnings about social dangers. This is not incidental — it is the primary function of storytelling across human cultures, and it has been for at least 40,000 years of recorded narrative tradition.

### 5. Natural Sharing Format

People naturally tell stories rather than recite facts. Even when sharing product experiences, people default to narrative: "My son just turned eight so we were planning his first trip to Disney World, and I was looking for a camera that..." The story format is the natural packaging for information transmission between humans. This means that messages embedded in stories have a structural advantage in word-of-mouth transmission — they travel in the format people already use.

## The Valuable Virality Framework

### The Core Problem

Berger's central insight is that **virality and value are independent dimensions.** Something can be:

| | Low Virality | High Virality |
|---|---|---|
| **Low Value** | Boring, irrelevant content | Viral but worthless (Evian Roller Babies) |
| **High Value** | Good message, no reach | **Valuable virality** (Jared/Subway) |

The goal is the upper-right quadrant: content that both spreads widely AND carries the intended message. Most viral content fails this test — it generates views without generating value.

### The Critical Detail Test

Berger proposes a simple but powerful diagnostic:

> **Can someone retell the story and still make sense *without* mentioning the brand/message?**
> - If YES: the brand is **extraneous.** It will be stripped away in retelling. The virality is not valuable.
> - If NO: the brand is **integral.** It survives retelling because the story does not work without it. The virality is valuable.

This test is grounded in Allport and Postman's rumor transmission research, which established that **70% of story details are lost within 5-6 transmissions.** As stories pass from person to person, they undergo two processes:

1. **Leveling:** Extraneous details are dropped. The story gets shorter and simpler.
2. **Sharpening:** Central details are emphasized and sometimes exaggerated. The core of the story becomes more vivid.

For a brand or message to survive this natural erosion, it must be a **critical detail** — so central to the story that removing it collapses the narrative. If the brand is merely a footnote or a logo at the end, leveling will strip it away within a few retellings.

## Case Studies

### Successful Trojan Horses

#### Jared and Subway

The Jared story is Berger's paradigm case of a successful Trojan horse. The surface story: an obese college student named Jared Fogle lost 245 pounds by eating Subway sandwiches. The embedded message carries at least five pieces of information about Subway:

1. Subway offers healthy food options
2. You can lose weight eating at Subway
3. The food is good enough to eat every day for a year
4. The diet is sustainable (not a crash diet)
5. Specific products (6-inch turkey sub, Veggie Delite) are low-fat

People do not tell the Jared story to help Subway. They tell it because it is a remarkable weight-loss story. But **Subway is integral to the narrative** — you cannot tell the story of Jared's weight loss without mentioning what he ate, and what he ate was Subway. The brand survives leveling because it is load-bearing.

The commercial impact was enormous: the Jared campaign ran for over a decade, became one of the most recognized advertising narratives in American history, and drove measurable increases in Subway's market share.

#### Blendtec's "Will It Blend?"

Tom Dickson, the CEO of Blendtec, created a YouTube series blending unlikely objects: iPhones, golf balls, glow sticks, a rake handle, marbles. The surface story is pure spectacle — will this thing survive the blender? The embedded message: Blendtec blenders are incredibly powerful.

**You cannot describe the videos without mentioning the blender.** The blender IS the story. The brand is not a sponsor or a logo — it is the protagonist. This makes the viral content valuable: every share, every conversation, every "did you see the one where he blends an iPhone?" is a brand impression.

#### Panda Cheese Commercials

A series of Egyptian commercials featuring a person who declines Panda cheese, after which a giant panda appears and trashes the room in comedic rage. The surface story is absurdist comedy. The embedded message: you should always choose Panda cheese.

**The panda IS the brand.** You cannot describe these commercials without saying "Panda cheese" — the panda's appearance makes no sense without the cheese. The brand name is built into the punchline.

#### Barclay Prime's $100 Cheesesteak

Howard Wein, a chef launching a new high-end Philadelphia restaurant, created a $100 cheesesteak made with Kobe beef, lobster, and truffles. The story spread rapidly through Philadelphia: "Can you believe there's a $100 cheesesteak?" Every telling included the restaurant name because **Barclay Prime is where you get the cheesesteak.** The restaurant was integral to the story.

### Failed Trojan Horses: Viral But Worthless

#### Ron Bensimhon and GoldenPalace.com (Olympics Belly Flop)

At the 2004 Athens Olympics, Ron Bensimhon, wearing a tutu and the GoldenPalace.com logo, belly-flopped into the diving pool during a competition. The stunt made global news. Millions saw the footage.

**But nobody mentioned the casino.** The story was about a ridiculous man in a tutu disrupting the Olympics. GoldenPalace.com was irrelevant to the narrative — you can tell the story perfectly well as "some guy belly-flopped into the Olympic diving pool." The brand name was stripped from retellings because it was extraneous. Massive virality, zero value.

#### Evian's "Roller Babies" (2009)

Computer-animated babies roller-skating to hip-hop music. The video was one of the most-watched ads on YouTube at the time, with 50+ million views. Evian presumably spent millions on production and promotion.

**Sales dropped 25% that year.** Why? Roller-skating babies have nothing to do with water. You can describe and share the video without ever mentioning Evian. The brand was extraneous — a logo at the end that got skipped, ignored, or forgotten. The story (cute babies doing impossible things) carried no brand information. 50 million people saw it; almost none associated it with Evian.

#### The Olympics Streaker Pattern

Berger identifies a general pattern with event disruptions: streakers, stage-rushers, and pranksters generate massive attention, but the attention is almost never transferable to a brand or message. The spectacle IS the story, and any brand association is extraneous. This is why GoldenPalace.com's various stunts (buying naming rights to babies, tattooing the URL on boxers) generated press coverage but not business.

### The Dove "Evolution" Video: A Borderline Case

Dove's "Evolution" video (2006) showed a time-lapse of an ordinary woman being transformed into a billboard model through makeup, lighting, and Photoshop. The video cost approximately $100,000 to produce and generated 16+ million views in its first year.

Is Dove integral to the story? Partially. The story is about unrealistic beauty standards, which is a broader cultural conversation that Dove did not invent. But Dove positioned itself as the brand that revealed and challenged these standards — the "Campaign for Real Beauty" — which made the brand conceptually integral to the message. You could retell the story without mentioning Dove (failing the strict critical detail test), but Dove's association with the real beauty conversation was strong enough that the brand benefited.

**Result:** Double-digit sales growth. But Berger would argue this success came partly from the broader Campaign for Real Beauty (which created a genuine conceptual link between Dove and the message) rather than from the video alone.

## Designing Effective Trojan Horse Stories

Based on Berger's analysis and the case studies above, effective Trojan horse stories share several design principles:

### 1. Make the Brand the Plot Element, Not the Sponsor

The brand or message should be a character, a setting, or a causal element in the story — not a logo at the end or a mention at the beginning. Jared's story is about *eating at Subway.* Blendtec's videos are about *the blender.* The Panda commercials are about *the cheese.* In each case, removing the brand collapses the story.

### 2. Ensure the Story is Worth Telling Independent of the Brand

The story must have inherent interest — [[social-currency]], [[practical-value]], emotional resonance, or humor — that motivates sharing. People share stories for social reasons (to look knowledgeable, funny, or connected), not to help brands. The brand is a stowaway, not the destination.

### 3. Match the Story to the Message

The story's emotional tone and theme should match the brand's positioning. Dove's "Evolution" worked because the story about beauty standards matched Dove's "real beauty" positioning. A comedy video would not have worked for the same message. The story and the brand should feel like they belong together.

### 4. Test with the Retelling Test

Before launching, ask: "If someone tells this story to a friend, will they include the brand?" If the answer is no, redesign. If the answer is "only if they remember to add it at the end," the brand is extraneous.

### 5. Leverage High-Arousal Emotions

Stories that trigger [[high-arousal-emotions]] (awe, excitement, humor, anger, anxiety) are more likely to be shared than those triggering low-arousal emotions (sadness, contentment). The Trojan horse must travel to be valuable, and emotional arousal is the fuel for sharing.

## Applications

- **Brand storytelling:** Build narratives where the brand or product is a critical plot element — not just a sponsor or afterthought. Ask: "Does the story make sense without us?"
- **Content marketing:** Create stories people want to tell that naturally include information about your product or cause. The content must be independently valuable (entertaining, useful, surprising); the brand message rides along.
- **Campaign testing:** Ask "Can someone retell this story without mentioning our brand?" If yes, redesign. This single question can save millions in wasted viral campaigns.
- **Customer experience:** Design experiences that generate stories where the brand is integral — Barclay Prime's $100 cheesesteak, Please Don't Tell's phone booth entrance (a hidden bar entered through a phone booth in a hot dog shop — you cannot describe the experience without describing the venue).
- **Message embedding:** Make sure the information you want transmitted is a critical detail, not an extraneous one, so it survives the inevitable leveling of retelling. If the message is in the footnotes, it will be the first thing dropped.
- **Educational content:** The same principle applies to teaching. A lesson embedded in a vivid story (the Trojan Horse itself, Newton's apple, Archimedes' bathtub) survives transmission across centuries. Abstract principles explained abstractly are forgotten within days.
- **Personal influence:** When trying to change someone's mind, embed your argument in a story rather than stating it directly. The story bypasses defensive processing and carries the message into the listener's mind without triggering counterarguing.

## Open Questions
- How does the length and complexity of the story affect transmission fidelity and brand retention? Are simpler stories (fewer details) more robust to leveling, or does complexity create more opportunities for brand integration?
- In digital contexts (where stories can be shared verbatim via links rather than retold from memory), does the "critical detail" requirement still apply? When someone shares a YouTube link, the brand survives perfectly. But does the recipient *process* the brand information, or just the entertainment?
- How do visual stories (video) differ from verbal stories in terms of brand integration requirements? Video preserves brand visuals automatically; verbal retelling requires the brand to be semantically integral.
- Can the Trojan horse principle be applied to negative messages? Is it easier or harder to embed a warning, a criticism, or a counter-narrative inside a story compared to a positive brand message?
- What is the half-life of a Trojan horse story? The original Trojan Horse has survived 3,000 years, but most brand stories have a much shorter shelf life. What determines longevity?
