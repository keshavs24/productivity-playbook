# Inside View and Outside View

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The **inside view** and **outside view** are two contrasting modes of forecasting and judgment, first formally distinguished by Kahneman and Tversky (1979b) and developed extensively in Kahneman and Lovallo (1993).

- **Inside view:** The forecaster focuses on the **specific case at hand** -- its unique features, the details of the plan, the obstacles anticipated and how they might be overcome. In its extreme form, the inside view involves constructing a "representative scenario" that captures the essential elements of the projected future history. The inside view is anchored on plans and on the most available scenarios.

- **Outside view:** The forecaster **ignores the details of the current case** and instead identifies a **reference class** of similar past cases. The forecast is derived from the statistical distribution of outcomes in that reference class. The outside view is "essentially statistical and comparative" and involves no attempt to forecast the specific future history of the project.

This distinction is one of the most practically important concepts in the entire decision science curriculum. It is the conceptual foundation of what Buehler, Griffin, and Ross (1994) later called the **planning fallacy**, and it maps directly onto the broader contrast between [[narrow-framing]] (treating each problem as unique) and broad framing (treating it as an instance of a category).

## Key Findings

### The Curriculum Story (Kahneman's Paradigm Case)
In 1976, Kahneman was part of a team developing a curriculum for teaching judgment and decision making in Israeli high schools. After about a year of work, each team member estimated the time to completion: 18 to 30 months. Kahneman then asked a team member who was an expert in curriculum development to recall comparable projects. The expert reported:
- About 40% of comparable teams **never finished at all**
- Of those that did finish, none completed in less than **seven years**, and none took more than ten
- The expert rated their own team as "slightly below average in terms of resources and potential"

Despite this devastating outside-view forecast, the team continued with the project. It took approximately **eight years** to complete -- squarely within the reference-class distribution, and wildly outside the inside-view estimates.

### Overconfidence and the Inside/Outside View
- When people evaluate **individual items** (inside view), they display robust overconfidence: they are correct only ~80% of the time when saying they are "99% sure" ([[overconfidence-in-calibration]])
- When people estimate their **overall accuracy** after a session (outside view), their global estimates are accurate or slightly pessimistic (Gigerenzer et al. 1991, Griffin and Tversky 1992)
- This asymmetry -- overconfidence on individual judgments but not on aggregate self-assessment -- is a direct manifestation of the inside/outside view distinction

### Entrepreneurial Optimism
- Cooper et al. (1988): over 80% of new entrepreneurs estimated their own chance of success at 70% or better; fully one-third described their success as certain
- The same entrepreneurs estimated that businesses "like theirs" had a 59% chance of success (closer to the outside view, but still optimistic)
- The actual five-year survival rate for new firms is approximately 33% (Dun and Bradstreet 1967)
- This gap between self-assessed odds (inside view) and reference-class odds (outside view) is a hallmark of the phenomenon

### Capital Projects and Organizational Forecasting
- Rand Corporation study of pioneer process plants in energy: almost all projects exceeded initial cost estimates by over 20%; the norm was for actual costs to **more than double** first estimates (Merrow et al. 1981)
- PIMS data on start-up ventures: more than 80% of projects fell short of planned market share (Davis 1985)
- Mergers and acquisitions: bidding firms on average do not make significantly positive returns. Roll (1986) offers a "hubris hypothesis" citing optimistic estimates of synergy and overestimation of control over the merged entity

## Mechanisms

### Why the Inside View Dominates Intuition
1. **Narrative construction:** The natural way to think about a problem is to bring to bear everything one knows about it, with special attention to its unique features. Constructing a detailed scenario of the future feels like serious, responsible analysis
2. **Moral preference:** The inside view is valued as a "serious attempt to come to grips with the complexities of the unique case at hand"; the outside view is rejected for "relying on crude analogy from superficially similar instances." Physicians and lawyers often argue against applying statistical reasoning to individual cases
3. **Availability of case-specific information:** When rich inside-view information is available, it dominates attention. Statistical knowledge that the forecaster possesses may not be retrieved or used (Kahneman and Tversky 1973)
4. **Consensus validation:** Shared adoption of the inside view produces consensus, which feels validating but is not evidence of accuracy -- "a shared deficiency of reasoning will also yield consensus"

### Why the Inside View Produces Optimism
1. **Scenario anchoring:** Forecasts are anchored on the plan and its best-case execution. The scenario of flawless execution is more probable a priori than any specific failure scenario, but the aggregate probability of some failure occurring may be much higher
2. **Neglect of unknowns:** There are many different ways for things to go wrong. Even if each individual failure mode seems unlikely, their union can be highly probable. The inside view cannot enumerate all possible failure modes
3. **Motivational optimism:** Unrealistically positive self-evaluations, unrealistic optimism about future events, and illusion of control (Taylor and Brown 1988) all feed into inside-view forecasts
4. **Organizational incentives:** Adversarial competition for resources rewards optimistic forecasts. The winning project is disproportionately likely to have the largest optimistic error (winner's curse logic)

### Why the Outside View is Statistically Superior
1. **The future is not foreseeable in detail.** The ensemble of possible future histories is enormous; the probability of any particular scenario is negligible
2. **Base rates are informative.** Even a rough reference class provides a reality check that scenario-based forecasting cannot
3. **It avoids "the snares of scenario thinking" (Dawes 1988).** The most plausible scenario is not necessarily the most probable outcome
4. **It is conservative** -- it will fail to predict extraordinary events, but it will do well with common ones. Giving up the attempt to predict extraordinary events is not a great sacrifice, because the only way to score "hits" on extraordinary events is to predict large numbers of them that do not materialize

## Applications

### Reference-Class Forecasting (The Practical Tool)
The outside view can be operationalized as a simple procedure:
1. **Identify a reference class** of past cases similar to the one at hand
2. **Assess the distribution of outcomes** in that reference class (base rate of success, typical duration, typical cost overrun, etc.)
3. **Estimate where the current case falls** within that distribution, adjusting for any known differences
4. **Use the reference-class distribution as an anchor** and adjust from it, rather than building up from case-specific scenarios

This procedure was later formalized by Flyvbjerg (2006) as **Reference Class Forecasting** and adopted by institutions including the UK Treasury and the American Planning Association.

### Organizational Debiasing
- Reduce the frequency of performance evaluations to encourage broader framing ([[myopic-loss-aversion]])
- Encourage the "you win a few and you lose a few" attitude as an antidote to narrow framing
- Executives should encourage subordinates to accept more risk than feels individually comfortable, because aggregation across many subordinates' decisions reduces portfolio risk
- Consider maintaining separate "optimistic" and "realistic" forecasting tracks (Bromiley 1986)
- Assign a dedicated role for outside-view forecasting -- someone whose function is to achieve forecasts free of optimistic bias

### Personal Decision Making
- Before committing to a project timeline, ask: "What happened to other people/teams who attempted something like this?"
- Before starting a business, look at base-rate survival statistics for similar ventures in your industry
- Before estimating costs, look at the ratio of actual-to-planned spending in comparable past projects
- Use the outside view as a sanity check on inside-view estimates, even when the inside view feels more informative

## Open Questions

- How should the inside and outside views be optimally combined? The chapter suggests using both but does not provide a formal integration procedure. Later work by Flyvbjerg, Kahneman, and others has explored this, but the optimal weighting remains context-dependent.
- When is the inside view actually appropriate? For truly novel situations with no meaningful reference class, the outside view has limited applicability. The chapter does not fully address this edge case.
- Is there a way to capture the motivational benefits of optimism while maintaining forecast accuracy? The chapter flags this as a genuine dilemma for organizations, noting Taylor and Brown's (1988) finding that optimistic self-delusion is diagnostic of mental health.
