# Three Conversations Framework

**Phase(s):** 4, 5, 7
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Three Conversations Framework is the central model from [[difficult-conversations]] by [[stone-patton-heen]]. It holds that every difficult conversation, regardless of topic, actually consists of three simultaneous conversations running beneath the surface:

1. **The [[what-happened-conversation]]** — Disagreement about facts, intentions, and blame. Contains three predictable errors: the Truth Assumption (I'm right, you're wrong), the Intention Invention (I know why you did that), and the Blame Frame (it's your fault).
2. **The [[feelings-conversation]]** — The emotional dimension. What to do with our feelings and the other person's feelings. Engaging without addressing feelings is "an opera without music."
3. **The [[identity-conversation]]** — The internal conversation about what this situation means for our self-image. Am I competent? Am I a good person? Am I worthy of love?

The framework's power lies in its diagnostic simplicity: when a conversation feels difficult, you can sort the tangle into these three categories and address each one deliberately rather than reacting blindly.

## Key Findings

- **Universal structure.** The authors claim that after studying "hundreds of conversations of every kind," all difficult conversations share this three-part structure. The content varies infinitely; the architecture does not. ([[summary-4-15-dc-ch1-three-conversations]])
- **Predictable errors.** Each conversation contains characteristic mistakes that distort our thinking. In the What Happened? Conversation: truth assumption, intention invention, and blame frame. In the Feelings Conversation: suppression or explosion. In the Identity Conversation: all-or-nothing thinking.
- **The gap between spoken and unspoken is where the real action is.** The Jack-Michael example demonstrates that what people think and feel but don't say is far more significant than what they actually say in a difficult conversation. ([[summary-4-15-dc-ch1-three-conversations]])

## Mechanisms

The framework works because it makes the implicit explicit. Most people enter difficult conversations reacting to a jumble of undifferentiated thoughts and feelings. By sorting these into three categories, you gain the ability to:

1. **Identify which conversation is causing the most distress** — Often it's not the surface disagreement (What Happened?) but the identity threat or unexpressed emotions underneath.
2. **Address each conversation with the appropriate tool** — The What Happened? Conversation requires curiosity and the [[and-stance]]. The Feelings Conversation requires acknowledgment. The Identity Conversation requires building a more complex self-image.
3. **Shift from a "battle of messages" to a [[learning-conversation]]** — The overall move from delivering messages to mutual exploration.

## Applications

- **Preparation:** Before entering a difficult conversation, sort your thoughts into the three categories. What is your story about what happened? What are your feelings? What identity issues are at stake?
- **Diagnosis during conversation:** When a conversation goes off the rails, ask yourself which of the three conversations is the source of the problem.
- **Conflict resolution:** Useful in mediation, couples therapy, workplace disputes, and any context where people are talking past each other.
- **Negotiation:** Directly applicable to Phase 5 content — the What Happened? Conversation parallels the distinction between positions and interests in [[interest-based-negotiation]].
