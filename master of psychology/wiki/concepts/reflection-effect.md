# Reflection Effect

**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Confidence:** 0.6

## Definition

The reflection effect is the pattern where **risk preferences reverse when outcomes are reflected around zero** — switching from gains to losses. People are risk-averse for gains but risk-seeking for losses. The mirror image of preferences around the reference point.

## Key Findings

- In every problem pair tested, the majority preference for gains was the *opposite* of the majority preference for equivalent losses ([[summary-1-02-prospect-theory]])
- Risk aversion for gains: prefer (3,000 certain) over (4,000, .80) — 80%
- Risk seeking for losses: prefer (-4,000, .80) over (-3,000 certain) — 92%
- This pattern held for all four problem pairs in Table I of the paper
- The reflection effect demolishes the explanation that risk aversion comes from a universally concave utility function — the same person who is risk-averse for gains is risk-seeking for losses

## Mechanisms

The [[value-function]] is S-shaped: concave above the reference point (gains → diminishing sensitivity → risk aversion) and convex below (losses → diminishing sensitivity → risk seeking). Combined with the [[certainty-effect]], this produces: preference for certain gains (a sure 3,000 over a probable 4,000) and preference for risky losses (a probable -4,000 over a certain -3,000).

Intuitively: "I'll take the sure win" but "I'll gamble to avoid the certain loss."

## Applications

- **Negotiation:** A party who perceives themselves as losing will take riskier positions (gamble to avoid loss); a party who perceives themselves as gaining will accept safer outcomes. This predicts escalation of commitment in losing negotiations and premature acceptance in winning ones.
- **Legal settlement:** Defendants (facing losses) are more willing to go to trial (gamble); plaintiffs (seeking gains) are more willing to settle (take the sure thing)
- **Business strategy:** Companies in decline take riskier bets than companies growing — desperation-driven risk-seeking
- **Personal finance:** Holding losing stocks (gambling on recovery) while selling winners (locking in certain gains) — the disposition effect
