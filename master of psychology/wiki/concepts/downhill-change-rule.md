# Downhill Change Rule

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The downhill change rule is the primary constraint governing [[counterfactual-thinking]] and the [[simulation-heuristic]]. When mentally undoing past events, people overwhelmingly make **downhill changes** -- modifications that remove surprising, exceptional, or abnormal elements and restore the story to its default or expected state. Uphill changes (introducing unlikely new elements) are rare, and horizontal changes (substituting one arbitrary value for another equally likely one) are essentially nonexistent.

The metaphor is borrowed from cross-country skiing: exceptional states are peaks, normal states are valleys. Moving from an exception to the norm (downhill) is psychologically easy; moving from a norm to an exception (uphill) is psychologically difficult. This asymmetry is the fundamental constraint that lends realism to counterfactual fantasy.

## Key Findings

- In the "Mr. Jones" accident study, subjects given a version where Jones took an unusual route overwhelmingly undid the route; subjects given a version where he left at an unusual time undid the timing. Each group restored the exceptional element to its normal value ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch14-simulation-heuristic]])
- Not a single subject in any condition undid the accident by imagining Jones arriving at the intersection 2-3 seconds earlier -- the objectively most mutable variable. Continuous variables are not altered by arbitrary amount shifts
- The focus rule interacts with the downhill rule: modifications center on the focal actor's exceptional behaviors, not peripheral characters' actions
- The downhill preference produces planning optimism: each step of a plan seems plausible (downhill from the current state), so the overall plan seems achievable, even when cumulative failure probability is high

## Mechanisms

The downhill change rule reflects a deep cognitive principle: mental simulation operates within constraints that mirror the causal structure of everyday experience. Exceptions are "undoable" because they are recognized as departures from a norm. The norm itself is psychologically stable and resistant to modification -- it is the default, the expected, the background against which exceptions stand out.

This creates asymmetric psychological distance: the distance from an exception to the norm it violates is short, but the distance from the norm to the same exception is long. This asymmetry is not a logical necessity but a psychological one -- people could in principle imagine any modification, but in practice they are constrained to realistic, norm-restoring changes.

## Applications

- **Scenario planning:** Awareness of the downhill bias can improve scenario quality. Effective planners should deliberately construct "uphill" scenarios -- imagining how unexpected elements could derail a plan -- to counteract the natural tendency to produce only plausible, downhill narratives
- **Risk assessment:** Downhill bias means that risks arising from unusual conjunctions of events are underestimated, because each individual event seems eliminable by restoring normalcy
- **Blame attribution:** The downhill rule predicts that exceptional behaviors will be blamed more than routine behaviors for negative outcomes, even when both are equally causal
