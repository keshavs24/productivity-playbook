# Internalization

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Internalization is the active psychological process of taking in values, beliefs, or behavioral regulations from external sources and transforming them into one's own (Ryan, Connell, & Deci, 1985). It is the internal counterpart to the externally observable process of socialization. Within [[organismic-integration-theory]], internalization can proceed to varying degrees, resulting in regulatory styles that differ in their autonomy: [[external-regulation]], [[introjected-regulation]], [[identified-regulation]], and [[integrated-regulation]].

## Key Findings

- Internalization is a **natural organismic tendency** -- humans are inherently inclined to assimilate ambient social regulations, not merely comply with them
- The process is **conditional on need support** -- satisfaction of [[basic-psychological-needs]] (competence, relatedness, autonomy) is required for internalization to function optimally
- When need support is absent, internalization can still occur but tends to stall at introjection -- producing rigid, internally controlling, guilt-driven regulation
- Internalization is **not merely passive absorption** -- it involves active transformation, especially at the level of integration where values are brought into coherence with other self-structures
- The process has **evolutionary significance** -- it supports group cooperation, cultural transmission, and adaptive functioning at both individual and collective levels
- Internalization can operate on **negative content** -- prejudice, malevolence, violence can be internalized, though not with equal ease or integrity as prosocial values
- The concept of **self-compatibility checking** (Kazen, Baumann, & Kuhl, 2003) distinguishes introjection (where checking fails) from identification and integration (where it succeeds)

## Mechanisms

Three facilitating conditions (Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, & Leone, 1994):
1. **Meaningful rationale** -- explaining why the behavior matters
2. **Acknowledgment of feelings** -- recognizing the person's internal frame of reference
3. **Emphasis on choice** -- minimizing controlling language and directives

The three basic needs play differential roles:
- **Relatedness** is the primary energizer -- people internalize values of those they feel connected to
- **Competence** is required at all levels -- people gravitate toward what they can do effectively
- **Autonomy** is critical for moving beyond introjection -- without it, internalization stalls at guilty compliance

## Applications

- **Parenting:** Providing rationales, acknowledging children's perspectives, and offering appropriate choice facilitates deep internalization of family values. Controlling approaches produce at best introjection.
- **Healthcare:** Physicians who are autonomy-supportive help patients autonomously internalize health behaviors, leading to sustained change (Williams et al., 2002)
- **Education:** Teacher autonomy support predicts identified regulation and engagement; controlling teaching predicts introjection and disengagement
- **Organizational change:** New policies and values are more durably adopted when employees understand the rationale and feel volitional rather than coerced
