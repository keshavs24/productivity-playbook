# Cognitive Dissonance

**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Confidence:** 0.6

## Definition

Cognitive dissonance is the psychological discomfort experienced when a person holds two or more contradictory beliefs, values, or attitudes, or when their behavior conflicts with their beliefs. The theory, proposed by Leon Festinger (1957), posits that this discomfort motivates people to reduce the inconsistency — by changing their beliefs, changing their behavior, or rationalizing the contradiction.

## Key Findings

- **Festinger (1957)**: Established the foundational theory that inconsistency between cognitions produces psychological discomfort that motivates change.
- **Aronson & Mills (1959)**: Women who underwent a severe initiation to join a boring group rated the group highly — reducing dissonance between the effort and the outcome by inflating the outcome's value. This connects directly to [[effortful-commitment]].
- **Knox & Inkster (1968)**: Racetrack bettors become more confident after placing bets — reducing dissonance between the commitment and any doubt about the horse.
- Cialdini positions the desire for consistency (the engine of [[commitment-and-consistency]]) as the behavioral manifestation of dissonance avoidance. Inconsistency feels bad; we are motivated to eliminate it.

## Mechanisms

1. **Discomfort as motivator**: The aversive state of dissonance drives attitude or behavior change toward consistency.
2. **Post-decisional rationalization**: After making a choice, people elevate the chosen option and devalue the rejected alternative to reduce dissonance.
3. **Effort justification**: The more effort invested, the more we value the outcome — because devaluing the outcome would create intolerable dissonance with the effort spent.
4. **Insufficient justification**: When external justification for behavior is minimal, people adjust internal attitudes to justify the behavior (connecting to [[inner-choice]]).

## Applications

- Underpins all of [[cialdini]]'s [[commitment-and-consistency]] principle
- Explains why [[effortful-commitment]] (hazing, initiation) increases group loyalty
- Explains why [[inner-choice]] (minimal external pressure) produces lasting attitude change
- Explains post-purchase rationalization in consumer behavior

## Bazerman & Moore Extensions (Chapters 7-8)

Bazerman and Moore ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch7]], [[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch8]]) connect cognitive dissonance to managerial decision-making:

- **Self-justification as the core mechanism of [[escalation-of-commitment]]:** Staw (1976) demonstrated that negative feedback creates dissonance with the initial decision. Decision-makers resolve this by escalating -- investing more to "prove" the initial choice will succeed. This is dissonance reduction through behavior change (doubling down) rather than belief change (admitting error).
- **Self-serving reasoning as unconscious dissonance reduction:** People first determine their preferred outcome, then select fairness criteria to justify it (Messick & Sentis, 1983). This is a form of dissonance avoidance -- preventing the dissonance between self-interest and fairness from ever arising consciously.
- **Motivated perception (Balcetis & Dunning, 2006):** The filters operate pre-consciously -- people literally cannot perceive information that would create dissonance with their desired outcome.
- **The most effective lies are those we tell ourselves** (von Hippel & Trivers, 2011): Self-deception is an evolved dissonance-avoidance mechanism that allows people to behave self-servingly while genuinely believing they are being fair.

## Open Questions

- To what extent does dissonance require conscious awareness? Bazerman's evidence strongly suggests it often operates below awareness -- Balcetis & Dunning (2006) show pre-conscious perceptual filtering.
- How does cognitive dissonance interact with [[loss-aversion]]? Bazerman's Ch.8 confirms: escalation is driven by both -- dissonance motivates self-justification, loss-aversion motivates risk-seeking in the loss domain.
- Can organizational structures be designed to reduce dissonance-driven escalation without eliminating beneficial commitment?
