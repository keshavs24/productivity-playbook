# Basking in Reflected Glory (BIRGing)

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Basking in reflected glory (BIRGing) is the tendency to strengthen one's visible connections to winners and weaken one's visible connections to losers. People associate themselves with successful others — even when they had no role in that success — to enhance their own perceived prestige and public image. The reverse behavior, distancing from losers, is sometimes called CORFing (cutting off reflected failure).

BIRGing is an application of the broader [[conditioning-and-association]] principle: by connecting oneself to positive things, the positive feeling transfers to the self in the eyes of observers.

## Key Findings

- **Sports fan pronouns — Cialdini et al. (1976):** Arizona State students used "we" to describe wins ("We beat Houston, 17 to 14") and "they" to describe losses ("They lost to Missouri, 30 to 20"). One student combined both: "Arizona State lost it, 30 to 20" — then — "*They* threw away *our* chance for a national championship!"
- **Belgian elections — Boen et al. (2002):** Homeowners left political lawn signs up longer after their party won, basking in the positive connection
- **The mute veteran:** A World War II soldier who had not spoken for 30 years broke his silence to defend his hometown soccer team during a radio broadcast, then returned to silence forever. The mere connection of birthplace tied him to the team's fate
- **Isaac Asimov (1975):** "All things being equal, you root for your own sex, your own culture, your own locality... and what you want to prove is that *you* are better than the other person"
- **Kevin Costner's Oscar speech:** Mentioned "the people I went to high school with" — those classmates would bask in reflected glory by telling others about their connection to the star
- **Name-droppers and groupies:** People with poor self-concepts seek prestige through external associations rather than personal attainments
- **Andres Escobar:** The Colombian soccer player who accidentally scored an auto-goal in the 1994 World Cup was shot 12 times two weeks later — the extreme negative consequence of being associated with a loss

## Mechanisms

1. **The [[conditioning-and-association]] principle:** According to the association principle, if we are connected with success — even superficially (birthplace, alma mater, distant acquaintance) — our public prestige rises
2. **Self-enhancement:** BIRGing serves the fundamental need to feel good about oneself, particularly when personal accomplishments are lacking
3. **Impression management:** By manipulating the visibility of connections to winners and losers, people manage how they are perceived by others

**Who BIRGs most?** People with poor self-concepts. The behavior is most pronounced in individuals who lack a sense of personal accomplishment and seek prestige from association with others' achievements. Cialdini describes this as "the rather tragic view of accomplishment as deriving from outside the self."

## Applications

- **Marketing:** Brands associate themselves with winning teams, successful events, and popular celebrities
- **Politics:** Candidates link themselves to popular figures and cultural successes; supporters display signs longer after victories
- **Personal branding:** Name-dropping, alma mater references, and social media displays of association with successful people
- **Organizational behavior:** Employees may BIRG with successful departments or projects to enhance their internal reputation

## Open Questions

- Is BIRGing psychologically healthy (a normal self-enhancement strategy) or maladaptive (a sign of weak self-concept)? Cialdini implies the latter for extreme cases
- How does social media amplify BIRGing? Instagram posts at exclusive events, photos with celebrities, etc. may represent BIRGing at unprecedented scale
- Does CORFing (distancing from losers) carry social costs when the "losing" entity is a family member, close friend, or marginalized group?
