# Option Fixation

> **This page has been superseded by [[keeping-doors-open]].** Both pages described the same phenomenon from Ariely Ch.9. See [[keeping-doors-open]] for the consolidated content.

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Option fixation is the irrational compulsion to keep options and alternatives available, even at significant cost to overall outcomes. People experience the closing of any option as a loss (via [[loss-aversion]]) and will sacrifice time, money, and focus to prevent doors from shutting — regardless of whether those doors lead anywhere valuable. The phenomenon extends beyond keeping genuinely valuable options open to include maintaining worthless alternatives simply because their elimination feels like a loss.

## Key Findings

- In Ariely and Shin's "door game" experiments at MIT, participants earned about **15 percent less** when they frantically kept disappearing doors open compared to participants whose doors stayed open ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch9-doors-open]])
- Making the cost of keeping doors open explicit (3 cents per door click) did not change behavior
- Providing full information about each door's payoff did not change behavior
- Hundreds of practice trials did not change behavior
- Even when doors could be "resurrected" after closing (making the loss illusory and costless), participants still wasted clicks keeping them alive
- The effect extends to career decisions, romantic relationships, consumer purchases, and children's activity scheduling
- Ariely himself fell victim when choosing between MIT and Stanford, despite full knowledge of the bias

## Mechanisms

Option fixation operates through several reinforcing mechanisms:

1. **[[loss-aversion]]:** Closing a door is coded as a loss, which is weighted more heavily than the equivalent gain from focusing on the best option. The psychological pain of loss exceeds the pleasure of the gain from commitment.
2. **Salience of closing vs. invisible opportunity costs:** The shrinking door is vivid and attention-grabbing; the foregone clicks in the best room are invisible. This is a failure to properly weigh [[opportunity-cost]].
3. **Uncertainty aversion:** Even with known payoffs, people seem uncomfortable committing because they cannot be 100% certain they've found the best option. The option to switch provides psychological insurance.
4. **Cultural reinforcement:** Modern culture emphasizes keeping options open ("you can be anything"), creating social norms that reinforce the tendency (Erich Fromm's *Escape from Freedom*, 1941).

## Applications

- **Career planning:** Commit to a direction rather than trying to keep every career door open simultaneously. The cost of keeping multiple paths active (split coursework, divided energy) often exceeds the cost of choosing a suboptimal path and excelling at it.
- **Relationship decisions:** Continuing to invest in dying relationships "just in case" prevents full investment in more promising ones.
- **Consumer behavior:** Buying expandable/premium products "just in case" wastes money on optionality that is rarely exercised.
- **Organizational strategy:** Companies that try to maintain every product line, every market, every strategic option often underperform focused competitors.
- **Parenting:** Over-scheduling children in multiple activities prevents depth in any single pursuit.

## Open Questions
- How does option fixation interact with maximizing vs. satisficing tendencies? Are maximizers more prone to keeping doors open?
- Does the effect diminish with larger stakes, or does it amplify?
- Can the reframing from "closing a door" to "choosing to focus" reduce the behavior? (Ariely suggests this but provides no experimental evidence)
- What is the neural basis? Is the same circuitry involved as in other loss-aversion phenomena?
