# Undermining Effect

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The undermining effect refers to the well-documented phenomenon in which the introduction of tangible extrinsic rewards for an intrinsically motivating activity reduces the person's subsequent intrinsic motivation for that activity. First demonstrated by Deci (1971) and replicated across hundreds of studies, it is one of the most robust findings in motivation science and a central empirical foundation of [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]].

The effect occurs because tangible rewards, when expected, salient, and contingent on task behavior, shift the perceived locus of causality (PLOC) from internal ("I do this because I enjoy it") to external ("I do this for the reward"), thwarting the basic psychological [[autonomy-need|need for autonomy]].

## Key Findings

- **Deci (1971):** First published demonstration. Monetary rewards for Soma puzzles decreased subsequent free-choice persistence
- **Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett (1973):** "Good player award" for drawing undermined children's subsequent free-choice drawing (the [[overjustification-effect]])
- **Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis:** 128 experiments (1971-1996):
  - All tangible rewards combined: d=-0.34* (significant undermining)
  - Expected tangible rewards: d=-0.36*
  - Unexpected tangible rewards: d=0.01 (no effect)
  - Engagement-contingent: d=-0.40*
  - Completion-contingent: d=-0.44*
  - Performance-contingent: d=-0.28*
  - Competitively contingent: largest undermining of all
- **Longevity:** Effect sizes are virtually identical for immediate vs. delayed assessments -- not a transitory phenomenon
- **Age differences:** Stronger undermining for children than adults
- **Neuroscience:** Murayama et al. (2010) fMRI study showed contingent rewards decreased reward-network activation in response to task success after rewards were removed -- the brain's intrinsic valuation of the task diminished

### Boundary Conditions (When Undermining Does NOT Occur)
- Rewards that are **unexpected** (not anticipated before engagement)
- Rewards that are **task-noncontingent** (given for showing up, not for specific task behavior)
- Rewards that are **not salient** (Ross, 1975: hidden marshmallows)
- Rewards administered in an **autonomy-supportive** manner (Ryan, Mims, & Koestner, 1983)
- **Naturally occurring rewards** (endogenous outcomes of self-directed activity)
- **Small rewards** used as informational acknowledgment rather than behavioral control

## Mechanisms

1. **PLOC Shift:** Rewards create an external perceived locus of causality -- the activity becomes something done *for* the reward rather than for its own sake
2. **Autonomy Thwarting:** The controlling functional significance of contingent rewards thwarts the basic need for autonomy
3. **Instrumental Framing:** The activity is re-framed as a means to an end rather than an end in itself
4. **Neural Devaluation:** Contingent rewards decrease the brain's intrinsic reward-network response to task success (Murayama et al., 2010)

## Applications

- **Education:** Grades, prizes, and sticker charts for interesting learning activities risk undermining students' natural curiosity. Performance-contingent systems (honor rolls, class rankings) undermine motivation for the majority who do not win
- **Workplace:** Ch. 21 of Ryan & Deci (2017) provides extensive field evidence:
  - **Kuvaas, Dysvik, & Buch (2014):** In gas stations and financial organizations, extrinsic motivation (working for money) and intrinsic motivation (working for interest) were **negatively correlated** -- direct field-level undermining evidence
  - **Gubler, Larkin, & Pierce (2016) -- "Motivational spillovers":** An attendance award program at a laundry plant (a) was gamed by workers, (b) did not produce lasting change, (c) negatively affected previously excellent attenders, and (d) **crowded out motivation and performance on tasks not targeted by the program** -- the awards hurt the best employees most
  - **Kuvaas et al. (2016):** Performance-contingent pay in Norwegian insurance companies negatively predicted autonomous motivation and positively predicted controlled motivation; resulted in less effort and greater turnover intentions
  - **Cerasoli, Nicklin, & Ford (2014) meta-analysis:** With performance-contingent rewards, there was evidence of undermining. Intrinsic motivation was stronger for heuristic/quality tasks; extrinsic rewards stronger for algorithmic/quantity tasks
  - **Weibel, Rost, & Osterloh (2010) meta-analysis:** Pay-for-performance improved performance on simple tasks but **impaired performance on complex tasks**
  - **Frey (1993):** Undermining can "spill over" across work domains -- if rewards undermine autonomy in one domain, the extrinsic focus generalizes
  - See [[sdt-compensation-design]] for SDT's full framework on pay and [[summary-3-10-sdt-ch21-work]] for the complete review
- **Parenting:** Material rewards for reading, homework, or creative play can damage children's intrinsic interest
- **Public policy:** Introducing payments for blood donation (Titmuss, 1971), environmental behavior, or civic participation can crowd out intrinsic prosocial motivation
- **Game design:** Small, informational rewards (acknowledgment, progress markers) maintain intrinsic motivation; large, controlling, contingent rewards risk undermining it
