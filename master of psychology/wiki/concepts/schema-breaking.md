# Schema Breaking

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Schema breaking is the deliberate violation of an audience's expectations — their mental "guessing machines" — to grab attention and create openings for new understanding. The [[heath-brothers]] argue that surprise is not random or uncontrollable but can be engineered by identifying the audience's existing schemas (their predictions about what will happen) and then breaking those schemas in a way that serves the core message.

Critically, effective schema breaking requires *postdictability* — the twist must make sense in retrospect, even though it was not predicted. Surprise without insight (HENSION) produces frustration. Surprise with insight (PHRAUG) produces an "Oh!" moment that rewrites the schema for the better.

## Key Findings

- **Schemas are guessing machines.** People constantly predict what will happen based on accumulated patterns. Minivans deliver kids safely; car commercials show happy families; good customer service means fast processing. When these predictions fail, surprise jolts us to attention.
- **The surprise brow is a biological response.** Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen documented a cross-culturally consistent facial expression of surprise: raised eyebrows, widened eyes, dropped jaw, slack muscles. The brow widens the visual field — the body's "emergency override" to take in more information. Surprise interrupts ongoing activity, focuses attention, and prompts deeper processing.
- **Surprise without insight is gimmickry.** A Super Bowl ad with wolves attacking a marching band is memorable but not sticky — the surprise is utterly disconnected from the product. The Enclave minivan ad uses surprise (a horrific crash) that directly reinforces its core message (buckle up). Surprise must serve the core, not distract from it.
- **Postdictability separates good surprise from bad.** PHRAUG causes surprise then an "Oh!" (it sounds like FROG). HENSION causes surprise then frustration (it looks familiar but goes nowhere). The Sixth Sense is postdictable; "it was all a dream" is not. A satisfying surprise makes you think, "I should have seen that coming."
- **Three-step process for effective schema breaking:** (1) Identify the central message you need to communicate — find the core. (2) Figure out what is *counterintuitive* about the message — what are the unexpected implications? Why isn't it already happening naturally? (3) Communicate the message in a way that breaks the audience's guessing machines along the critical, counterintuitive dimension. Then, once their guessing machines have failed, help them *refine* their machines.

## Mechanisms

- **Attentional capture:** Surprise triggers an orienting response that interrupts current processing and redirects attention to the unexpected stimulus. This connects to the [[availability]] heuristic — surprising events become highly available in memory.
- **Schema repair:** The biological purpose of surprise is to flag schema failure so we can update our mental models. Effective communicators exploit this by breaking a schema and then offering a better one.
- **Deeper processing:** Unexpected information receives more cognitive processing than expected information, leading to better encoding and recall. This explains why unexpected ideas are inherently stickier.
- **Enduring attention via mystery:** Schema violation is a onetime transaction (first-level unexpectedness). Mystery extends the journey (second-level unexpectedness) — the audience wants to solve the puzzle but isn't sure how. See [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]].

## Applications

- **Nordstrom's "Nordie" stories:** Stories about employees wrapping Macy's gifts and refunding tire chains break new employees' schemas of "good service" and replace them with Nordstrom's extreme-service schema
- **Nora Ephron's journalism teacher:** "The lead is: There will be no school next Thursday" — broke students' schema of journalism as fact-regurgitation and replaced it with journalism as meaning-finding
- **Southwest Airlines:** "THE low-fare airline" sounds like common sense until Kelleher refuses chicken salad that passengers want — the uncommon-sense implications break the schema of "please the customer"
- **Hoover Adams's Daily Record:** "Names, names, and names" sounds conventional for a local newspaper until Adams says he'd print the phone book if he could — breaking the schema of "cover local news" and replacing it with "be obsessively local"

## Open Questions

- How does schema breaking relate to the [[contrast-principle]] from Cialdini? Both involve violated expectations — but the contrast principle is about sequential comparison, while schema breaking is about violating predicted patterns. Are they the same mechanism at different levels?
- What happens when schema breaking is overused? Does repeated surprise create a new schema (of constant surprise) that dulls the effect? The Heaths note this risk with dot-com Super Bowl ads.
