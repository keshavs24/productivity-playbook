# Autonomy (Basic Psychological Need)

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Autonomy is one of the three basic psychological needs posited by [[self-determination-theory]] (alongside [[competence-need|competence]] and relatedness). It refers to the experience of volition and willingness -- the sense that one's actions emanate from oneself and are self-endorsed, rather than being controlled by external or internal pressures.

Autonomy is **not** independence, selfishness, or detachment from others. One can be autonomously dependent (willingly relying on others) or heteronomously independent (feeling forced to go it alone). The essence of autonomy is the perceived locus of causality (PLOC): behavior with an internal PLOC (I-PLOC) is experienced as autonomous; behavior with an external PLOC (E-PLOC) is experienced as controlled.

Within [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]], autonomy satisfaction is a necessary condition for [[intrinsic-motivation]]. Events that thwart autonomy (shifting PLOC from internal to external) undermine intrinsic motivation; events that support autonomy enhance it.

## Key Findings

- **Deci (1971); Lepper et al. (1973):** Tangible contingent rewards shift PLOC from internal to external, thwarting autonomy and undermining intrinsic motivation -- the [[undermining-effect]]
- **Houlfort et al. (2002):** Contingent rewards directly undermine participants' sense of autonomy
- **Ryan (1982):** Positive feedback delivered controllingly undermined intrinsic motivation despite enhancing competence -- demonstrating that autonomy and competence are independently necessary
- **Deci, Schwartz, Sheinman, & Ryan (1981):** [[autonomy-support|Autonomy-supportive]] teachers predicted higher student intrinsic motivation, perceived competence, and self-esteem
- **Zuckerman et al. (1978); Patall et al. (2008):** Providing choice enhances autonomy and intrinsic motivation
- **Murayama et al. (2015):** Choice improved performance and made participants resilient to negative feedback (no vmPFC drop)
- **Li et al. (1998); Jang, Reeve, Ryan, & Kim (2009):** Perceived autonomy and perceived competence both predict intrinsic motivation in structural equation models
- **Ryan, Rigby, & Przybylski (2006):** Autonomy and competence ratings predict video game intrinsic motivation and preferences

### Autonomy Thwarting vs. Low Support
- Autonomy thwarting (actively pressuring, controlling, coercing) has worse outcomes than merely low autonomy support
- Bartholomew et al. (2011): Thwarting predicted need frustration, negative affect, and stress biomarkers independently of low support

## Mechanisms

1. **PLOC:** Autonomy is operationalized through the internal vs. external perceived locus of causality. Internal PLOC = "I choose to do this." External PLOC = "I do this because I'm made to"
2. **Volition:** The phenomenological experience of willingness and endorsement of one's actions
3. **Self-regulation:** Autonomous functioning involves self-organized, flexible, interested engagement. Controlled functioning involves rigid, pressured, narrowed engagement
4. **Need for autonomy is universal:** SDT posits that all humans require autonomy satisfaction for optimal functioning, though the vehicles for satisfying autonomy vary across cultures

## Applications

- **Education:** Support student autonomy through meaningful choice, rationale provision, acknowledgment of perspectives, and minimization of controlling language
- **Workplace:** Create conditions for autonomy by allowing flexibility in how tasks are accomplished, explaining the reasons behind directives, and avoiding micromanagement
- **Parenting:** Provide structure and limits while supporting children's sense of volition -- the key is how limits are communicated, not whether they exist
- **Health behavior:** Autonomous motivation for health behaviors (diet, exercise, medication adherence) predicts sustained behavior change better than controlled motivation
- **Design:** Products and systems should support users' sense of choice and self-direction rather than funneling them through controlling pathways

## BPNT Findings on Autonomy (Ch. 10)

- **Autonomy support facilitates all three needs** (BPNT Proposition II): The reason for focusing on autonomy support as a predictor of all three needs is not that the need for autonomy is more important, but that autonomy support allows individuals to actively seek out and find satisfactions for all their needs -- to gravitate toward, make relevant choices, and employ optimizing strategies.
- **Vitality and autonomy:** Autonomous activity enhances subjective vitality; controlling regulation depletes it (BPNT Proposition VI). Moller, Deci, & Ryan (2006) showed that true choice does not produce ego depletion, while compelled choice does.
- **Mindfulness facilitates autonomy:** Brown & Ryan (2003) -- trait and state mindfulness predicted more autonomous activity in daily life (BPNT Proposition VIII). Mindfulness provides the "space" for reflective, autonomous regulation.
- **Autonomy and relatedness are positively correlated**, not opposed as some cultural relativists claim (Markus et al., 1996). SDT distinguishes autonomy from independence/separateness.
- **Weekend effect:** Workers experience greater well-being on weekends, mediated by enhanced autonomy and relatedness satisfaction (Ryan, Bernstein, & Brown, 2010)
- **Nature exposure** promotes feelings of autonomy and intrinsic goal valuation (Weinstein, Przybylski, & Ryan, 2009)

### Autonomy in OIT and COT (Ch. 8-9)

- **Autonomy is critical for moving internalization beyond introjection:** Within [[organismic-integration-theory]], [[relatedness-need|relatedness]] energizes initial uptake of values, but without autonomy support, internalization stalls at [[introjected-regulation]] -- producing guilt-driven compliance rather than genuine endorsement. Autonomy support is what enables the transition to [[identified-regulation]] and [[integrated-regulation]].
- **Autonomy orientation as individual difference:** [[causality-orientations-theory]] describes the [[autonomy-orientation]] as a personality-level propensity to orient toward informational features of environments and experience choice. High autonomy orientation buffers against reward undermining (Hagger & Chatzisarantis, 2011) and predicts personality integration (Koestner et al., 1992).
- **Reflective vs. reactive autonomy:** Koestner and Losier (1996) distinguished reflective autonomy (volition, SDT sense) from reactive autonomy (independence, defiance). Reflective autonomy is compatible with positive relationships and following credible advice; reactive autonomy is not.

## Open Questions
- How does autonomy satisfaction differ across collectivist vs. individualist cultures? SDT claims it is universal but expressed differently. Chen et al. (2015) found equivalent effects across Belgium, China, U.S., and Peru, with no moderation by self-reported need strength.
- What is the relationship between autonomy and self-control? SDT distinguishes them: self-control can be controlling (introjected), whereas autonomy involves wholehearted engagement. Autonomous self-regulation does not produce ego depletion (Moller et al., 2006).
- Can autonomy satisfaction compensate for competence frustration, or vice versa? Balance across needs matters (Sheldon & Niemiec, 2006), but all three are independently important.
- How does the facilitative role of autonomy in seeking other need satisfactions work mechanistically? Autonomy appears to be "first among equals" in enabling need satisfaction, but SDT insists all three are equally essential.
