# Causal vs. Incidental Base Rates

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

A **causal base rate** is one that suggests the existence of a causal factor explaining why any particular instance is more likely to yield one outcome rather than another. An **incidental base rate** is one that does not lead to such a causal inference -- it is a mere statistical artifact of sampling or population composition.

This distinction, introduced by Tversky and Kahneman (1982), is the key to understanding when base rates are used versus neglected. Causal base rates are integrated into judgments; incidental base rates are largely ignored.

## Key Findings

- **Ajzen (1977) exam study:** "75% of students failed the exam" (causal -- implies the exam was difficult) produced a .34 shift in judged probability of success; "75% of the sample had failed" (incidental -- the interviewer selected mostly failing students) produced only a .12 shift. ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch10-evidential-base-rates]])
- **The cab problem (Kahneman & Tversky, 1972a; Bar-Hillel, 1980a; Tversky & Kahneman, 1980):** When 85% of cabs are Green (incidental), the base rate is ignored (median response .80, matching witness reliability). When 85% of *accidents* involve Green cabs (causal -- implies reckless drivers), the base rate is integrated (median response .60). The posterior probability is .41 in both cases.
- **Ajzen (1977) course preference study:** Enrollment proportions (.70/.30) served as a causal base rate for course attractiveness -- strong effect (mean judged probability .65 vs. .36). The same proportions presented as an interviewer's selection -- no significant effect.
- The incidental base rate is not just "weaker" -- it is often completely ignored, whereas the causal base rate exerts substantial influence.

## Mechanisms

Causal base rates are utilized because they are integrated into the person's causal model of the situation. When told 75% of students failed, the judge infers the exam was hard -- a cause that applies to the target student as well. This causal interpretation makes the base rate feel relevant and applicable to the individual case.

Incidental base rates lack this causal link. The fact that a psychologist's sample contained 75% failures says nothing about *why* a particular student might fail. There is no mechanism connecting the sampling procedure to the individual's outcome. Without a causal schema to carry the base rate, it feels irrelevant and is discarded.

This connects to the broader finding that [[representativeness]] operates through similarity and causal reasoning: information that fits into a causal story receives weight; information that is merely statistical does not. See also [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]] for the related principle that abstract statistical data lacks psychological impact.

## Applications

- **Medical communication:** Presenting risk information in causal terms ("this drug weakens the immune system, which is why 30% of patients develop infections") will be more impactful than incidental framing ("in clinical trials, 30% of patients happened to develop infections").
- **Policy:** Framing social statistics causally rather than incidentally increases their persuasive force and their use in individual judgment.
- **Legal reasoning:** Base rates framed causally (accident rates due to reckless driving) influence juries more than incidental statistics (proportion of cabs in the city).
- **Risk assessment:** Organizational risk frameworks should present base-rate data with causal explanations to ensure decision-makers incorporate them.
