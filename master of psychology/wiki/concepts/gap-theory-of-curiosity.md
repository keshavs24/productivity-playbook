# Gap Theory of Curiosity

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The gap theory of curiosity, proposed by George Loewenstein (Carnegie Mellon University, 1994), holds that curiosity arises when we perceive a gap between what we know and what we want to know. This gap functions like an itch — it creates a form of psychological pain that motivates us to seek information to close it. Curiosity is not a trait or a stable preference but a *situational* response to perceived information gaps.

The [[heath-brothers]] use the gap theory as the foundation for the "Unexpected" principle's second function: not just getting attention (through surprise/[[schema-breaking]]) but *keeping* attention through sustained curiosity.

## Key Findings

- **Gaps cause pain.** Loewenstein argues that knowledge gaps are aversive — we sit through bad movies because it is too painful not to know how they end. We don't put mystery novels down before the last chapter. We don't turn off the television before the final inning of a close ball game.
- **We must open gaps before closing them.** Our natural tendency when communicating is to tell people the facts. But Loewenstein argues we must first highlight *what people don't know* — make them feel the gap — before providing the answer. "The trick to convincing people that they need our message is to first highlight some specific knowledge that they're missing."
- **More knowledge creates more curiosity, not less.** Counterintuitively, as people gain knowledge they become *more* curious, not less, because they become increasingly aware of what they still don't know. Someone who knows 47 of 50 state capitals focuses on the 3 they're missing, not the 47 they know.
- **Gaps require a foundation of knowledge.** You can't create a gap if people have no knowledge at all — they have an abyss, not a gap. Sometimes you must provide context first (Roone Arledge showing campus life before the football game) to *prime* the gap.
- **Overconfidence blocks gap formation.** People who believe they already know everything won't feel knowledge gaps. Strategies for combating overconfidence include: making people commit to predictions before revealing outcomes (Nora Ephron's journalism teacher, Eric Mazur's concept testing), and exposing people to disagreement (Lowry & Johnson found disagreement increased curiosity more than consensus).
- **Mysteries sustain interest better than schema violations.** A schema violation is a onetime transaction — boom, your expectations are broken. A mystery creates an extended journey — you want to solve it but aren't sure how. This is "second-level unexpectedness" — from fleeting surprise to enduring interest.

## Mechanisms

The gap theory works through several cognitive pathways:

1. **Aversive arousal:** The gap creates a state of mild discomfort that motivates information-seeking behavior — similar to hunger or thirst but for knowledge
2. **Need for closure:** Once a gap is opened, the mind seeks resolution — this is why cliffhangers, teasers, and mysteries are so effective. See [[commitment-and-consistency]].
3. **Reference-dependent curiosity:** The gap is felt relative to what one already knows. More context makes the gap more salient and more painful. This parallels [[reference-dependence]] in prospect theory — outcomes are evaluated relative to a reference point.

## Applications

- **Teaching:** Structure lessons around mysteries rather than fact-dumps. Cialdini found that the best science writing opens with a puzzle, and he used mysteries in his own classroom with dramatic results (students protested when the bell rang before the mystery was resolved).
- **Presentations:** Start with a question or puzzle the audience cares about answering, not with the answer. The fundraising clinic example: "This year we set out to answer a question: Why do people under thirty-five provide only 10 percent of our donations?" is stickier than a summary of facts.
- **News and media:** Local news teasers exploit the gap theory sensationally: "There's a new drug sweeping the teenage community — and it may be in your own medicine cabinet!" A little teaser goes a long way.
- **Vision-setting:** Sony's "pocketable radio" and JFK's "man on the moon" both created massive, motivating knowledge gaps — audacious enough to create surprise but not so impossible as to create paralysis. The gaps sustained motivation across years and thousands of people.
- **Priming the gap:** Roone Arledge pioneered providing backstory before NCAA football games — showing campuses, fans, rivalries, emotions — to convert an abyss of ignorance into a gap of curiosity. This became the template for *Monday Night Football* and *Wide World of Sports*.

## Open Questions

- How does gap theory interact with [[intrinsic-motivation]]? Curiosity is often considered an intrinsically motivated behavior (SDT would classify it under the [[competence-need]]). Does opening a gap *create* intrinsic motivation or merely redirect it?
- Is there an optimal gap size? Too small a gap (trivially answered) creates boredom; too large (impossibly complex) creates paralysis. Loewenstein's theory predicts an inverted-U, but the Heaths don't quantify this.
- How does individual difference in need for cognition (Cacioppo & Petty) moderate susceptibility to knowledge gaps?
