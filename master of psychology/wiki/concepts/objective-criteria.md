# Objective Criteria

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

"Insist on using objective criteria" is the fourth principle of [[principled-negotiation]]. It means basing the outcome of a negotiation on some fair standard or fair procedure independent of the naked will of either side. Rather than deciding through a contest of stubbornness, negotiators identify and apply legitimate external benchmarks — market value, precedent, scientific judgment, professional standards, efficiency, reciprocity, or what a court would decide.

## Key Findings

- **Deciding on the basis of will is costly.** Without objective criteria, negotiation becomes a contest of who can be more stubborn — inefficient, damaging to relationships, and unlikely to produce wise agreements. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch5-criteria]])
- **Two types of objective criteria exist:** ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch5-criteria]])
  - **Fair standards:** Market value, precedent, scientific judgment, professional standards, efficiency, costs, moral standards, equal treatment, tradition, reciprocity, what a court would decide
  - **Fair procedures:** "One cuts, the other chooses," taking turns, drawing lots, last-best-offer arbitration, mediation, third-party expert opinion
- **The reciprocity test:** Objective criteria should apply at least in theory to both sides. A real estate agent selling you a house using a standard form contract — you would be wise to ask if it is the same standard form they use when *they* buy a house. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch5-criteria]])
- **Three principles for negotiating with objective criteria:** (1) Frame each issue as a joint search for fair standards, (2) Reason and be open to reason, (3) Never yield to pressure, only to principle. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch5-criteria]])
- **The MIT model at the Law of the Sea Conference** demonstrated how an objective economic model can break deadlocks by providing a standard both sides accept as legitimate. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch5-criteria]])
- **The insurance case study ("It's Company Policy")** demonstrated principled negotiation in action: Tom used blue book values, comparable car prices, and mileage adjustments to get $8,024 instead of the initial $3,300 offer — without threatening, without digging into a position, simply by insisting on objective criteria. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch5-criteria]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Countering [[anchoring-and-adjustment]]:** When the other side opens with an extreme number, insisting on the standard behind it ("How did you arrive at that figure?") prevents the anchor from dominating the negotiation.
2. **Reducing the number of commitments required:** In positional bargaining, each side must make and then unmake commitments as they move toward agreement. Objective criteria eliminate this dance — both sides can move toward the standard together.
3. **Protecting against pressure:** "Never yield to pressure, only to principle" is a powerful commitment device. It is easier to resist an arbitrary concession than it is for the other side to resist advancing some objective standard.
4. **Preserving relationships:** Discussing objective criteria is less threatening than a battle of wills. Both sides can defer to the standard rather than having to "give in" to the other side. This protects face and preserves the relationship.

## Applications

- **Car insurance claims:** Use blue book values, comparable listings, depreciation schedules
- **Salary negotiations:** Use market surveys, industry standards, cost-of-living data, pay equity analyses
- **Contract disputes:** Use precedent, industry custom, arbitration procedures
- **Real estate:** Use comparable sales, appraisals, price-per-square-foot standards
- **International disputes:** Use international law, precedent from similar agreements, expert economic models

## Open Questions
- What happens when both sides advance different but equally legitimate objective criteria that produce different results? Fisher and Ury say you can split the difference between the two results or resort to fair procedures — but this can itself become a positional dispute about which standard to use.
- Agreement on the "best" standard is not necessary — the process of discussing standards still produces better results than a pure contest of will.
