# Attribution Theory

**Phase(s):** 1, 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Attribution theory addresses how people make causal explanations for events, particularly their own successes and failures. In the context of motivation, Weiner's (1985) attributional framework examines how retrospective judgments of the causes of one's performances have motivational effects. The analytical procedure is usually confined to four categories of information: ability, effort, task difficulty, and luck. Ability is treated as a stable internal cause; effort as an unstable internal cause; task difficulty as a stable external factor; and luck as an unstable external factor.

## Key Findings

- People who credit their successes to personal capabilities and their failures to insufficient effort will undertake difficult tasks and persist in the face of failure; those who ascribe failures to deficiencies in ability display low strivings and give up readily (Weiner, 1985)
- Causal attributions can serve different purposes: as genuine belief modifiers that alter future actions, or as self-serving excuses that do not affect future performances (Covington & Omelich, 1979). Connects to [[self-serving-attribution]].
- **Self-efficacy mediates the effect of causal attributions on performance almost entirely** (Relich, Debus, & Walker, 1986; Schunk & Gunn, 1986; Schunk & Rice, 1986) -- this is a critical finding from Bandura's (1997) Ch.4
- The types of factors singled out by attribution theory serve as conveyors of efficacy-relevant information that influence performance mainly by altering people's beliefs in their efficacy
- Self-efficacious individuals view attainments as personally controllable and ascribe failures to insufficient effort or situational impediments; those with low efficacy view failures as stemming from lack of ability -- this pattern replicates across academic, interpersonal, physical, and health domains
- Attribution retraining alone has limited success in producing enduring behavioral change; sociocognitive programs fostering personal enablement produce uniformly beneficial changes (Bandura, 1986a)
- Effort attributions have variable effects on efficacy beliefs depending on conceptions of ability: high effort enhances efficacy for those who view ability as acquirable but undermines it for those who view ability as inherent (Schunk & Cox, 1986)
- In a sociocognitive analysis, ability, effort, task difficulty, and luck are not discrete causal categories but conveyors of efficacy information -- and additional factors beyond these four (contextual conditions, physical and emotional states, temporal patterning, social comparisons) also enter into efficacy judgments

## Mechanisms

Attribution theory proposes a causal chain: Performance outcome --> Causal attribution --> Emotional response + Future expectations --> Future behavior.

Bandura's (1997) integration argues that this chain is incomplete -- the causal attributions affect behavior primarily through their impact on self-efficacy beliefs. When efficacy beliefs are controlled, the independent effect of attributions on performance tends to be small and equivocal.

## Applications

- Framing failures as due to controllable, changeable factors (insufficient effort, poor strategy) rather than uncontrollable factors (lack of ability) -- but this must be accompanied by actual skill-building, not just relabeling
- Attribution retraining is most effective when embedded within a comprehensive sociocognitive program that builds actual competencies and provides mastery experiences ([[mastery-experience]])
- Be cautious with effort attributions -- repeatedly telling people their success is due to hard work can inadvertently convey that they have limited talent
