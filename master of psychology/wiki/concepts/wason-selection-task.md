# Wason Selection Task

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Wason selection task is a classic reasoning experiment in which participants are shown four cards (e.g., E, K, 4, 7) and told each has a letter on one side and a number on the other. Given a rule like "If a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other," participants must select which cards to turn over to verify the rule. The logically correct answer (E and 7) is chosen by only about 10% of participants. The task has become a benchmark for understanding the gap between formal logic and everyday reasoning.

## Key Findings

- About 90% correctly select E (modus ponens), but only 25% select 7 (modus tollens); 60% incorrectly select 4 (affirming the consequent); 15% select K (irrelevant) ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch10]])
- Even PhDs in mathematics and physics perform poorly -- Wason presented it at IBM Research Center to an audience of PhDs who "harassed" him about the problem when they got it wrong
- Performance dramatically improves when the conditional is framed as a social rule to be enforced: 74% correct with drinking-age content (Griggs & Cox, 1982), 80% with cheating-detection framing (Gigerenzer & Hug, 1992)
- The [[permission-schema]] (Cheng & Holyoak, 1985) explains the improvement: people have evolved mechanisms for checking whether social rules have been violated
- Oaksford and Chater (1994) offered an alternative explanation: people treat the abstract task probabilistically, assuming the referenced properties are rare, which makes their card selections informationally optimal

## Mechanisms

The task reveals a fundamental conflict between logical reasoning (which requires checking for falsification) and natural human reasoning strategies (which favor confirmation). When the task triggers social contract / cheater-detection mechanisms, these naturally implement modus tollens (checking whether someone took a benefit without meeting the obligation). The abstract version fails to activate these mechanisms, leaving people to rely on [[conditional-reasoning]] strategies that favor modus ponens over modus tollens.

## Applications

- **Detecting rule violations:** Humans are naturally better at finding rule-breakers than at abstract logical verification -- design compliance systems that leverage this
- **Critical thinking pedagogy:** Demonstrates that logic training alone is insufficient (only 3% improvement after a semester of logic); training should emphasize real-world deontic reasoning and falsification strategies
- **Scientific methodology:** The Wason task shows why falsification must be an explicit institutional norm rather than relying on individuals' natural tendencies

## Open Questions
- Do evolutionary social-contract explanations (Cosmides) or cultural permission-schema explanations (Cheng & Holyoak) better account for the content effect? Evidence supports both.
- Is the Oaksford & Chater rarity-assumption explanation truly general, given that the abstract task uses letters and numbers (not rare)?
