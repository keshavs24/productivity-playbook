# Phantom Alternative

**Phase(s):** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

A phantom alternative is an unavailable option introduced into a choice set that systematically shifts preferences for the available options. Unlike the [[decoy-effect]] (where an inferior option makes a target look better), a phantom is an *attractive but unavailable* option that (1) makes available options look less attractive by contrast, and (2) changes the decision criteria — the attribute on which the phantom excels becomes the most important factor, shifting choice toward the available option strongest on that attribute.

## Key Findings

### Pratkanis and Farquhar's Experiments

College students chose among brands of products. When a very attractive but unavailable alternative was introduced:
1. **Contrast effect:** Other brands looked less attractive (opposite direction from decoy effects)
2. **Criteria shift:** The phantom's best attribute became the dominant decision criterion
3. **Choice shift:** Students chose the available brand highest on the phantom's key attribute

### The Paper Clip Experiment (Pratkanis, Freeman, & Farquhar)

Students earned money through manual labor, then encountered a surprise phantom — after selecting their preferred paper clips, they were told they couldn't afford them. Two conditions:
- **Direct pressure** ("It's your only choice"): Subjects refused and chose something deliberately different ([[reactance]])
- **Subtle pressure** ("You might want to consider..."): Almost all subjects chose the recommended alternative

In both cases, decisions were driven by phantom-aroused emotions rather than product merits.

### The Phantom Trap

The tendency to marshal most or all resources trying to obtain the unobtainable. **Phantom fixation** (illustrated by Mischel's delay-of-gratification research): children who focused on the unavailable prize experienced unbearable conflict and chose the lesser immediate option; children who distracted themselves waited longer and got the better prize.

## Mechanisms

Scarcity and unavailability trigger a cascade of effects:
1. **Heuristic inference:** Rare = valuable (if it's hard to get, it must be good)
2. **Emotional arousal:** Uniqueness ("I am special because I own something rare"), inadequacy ("I can't get it"), and [[reactance]] (desire to restore lost freedom)
3. **Attentional capture:** The phantom focuses attention on itself and on the criteria where it excels, away from other considerations
4. **The consumer Catch-22 carousel** (C. R. Snyder): Acquiring a rare product makes you like everyone else, heightening the need for the *next* phantom — an endless treadmill of dissatisfaction

## Applications

- **Vaporware:** Preannounced but unavailable products freeze consumer decisions and shift industry criteria
- **Sales tactics:** "Sorry, that model is no longer available, but let me show you something similar..." (closely related to [[lowballing]])
- **Censorship backfire:** Banning *The Satanic Verses* or *Shanghai Baby* increased their perceived value and sales dramatically
- **Forbidden fruit:** Age-restricting content makes it more desirable to young people (Zellinger et al.)

### Three Defenses

1. **Anticipate phantoms** — develop contingency plans in advance; be prepared to walk away
2. **Imagine alternative scenarios** — considering other phantoms or accepting available options reduces fixation
3. **Use phantom emotions as a warning signal** — urgency, inadequacy, and fear of missing out are cues that manipulation may be occurring
