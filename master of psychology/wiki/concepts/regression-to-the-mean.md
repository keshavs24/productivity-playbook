# Regression to the Mean

**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Regression to the mean is the statistical phenomenon where **extreme values on any measure tend to be followed by values closer to the average** on subsequent measurement. People systematically fail to recognize this and instead invent causal explanations for what is a purely statistical effect.

## Key Findings

- Flight instructors observed that praise after a good landing was followed by worse performance, and criticism after a bad landing was followed by improvement. They concluded praise was harmful and criticism was effective. In reality, both outcomes were simply regression to the mean — extreme performances are statistically likely to be followed by more moderate ones. ([[summary-1-01-judgment-under-uncertainty]])
- "The phenomenon of regression remains elusive because it is incompatible with the belief that the predicted outcome should be maximally representative of the input."
- People fail to understand regression because the [[representativeness]] heuristic demands that outcomes match inputs: a brilliant performance "should" predict another brilliant performance

## Mechanisms

Regression is a mathematical inevitability whenever the correlation between two measures is less than perfect (i.e., always in practice). But it feels wrong because the [[representativeness]] heuristic insists that outputs should resemble inputs. An extreme input (a great performance) "should" produce an extreme output (another great performance). When it doesn't, people search for causes rather than recognizing the statistical regularity.

This has a profound secondary effect: it causes people to systematically overvalue punishment and undervalue reward, because punishment is more often followed by improvement (regression from a bad performance) and reward is more often followed by decline (regression from a good performance).

## Applications

- **Management:** Leaders may wrongly conclude that criticism works and praise doesn't, because of the regression pattern
- **Education:** "Sophomore slumps" — exceptional first-year students often regress toward average performance, leading to misattributed explanations
- **Sports:** The "Sports Illustrated cover jinx" — athletes on the cover after exceptional seasons tend to regress
- **Medicine:** Patients who seek treatment at their worst often improve regardless of treatment — regression to the mean is confused with treatment effect
- **Self-improvement:** Extremely good or bad days are followed by more average days; this is statistics, not motivation or willpower
