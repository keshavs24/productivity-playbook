# Status Quo Bias

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Status quo bias is the systematic preference for the current state of affairs. People disproportionately favor options that maintain the status quo over alternatives, even when the alternatives are objectively superior. The bias is a direct consequence of [[loss-aversion]]: any change from the status quo involves potential losses on some dimensions, and these losses loom larger than the corresponding gains.

## Key Findings

- **Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988) demonstrated the bias across domains:** In hypothetical choice problems, designating an option as the status quo significantly increased its selection. The pattern was well described by P(SQ) = 0.17 + 0.83 P(N), where the 0.17 represents a pure status quo advantage. ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch8-endowment-loss-aversion-status-quo]])
- **Real-world evidence from Harvard health plans:** New employees were more likely to choose new health plan options than existing employees -- in spite of minimal switching costs and yearly opportunities to change. Existing employees strongly favored their current plans.
- **TIAA-CREF pension allocations** remained remarkably stable year to year despite large variations in investment returns, consistent with status quo inertia.
- **Knetsch (1989) trading experiment:** When students were given mugs or chocolate bars and offered the chance to trade, approximately 90% retained their initial endowment -- far more than the 50% predicted if preferences were independent of endowment.
- The status quo bias can explain **brand loyalty, pioneer firm advantage, and the prediction that rational models overestimate responsiveness** to changing economic conditions.

## Mechanisms

Status quo bias arises from multiple reinforcing mechanisms:

1. **[[loss-aversion]]:** The primary driver. Any change from the status quo involves giving something up (a loss) and gaining something new (a gain). Because losses loom ~2x as large as gains, the change must be substantially better on the gain dimension to overcome the pain of the loss.
2. **[[endowment-effect]]:** A special case where ownership of the status quo creates inflated valuation.
3. **Cognitive effort:** Maintaining the status quo requires no new thinking; changing requires evaluation effort.
4. **Commitment and consistency:** Psychological commitment to prior choices creates resistance to change (connecting to [[commitment-and-consistency]]).
5. **Regret avoidance:** Changing and experiencing a bad outcome feels worse than not changing and experiencing the same outcome (omission bias).

## Applications

- **Default effects in policy:** Opt-in vs. opt-out framing for organ donation, retirement savings (401k), etc. dramatically changes participation rates because the status quo is sticky
- **Organizational change:** Resistance to change is partly rational (loss aversion) and partly inertial. Leaders must frame changes to minimize perceived losses
- **Negotiation:** The status quo serves as the reference point. Any concession from the status quo is perceived as a loss, making concessions psychologically costly
- **Product design:** Switching costs need not be monetary -- the psychological status quo bias creates its own switching costs
- **Investment:** Investors hold losing stocks too long and fail to rebalance portfolios, partly due to status quo bias
