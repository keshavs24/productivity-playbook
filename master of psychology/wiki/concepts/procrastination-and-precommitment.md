# Procrastination and Precommitment

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

**Procrastination** is the act of giving up long-term goals for immediate gratification — choosing the present "hot" self's desires over the future "cold" self's plans. It is rooted in the same [[hot-cold-empathy-gap]] that causes us to underestimate the pull of temptation. Procrastination is not laziness or poor time management; it is a predictable consequence of [[hyperbolic-discounting]] — the biological tendency to overweight immediate costs and benefits relative to future ones.

**Precommitment** is the strategy of restricting one's own future choices in advance, during a rational "cold" state, to prevent procrastination. Precommitment devices bind the future hot-state self to the cold-state self's plan. The logic is Odyssean: because I know I cannot resist the Sirens, I will have myself tied to the mast before I hear their song.

## The Odysseus Framework

The precommitment concept traces back to Homer's *Odyssey* and was formalized by economist Thomas Schelling in his influential 1978 essay "Egonomics, or the Art of Self-Management" and later in *The Strategy of Conflict*. The framework treats the individual not as a single unified agent but as a series of temporal selves with potentially conflicting preferences:

- **The planner self** (cold, rational, future-oriented) wants to save money, eat well, exercise, and finish projects on time
- **The doer self** (hot, impulsive, present-oriented) wants the dessert, the new gadget, the Netflix episode, and to put off unpleasant work
- **Precommitment** is any arrangement by which the planner self constrains the doer self's options before the moment of temptation arrives

Odysseus knew he could not resist the Sirens' song, so he ordered his crew to tie him to the mast and plug their own ears with wax. He did not attempt to resist through willpower — he made resistance unnecessary by eliminating his future self's options. This is the template for all effective precommitment.

### Schelling's Self-Command

Thomas Schelling extended the Odysseus metaphor into a general theory of "self-command." He argued that many everyday behaviors are strategies of self-management:

- **Burning bridges:** Making it impossible to retreat (Xiang Yu's burned ships, Cortez's scuttled fleet)
- **Side bets:** Wagering money or reputation that you will follow through — the loss if you fail creates an immediate cost to match the deferred benefit
- **Public commitment:** Telling others about your plan, which recruits social pressure as an enforcement mechanism (connects to [[commitment-and-consistency]])
- **Environmental control:** Removing temptations from the environment (no junk food in the house, no credit cards in the wallet, no phone in the bedroom)
- **Bright lines:** Setting clear, non-negotiable rules ("I never eat dessert on weekdays") rather than flexible guidelines ("I'll try to eat less sugar") because bright lines are harder to rationalize around

Schelling noted a paradox: the need for self-command implies that the unified rational agent assumed by classical economics does not exist. We are, in effect, multiple agents with conflicting time preferences occupying the same body.

## Key Findings: Ariely's Three-Class Deadline Experiment

The most important experimental demonstration of both procrastination and precommitment comes from Ariely and Wertenbroch's study at MIT, reported in Ariely Ch. 7 and published as "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment" (2002).

### Experimental Design

Ariely taught three sections of the same class, each with three papers due during the semester. The sections differed only in their deadline structure:

- **Class 1 — Dictated deadlines (maximum external control):** Papers due at weeks 4, 8, and 12, evenly spaced across the 14-week semester. Students had no choice about timing. Late papers penalized 1% per day.
- **Class 2 — Self-imposed deadlines (precommitment opportunity):** At the beginning of the semester, students chose their own deadlines for each of the three papers. Once set, deadlines were binding — late papers penalized 1% per day. Students could set all three at the end if they wished (equivalent to no deadlines), but they could also space them out.
- **Class 3 — No deadlines (maximum freedom):** All three papers due on the last day of class. Students could submit earlier if desired, but there was no penalty for waiting.

### Results

- **Dictated deadlines:** Best grades. Evenly spaced deadlines forced regular engagement with the material and prevented last-minute cramming.
- **Self-imposed deadlines:** Middle grades. Most students recognized their procrastination tendency and voluntarily spaced their deadlines across the semester. But their self-imposed spacing was suboptimal — many bunched deadlines later than the evenly-spaced ideal, and some set all three at the end (effectively opting out of precommitment).
- **No deadlines:** Worst grades. Maximum flexibility produced maximum procrastination. Students waited until the final weeks, then rushed all three papers, producing lower quality work.

### What This Tells Us

1. **People know they procrastinate.** The fact that most Class 2 students voluntarily set early deadlines confirms self-awareness of the problem. This is not a lack of insight — it is a lack of follow-through.
2. **Self-knowledge is imprecise.** Students knew they procrastinated but could not accurately calibrate *how much*. Their self-imposed deadlines were better than nothing but worse than optimal (the dictated spacing). This imprecision is itself a form of the [[hot-cold-empathy-gap]] — cold-state assessment of future hot-state behavior is systematically overoptimistic.
3. **External constraints outperform self-imposed ones.** The best outcomes came from the condition where students had no choice. This is counterintuitive in a culture that values freedom and autonomy, but it is consistent with the precommitment logic: the planner self needs tools that the doer self cannot override.
4. **Freedom can be costly.** The no-deadline condition produced the worst outcomes precisely because it maximized freedom. More options is not always better — a theme that connects to [[keeping-doors-open]] and the [[paradox-of-choice]].

### Interferon Compliance Story

Ariely provides a powerful personal example: during treatment for hepatitis C, he endured 18 months of interferon injections that caused severe side effects (nausea, fever, headaches, vomiting). He was the only patient in his trial cohort who completed the full injection protocol.

His method: **reward substitution.** He rented movies he was excited to watch and paired the movie-watching with the injection. The immediate pleasure of the movie replaced the delayed benefit of treatment as the motivating force. This gave him something to look forward to on injection days rather than something to dread.

The other patients, who relied on willpower alone, gradually stopped complying. The lesson: do not fight the immediacy bias — redirect it. Pair immediate positive reinforcement with delayed-benefit behaviors, replacing the absent immediate reward with a present one.

## Modern Commitment Devices

The precommitment principle has spawned an industry of tools and platforms:

### StickK (Founded by Dean Karlan, Yale)
- Users set a goal and pledge money to an "anti-charity" (an organization they dislike) if they fail
- The threat of money going to a cause you oppose creates an immediate emotional cost for procrastination
- Studies show StickK users with financial stakes are significantly more likely to achieve their goals than those without
- The anti-charity mechanism is particularly clever because it leverages [[loss-aversion]] — the pain of losing money to a hated organization exceeds the pleasure of keeping it

### Beeminder
- Tracks quantified goals (steps, pages written, hours studied) and charges real money when users go off track
- The "Yellow Brick Road" — a visual path showing required daily progress — makes the abstract concrete
- Users can adjust goals, but changes take effect only after a delay (typically a week), preventing hot-state self from dismantling cold-state plans

### Other Modern Devices
- **Freedom / Cold Turkey:** Apps that block distracting websites and apps for set periods, with no override during the block
- **Automatic payroll deductions:** [[save-more-tomorrow]] exploits precommitment for retirement savings — commit now to save more from future raises
- **Gym membership contracts:** Monthly fees create sunk costs that motivate attendance (though this also connects to [[sunk-cost-fallacy]])
- **Deposit-based programs:** Weight loss programs where you deposit money and get it back only if you hit your target

## Connection to Hyperbolic Discounting

Procrastination is the behavioral expression of [[hyperbolic-discounting]]. The formal connection:

- **Exponential discounting** (rational): A person who discounts the future at a constant rate has time-consistent preferences. What they plan to do next month, they will actually do next month. Precommitment is unnecessary.
- **Hyperbolic discounting** (actual human behavior): The discount rate is steep for near-future outcomes and shallow for far-future outcomes. This creates **preference reversals**: from the vantage point of today, you prefer to diet starting Monday. But when Monday arrives, the immediate pain of dieting looms larger, and you prefer to start next Monday instead. This pattern repeats indefinitely.
- **Precommitment solves the preference reversal.** By binding your Monday self to the diet plan during your Sunday self's planning session, you prevent the reversal from occurring.

The formal model: U = u(today) + beta * [delta * u(tomorrow) + delta^2 * u(day after) + ...], where beta < 1 represents present bias. When beta = 1, preferences are time-consistent. When beta < 1, immediate outcomes are overweighted, producing procrastination.

## Mechanisms

1. **Present bias:** Immediate costs and benefits loom larger than future ones. The pain of dieting today outweighs the abstract benefit of future health. The pleasure of watching TV now outweighs the abstract cost of a missed deadline.
2. **Hot-cold gap:** Cold-state plans assume future cold-state compliance. Hot-state impulses (dessert cart, sale, new gadget) override them. The gap between planned and actual behavior is the procrastination gap.
3. **Insufficient self-knowledge:** People recognize they procrastinate but cannot accurately calibrate how much. Self-imposed restrictions are therefore suboptimal — they address some of the problem but not all of it.
4. **Reward substitution:** The most effective precommitment pairs an immediate reward with the desired behavior (movies with injections, social time with exercise, good coffee with studying), replacing the delayed reward with an immediate one. This does not fight the immediacy bias — it redirects it.
5. **Choice architecture:** The default option matters enormously. When the default is "do nothing" (no deadline, opt-in savings), procrastination wins. When the default is "do something" (automatic enrollment, imposed deadlines), long-term interests win. This connects to [[nudge-theory]].

## Applications

- **Personal finance:** Automatic payroll deductions for savings, the "ice glass" method (freezing credit cards in ice to create a cooling-off period), [[save-more-tomorrow]] programs, self-control credit cards with spending limits
- **Health:** Bundling medical procedures into simple, predictable schedules (like Honda's 3-interval maintenance system) reduces procrastination. Refundable deposits for health checkups create commitment. Reward substitution for unpleasant but necessary treatments.
- **Education:** Externally imposed deadlines produce best results, but self-imposed deadlines with penalties are a good second choice. Worst: complete freedom with a single final deadline. Study groups create social accountability — a form of public commitment.
- **Work:** Break large projects into milestones with intermediate deadlines. External accountability (partners, coaches, public commitments) outperforms willpower alone. The "working with the garage door open" movement (public daily logs) is a modern precommitment device.
- **Habit formation:** Pair unpleasant-but-beneficial activities with enjoyable ones (exercise while watching favorite shows, study with good coffee, deep work paired with favorite music). The reward substitution principle.
- **Ford's maintenance bundling:** Ford adopted Honda's 3-interval approach, simplifying 18,000 maintenance items into 3 simple service events. Service bay utilization went from 40% to full in 3 years. Simplification is itself a precommitment device — it removes the decision point where procrastination enters.

## Open Questions
- What is the optimal level of external restriction? Too little and people procrastinate; too much and they resent the constraint. Does self-determination theory ([[basic-psychological-needs]]) suggest that autonomy-supportive precommitment (choosing your own constraints) is more sustainable than imposed ones?
- Can technology (apps, smart devices) serve as effective precommitment devices, or does the ease of overriding them (deleting the app, changing settings) undermine their function?
- How does cultural variation in time orientation affect procrastination and the effectiveness of precommitment?
- Is there a "precommitment personality"? Do people who naturally use precommitment strategies differ in trait self-control, conscientiousness, or temporal orientation?
- How do we design precommitment devices for collective action problems (climate change, public health) where no single individual can bind the group?
