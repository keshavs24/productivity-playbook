# Availability Heuristic

**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Confidence:** 0.8

## Definition

The availability heuristic is a mental shortcut where people assess the frequency of a class or the probability of an event by **the ease with which instances or occurrences can be brought to mind**. Events that are easier to recall or imagine are judged as more probable.

## Key Findings

- Ease of retrieval is used as a proxy for frequency/probability ([[summary-1-01-judgment-under-uncertainty]])
- Factors that affect retrieval but not actual frequency — **familiarity, salience, recency, vividness** — systematically bias frequency judgments
- Famous names on a list cause people to overestimate that gender's proportion, because fame increases retrievability
- People judge words starting with "r" as more common than words with "r" in third position — because the search-by-first-letter strategy is easier, even though the latter is actually more frequent
- When frequency must be assessed by mental construction (imaginability), the ease of imagining scenarios dominates rather than their actual likelihood
- **[[illusory-correlation]]:** Associatively linked events are judged to co-occur more often than they actually do, because the association makes co-occurrences more available in memory

## Mechanisms

Availability works because memory retrieval is the mind's most accessible way to estimate "how often does this happen?" The speed and ease of recall correlate with actual frequency often enough to be useful — things that happen often really are easier to remember. But the correlation is imperfect: many factors inflate availability without increasing frequency (media coverage, emotional salience, personal experience, narrative vividness).

The deeper issue: people do not distinguish between "I can think of many examples" and "there are many examples." The subjective experience of ease-of-recall is treated as evidence about the world.

## Applications

- **Risk assessment:** People overestimate risks that are vivid and media-covered (plane crashes, terrorism) and underestimate mundane high-frequency risks (heart disease, car accidents)
- **Marketing:** Products and brands that are more "available" (through advertising, social proof, word-of-mouth) are perceived as more popular and trustworthy
- **Medical diagnosis:** Recent or dramatic cases bias a physician's diagnostic probabilities
- **Project planning:** Scenarios that are easy to imagine (success stories) dominate planning; hard-to-imagine failure modes are underweighted

## Availability in Attribution (Ross & Anderson, Ch. 9)

Ross and Anderson ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch9-attribution-shortcomings]]) identify availability/salience as a major driver of attribution biases:

- Whenever an aspect of the environment is disproportionately salient, it receives more weight in causal attribution. Actors are generally more salient than situations, which may be the root mechanism of the [[fundamental-attribution-error]].
- **Taylor et al. (1976):** Actors with unique racial or sexual status in a group are assigned disproportionate causal responsibility.
- **Storms (1973); Taylor & Fiske (1975):** Manipulating visual perspective shifts attributions -- the person you can see is judged more causally responsible.
- **Non-occurrences** (the "dog that didn't bark") are informationally valuable but cognitively unavailable, and thus systematically underweighted in attribution.
- The [[false-consensus-effect]] operates partly through availability: behaviors we have chosen are more easily recalled and imagined, inflating our estimates of their commonness.

## Bazerman & Moore Extensions

Bazerman and Moore ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch1]], [[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch3]]) add substantial managerial applications:

- **Ease of recall bias:** WHO data shows people overestimate vivid causes of death (war, starvation) and underestimate common ones (respiratory infections -- the actual leading cause); vivid media coverage distorts perceived frequency
- **Schwarz et al. (1991):** People asked to list 12 examples of their assertiveness rated themselves as *less* assertive than those asked for 6 -- the difficulty of retrieval was interpreted as evidence of low assertiveness, despite listing more instances
- **Risen and Gilovich (2008):** "Tempting fate" actions (not doing assigned reading) make negative outcomes feel more available and likely
- **Performance appraisals:** Managers weight employee behavior immediately prior to evaluations more heavily than the previous nine months -- recency makes recent behavior more available
- **Insurance purchasing:** People are more likely to buy earthquake/flood insurance after experiencing a disaster, even when objective risk has not increased (Lindell & Perry, 2000; Palm, 1995)
- **Investment:** Peter Lynch (Fidelity Magellan Fund) argued for buying stock in firms unavailable to most investors' minds -- the more available, the more overvalued
- **Distinction from [[bounded-awareness]]:** Availability is a general cognitive heuristic (vivid = common across contexts); bounded awareness identifies domain-specific variables predictably out of focus ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch4]])

## Open Questions
- How does availability interact with representativeness when they conflict? (A thing can be easy to recall but not representative, or vice versa.)
- What is the role of affect and emotion in availability? (Later research -- the "affect heuristic" -- suggests emotional reactions amplify availability.)
- Can deliberate strategies (e.g., "consider the opposite") effectively counteract availability bias?
- How does availability interact with [[bounded-awareness]]? Both involve information that is missed, but through different mechanisms.
