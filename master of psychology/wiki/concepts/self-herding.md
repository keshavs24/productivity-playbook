# Self-Herding

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Self-herding occurs when we use our own previous behavior as a signal of our preferences, following behind our past selves rather than re-evaluating the decision from scratch. Unlike regular herding (following others), self-herding involves "lining up behind yourself" -- assuming that because you did something before, it must have been a good decision, and therefore repeating it.

## Key Findings

- **Starbucks example (Ariely):** Once you buy a coffee at Starbucks, the next time you pass by, you recall "I went there before, so it must be a good decision." You become the second person in line -- behind yourself. Each repetition strengthens the conviction. What started as an arbitrary first decision compounds into a deeply held "preference." ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch2-supply-demand]])
- Self-herding transforms arbitrary initial decisions (set by [[arbitrary-coherence]] and [[anchoring-and-adjustment]]) into stable behavioral patterns that feel like genuine preferences
- The mechanism explains how consumers move from a first Starbucks purchase to habitual patronage without ever re-evaluating whether the coffee is worth the premium over Dunkin' Donuts

## Mechanisms

1. **Behavioral consistency:** We use our past actions as evidence for what we like -- a form of self-attribution (see [[commitment-and-consistency]])
2. **Cognitive economy:** Re-evaluating every decision from scratch is costly. Using past behavior as a shortcut is efficient, even if the original decision was arbitrary
3. **Compounding effect:** Each repetition strengthens the behavioral evidence, making the "preference" feel more authentic over time
4. **Lateral spreading:** Once we accept one price point (e.g., small Starbucks coffee at $2.20), moving to related products at the same brand (medium for $3.50, Venti for $4.15) feels coherent

## Applications

- **Consumer behavior:** Companies benefit from getting a first purchase at any cost -- free samples, deep discounts -- because self-herding will bring the customer back at full price
- **Habit formation:** Self-herding explains why habits are so sticky -- each repetition serves as evidence that the behavior is worthwhile
- **Debiasing:** To break self-herding, explicitly question the first decision that started a behavioral chain. Ask: "How did this habit begin? Is the pleasure as much as I thought it would be?"
