# Cooperation and Liking

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Cooperation toward shared goals is one of the most powerful generators of liking between individuals and groups. While mere contact or even pleasant shared activities do not reliably produce liking (and can backfire under competitive conditions), structured cooperation toward mutually beneficial goals consistently produces friendship, reduces prejudice, and increases intergroup harmony.

This finding has profound implications for both compliance practice and social policy, particularly in education and intergroup relations.

## Key Findings

### Sherif's Robbers Cave Experiment (Sherif et al., 1961)

Muzafer Sherif investigated intergroup conflict in boys' summer camps:

- **Creating conflict was easy:** Separating boys into two groups ("Eagles" and "Rattlers") and introducing competitive activities rapidly produced hostility — name-calling, cabin raids, food fights
- **Mere contact failed:** Pleasant joint activities (movies, picnics, social events) between hostile groups were disastrous — food fights, shouting contests, shoving matches
- **Cooperative goals succeeded:** Situations requiring cooperation for mutual benefit (stuck truck, water supply crisis, pooling money for a movie) steadily bridged the rift. Boys began to intermix, list cross-group best friends, and even spent their treasury buying milkshakes for former adversaries

### Aronson's Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson et al., 1978)

- Students grouped into cooperating teams where each holds one piece of the information needed to pass a test
- Produced significantly more cross-ethnic friendship and less prejudice
- Increased minority students' self-esteem, liking for school, and test scores
- White students' performance was at least as high as in traditional classes (Aronson, Bridgeman, & Geffner, 1978a, 1978b)

### Broader Findings

- Successful cooperation reduces intergroup hostility in college groups (Worchel, 1979) and business organizations (Blake & Mouton, 1979)
- Cooperation leads to both greater liking *and* greater group success (Stanne, D. W. Johnson, & R. T. Johnson, 1999)
- Cooperative learning issues are complex (Rosenfield & Stephan, 1981; Slavin, 1983) — competition still has its place as a motivator

## Mechanisms

1. **Shared goals transform rivals into allies:** When success requires cooperation, group members experience each other as valuable helpers rather than competitors
2. **Mutual success breeds positive associations:** The positive emotion of shared triumph becomes associated with the cooperating partners ([[conditioning-and-association]])
3. **Individuation:** Cooperation requires working closely enough with others to see them as individuals rather than interchangeable group members
4. **Reciprocal helping:** Each member's contribution creates a web of mutual obligation and gratitude

## Applications

- **Education:** Jigsaw classroom and cooperative learning techniques reduce intergroup prejudice while maintaining or improving academic performance
- **Compliance — Good Cop/Bad Cop:** Police interrogators manufacture cooperation by positioning Good Cop as working *with* the suspect against Bad Cop, combining cooperation with [[contrast-principle]] and [[reciprocation]]
- **Sales:** Car salespeople position themselves as cooperating with the customer against the dealership/sales manager to secure a good deal
- **Team building:** Any organizational structure that creates shared goals across group boundaries can leverage the cooperation-liking mechanism
- **Conflict resolution:** The Robbers Cave findings suggest that dialogue and pleasant contact are insufficient — structural interdependence is required
