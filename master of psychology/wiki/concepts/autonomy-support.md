# Autonomy Support

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Autonomy support is an interpersonal orientation in which an authority figure (teacher, parent, manager, coach) relates to others by understanding their perspectives, providing meaningful choices, offering rationale for requests, minimizing controlling language, and acknowledging feelings. It contrasts with a **controlling** orientation, which relies on external pressures (rewards, punishments, evaluations, deadlines) to motivate behavior.

Within [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]], autonomy-supportive environments facilitate [[intrinsic-motivation]] by supporting basic psychological needs for [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], [[competence-need|competence]], and relatedness. Controlling environments undermine intrinsic motivation by thwarting these needs.

Autonomy support is not permissiveness or absence of structure. Structure and limits can be provided autonomy-supportively -- the key is **how** they are communicated, not whether they exist.

## Key Findings

### Education
- **Deci, Schwartz, Sheinman, & Ryan (1981):** Teachers rated as more autonomy-supportive (via "Problems in School" vignettes) had students with higher intrinsic motivation, perceived competence, and self-esteem within 2 months. Effects persisted at 8 months
- **Ryan & Grolnick (1986):** Students' perceptions of classroom climate as autonomy-supportive vs. controlling predicted intrinsic motivation, self-esteem, and feelings of personal control. Children wrote projective stories reflecting their actual classroom climate
- **Grolnick & Ryan (1987):** Noncontrolling learning conditions produced more interest and greater conceptual understanding; controlling conditions produced more rote memorization that deteriorated faster
- **Reeve & Jang (2006):** Identified 8 specific autonomy-supportive and 6 controlling teacher behaviors empirically validated through correlations with students' autonomous motivation (see [[autonomy-support-in-education]])
- **Tsai, Kunter, Ludtke, Trautwein, & Ryan (2008):** Even lesson-to-lesson fluctuations in perceived teacher autonomy support covaried with students' interest in German schools
- **Jang, Reeve, & Deci (2010):** Autonomy support was a very strong predictor of student engagement; structure contributed additional variance. High autonomy support + clear structure = best outcomes
- **Black & Deci (2000):** College organic chemistry -- autonomy-supportive instructors facilitated autonomous self-regulation, higher grades (controlling for ability), and decreased anxiety
- **Su & Reeve (2011):** Meta-analysis of 19 training studies: large effect size (d=0.63) for training teachers/supervisors to be more autonomy-supportive
- **Deci, Spiegel, Ryan, Koestner, & Kauffman (1982):** Performance-standards pressure caused teachers to become controlling (talked 5x as much, more directives), leading to poorer student performance
- See [[autonomy-support-in-education]] for comprehensive education-specific treatment including special populations and the umbrella effect

### Parenting
- **Deci, Driver, Hotchkiss, Robbins, & Wilson (1993):** Mothers rated as more autonomy-supportive during play had children who showed more free-choice intrinsic motivation for the target activity
- **Gottfried, Marcoulides, Gottfried, & Oliver (2009):** Task-intrinsic parenting practices predicted higher motivation through age 17; task-extrinsic (reward-based) practices predicted adverse effects

### Workplace
- **Deci, Connell, & Ryan (1989):** First large-scale SDT field intervention. Fortune 500 company -- managers trained in three autonomy-supportive concepts (perspective-taking, informational feedback, supporting initiative/choice). Over 13 months, trained managers became more autonomy-supportive; subordinates showed positive changes in perceptions, satisfaction, and trust -- including trust in top management. Company attitude surveys confirmed positive change in intervention vs. control branches. **ROI exceeded 3:1** when mental health savings were calculated (Forest, Gilbert, Beaulieu, Le Brock, & Gagne, 2014)
- **Hardre & Reeve (2009):** Fortune 500 managers randomly assigned to autonomy-support training vs. control. Five weeks later, trained managers were significantly more autonomy-supportive; their employees were significantly more autonomous and engaged
- **Olafsen, Halvari, Forest, & Deci (2015):** Autonomy support was strongly related to [[need-satisfaction-at-work]], [[intrinsic-motivation]], and procedural justice. Pay level was not related to any of these outcomes
- **Baard, Deci, & Ryan (2004):** In banking, both autonomy orientation and perceived autonomy support predicted basic need satisfaction, which predicted work performance and well-being
- **Lynch, Plant, & Ryan (2005):** Hospital staff receiving autonomy-supportive change management internalized new practices; as staff became less controlling, patients displayed more autonomous motivation for treatment
- **Gagne, Koestner, & Zuckerman (2000):** Autonomy-supportive introduction of organizational change (rationale + choice + acknowledgment) reduced employee resistance even during disruptive downsizing
- See [[autonomy-supportive-management]] for the full workplace application and [[summary-3-10-sdt-ch21-work]] for comprehensive review

### Coaching/Athletics
- **Bartholomew, Ntoumanis, Ryan, Bosch, & Thogersen-Ntoumani (2011):** Coaches' autonomy-supportive vs. autonomy-thwarting styles independently predicted need satisfaction/frustration. Thwarting coaches' athletes showed elevated stress biomarkers (SIgA)

### Limit Setting
- **Koestner, Ryan, Bernieri, & Holt (1984):** Limits on children's painting set autonomy-supportively (minimizing controlling language, acknowledging feelings, providing rationale) maintained intrinsic motivation. Controlling limits undermined it

### Autonomy Support as Moderator
- **Ryan, Mims, & Koestner (1983):** Performance-contingent rewards in an autonomy-supportive context led to higher intrinsic motivation than a neutral control. Same rewards in controlling context undermined motivation

## Mechanisms

Elements of autonomy-supportive communication:
1. **Minimizing controlling language:** Avoiding "should," "must," "have to"; using invitational language
2. **Acknowledging feelings:** Recognizing that the person may not want to do something, validating their perspective
3. **Providing rationale:** Explaining why a limit, rule, or request exists rather than simply imposing it
4. **Offering meaningful choice:** Allowing decisions about what to do, how, and when (action choice is more effective than option choice; Reeve, Nix, & Hamm, 2003)
5. **Taking the other's perspective:** Understanding the subordinate's frame of reference (de Charms, 1976: treating people as "origins" rather than "pawns")

Autonomy support and relatedness support tend to co-occur: when people are autonomy-supportive, they naturally take the other's perspective, which is experienced as caring (Costa, Ntoumanis, & Bartholomew, 2015).

## Applications

- **Teaching:** Use informational rather than evaluative feedback; provide choice in assignments; explain rationale for requirements; avoid using grades as threats
- **Parenting:** Set limits with explanation and acknowledgment; avoid contingent self-worth; support children's interests rather than imposing achievement agendas
- **Management:** Explain the "why" behind directives; offer flexibility in how tasks are accomplished; use feedback to inform rather than control; avoid surveillance as a motivational tool
- **Coaching:** Provide structure and strategy while supporting athletes' sense of volition; avoid need-thwarting (which independently predicts negative outcomes beyond mere absence of support)

### Close Relationships (RMT Findings, Ch. 12)
[[Relationships-motivation-theory|Relationships Motivation Theory]] identifies autonomy support as the central contextual factor in high-quality close relationships:

- **Emotional reliance:** Individuals who experience autonomy support from close partners are more willing to turn to those partners during emotional events. This holds across four cultures -- Russia, Turkey, South Korea, and the U.S. (Ryan, La Guardia, Solky-Butzel, Chirkov, & Kim, 2005)
- **Authenticity and ideal self:** People express personality traits closer to their ideal self (Big Five) when with autonomy-supportive partners, across Russia, U.S., and China (Lynch et al., 2009). Less autonomy support pushed people toward less extraverted, less open, less agreeable, and more neurotic expression.
- **Concealment decreases:** Autonomy support is associated with greater self-disclosure and less self-concealment; concealment thwarts autonomy and relatedness and predicts lower relationship satisfaction (Uysal, Lin, & Knee, 2010, 2012)
- **Giving autonomy support benefits the giver:** In a landmark finding, Deci, La Guardia, Moller, Scheiner, & Ryan (2006) showed that *giving* autonomy support to a best friend was more strongly related to the giver's own well-being than *receiving* it. Giving had positive relations to every measured variable except negative affect.
- **Mutuality amplifies effects:** When both partners in a dyad perceive mutual autonomy support, relationship satisfaction, attachment security, emotional reliance, and well-being are all enhanced beyond the effects of one-sided autonomy support (RMT Proposition V; Deci, La Guardia et al., 2006)
- **Priming autonomy improves stranger interactions:** Strangers primed with autonomy orientations developed more satisfying new relationships with more positive affect and behavioral closeness (Niemiec, 2010). Primed dyads were more attuned, empathic, and effective at collaborative tasks (Weinstein, Hodgins, & Ryan, 2010).

## Open Questions
- Autonomy-support training for teachers is effective (Su & Reeve, 2011; d=0.63). Key question remaining: what specific training curricula produce the most durable changes?
- Is there a point at which too much choice becomes overwhelming and counterproductive (choice overload)?
- How does autonomy support need to be calibrated for different developmental stages (children vs. adolescents vs. adults)?
- In high-stakes safety-critical contexts (surgery, aviation), how does autonomy support coexist with necessary compliance requirements?
