# Big Idea (Presentation)

**Phase(s):** 7
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Big Idea is the one key message a presenter wants to communicate — the controlling idea, thesis statement, or single unifying message that compels the audience to set a new course with a new compass heading. Coined in the context of presentations by [[duarte|Nancy Duarte]] in *[[resonate]]*, the term is adapted from screenwriting where it is called the "controlling idea." It serves as both the well from which all supporting ideas spring and the filter to sort ideas down to the most applicable ones.

## Key Findings

A Big Idea has three required components:

1. **Your unique point of view on a topic.** People came to hear *you* speak. "The fate of the oceans" is a topic, not a big idea. "Worldwide pollution is killing the ocean and us" is a big idea — it has a pointed perspective.

2. **A clear statement of what is at stake** for those who do or do not adopt your point of view. The big idea should articulate the reason the audience should care enough to adopt your perspective. "Without better legislation, the destruction of the wetlands will cost the Florida economy $70 billion by 2025" conveys stakes.

3. **It must be a complete sentence** — with a noun and a verb. This forces specificity and emotional charge. "Third-quarter update" is not a big idea. "Third-quarter numbers are down, and to stay in the game, every department needs to support the sales initiative" is a big idea.

Emotion is a critical component. Ultimately, there are only two emotions — pleasure and pain. A truly persuasive presentation plays on these:
- Raise the likelihood of pain and lower the likelihood of pleasure if they reject the big idea
- Raise the likelihood of pleasure and lower the likelihood of pain if they accept the big idea

The gravity of the presentation should match the severity of the situation and accurately reflect what is at stake — no more, no less.

## Mechanisms

The big idea works as both a **generative device** and a **filtering device**. As a generative device, it is the well from which all supporting ideas spring. As a filter, it eliminates any content that does not distinctly support the one idea. Most presentations suffer from too many ideas, not too few. The big idea provides the discipline to "murder your darlings" — cut anything that does not serve the central message, no matter how interesting it is on its own.

This connects to [[commanders-intent]] from *Made to Stick*: both concepts emphasize finding the single most important thing and making it the core of all communication. It also connects to [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]]: the big idea opens a gap between what is and what could be that the audience wants to resolve.

## Applications

- Before creating any presentation content, write out the big idea as a complete sentence
- Test it against the three criteria: unique POV, clear stakes, sentence form
- Use the big idea to evaluate every piece of content: does it support the big idea? If not, cut it
- Include the word "you" in the big idea sentence to ensure it is written to someone
- Practice articulating the big idea in one sentence — if you cannot, it is not clear enough

|---|---|
