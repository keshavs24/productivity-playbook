# Competitive Escalation

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Competitive escalation is the tendency for competing parties to escalate their commitments beyond rational limits when the motive shifts from profit-seeking to "winning" the competition. Unlike unilateral escalation (driven by self-justification), competitive escalation adds the force of rivalry and the desire to beat the other party.

## Key Findings

- **Shubik (1971) dollar auction:** Highest bidder wins $20, but second-highest bidder also pays their bid and gets nothing. Bidding typically ends between $20-$70, has hit $100. Authors earned over $30,000 running this in classes over 20 years. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch8]])
- **Mechanism of the trap:** At $16 vs. $17, the $16 bidder faces a choice between a certain $16 loss (quit) or the uncertain gamble of bidding $18 (might win). Risk seeking in the loss domain makes the gamble more attractive. "The decision to bid $21 is very similar to all previous decisions."
- **Corporate escalation cases:**
  - Companies A, B, C scenario: Acquiring company C (worth $1B standalone, $1.2B if acquired) escalates to $1.7B+ as A and B try to avoid $0.5B competitive disadvantage
  - 1995 USAir: American Airlines' Crandall avoided escalation through an open letter signaling mutual destruction
  - 2004-2006 Guidant: J&J and Boston Scientific engaged in classic escalation despite product defects. Boston Scientific paid $27B (more than J&J's pre-scandal bid). Boston Scientific's stock fell from $35.88 to below $17
- **Ku, Malhotra, & Murnighan (2005):** Art auction bidders routinely bid more than their pre-auction valuation
- **Ku, Galinsky, & Murnighan (2006):** Products available more cheaply elsewhere were bid up in auctions with many bidders
- The competitive-escalation paradigm adds one crucial element to unilateral escalation: "the desire to win serves as added motivation to escalate commitment"

## Mechanisms

All four causes of [[escalation-of-commitment]] apply, plus competitive irrationality:
- The goal shifts from profit to beating the opponent
- Each party's decision to continue is individually defensible given the other's likely behavior
- Communication can break the cycle (Crandall's letter) but often fails when pride and face are at stake
- **The key is recognizing traps before entry.** Once inside, each incremental step looks rational.

## Applications

- **Auctions:** Set hard limits before bidding begins and commit to walking away
- **M&A:** Consider whether you are acquiring strategic value or merely winning a bidding war
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Watch for competitive escalation dynamics. Try to reframe from "winning" to "finding the best outcome"
- **Reverse-bid procurement:** Same dynamics in reverse -- sellers escalate by underbidding
- **Personal conflicts:** Divorce proceedings, custody battles, neighbor disputes can all exhibit competitive escalation
