# Arbitrary Coherence

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Arbitrary coherence is the phenomenon whereby **initial prices (or valuations) are largely arbitrary**, but once established in the mind, they shape **all subsequent pricing judgments in a coherent, internally logical manner**. The first price serves as an anchor ([[anchoring-and-adjustment]]) and all future decisions maintain logical relationships relative to that anchor -- even though the anchor itself was random.

The term was coined by Dan Ariely, Drazen Prelec, and George Loewenstein.

## Key Findings

- **Social security number experiment ([[summary-2-S1-ariely-ch2]]):** Students who wrote down higher last-two-digits of their SSN bid 216-346% more for products than those with lower digits. Yet **within** categories, relative pricing was perfectly coherent (everyone bid more for the keyboard than the trackball)
- **Annoying sounds experiment:** Participants anchored at 10 cents demanded ~33 cents to hear an annoying sound again; those anchored at 90 cents demanded ~73 cents. The first anchor persisted across three phases of the experiment, even when new anchors were introduced
- **Poetry reading experiment:** Half the students were asked if they'd pay $10 to hear Ariely read poetry; the other half were asked if they'd accept $10 to listen. Those framed as "paying" offered $1-$3; those framed as "being paid" demanded $1.30-$4.80. The same ambiguous experience was made positive or negative by the arbitrary initial framing
- **Salvador Assael's black pearls:** Created an entirely new luxury market by arbitrarily placing previously worthless Tahitian black pearls in Harry Winston's Fifth Avenue window and advertising them alongside diamonds. The initial high-price anchor stuck
- **Starbucks:** Howard Shultz deliberately made the Starbucks experience completely different from Dunkin' Donuts (different sizes, names, ambience) to prevent customers from anchoring to Dunkin' prices

## Mechanisms

1. **Imprinting:** Like Konrad Lorenz's goslings that follow the first moving object they see, humans "imprint" on the first price they encounter for a product category
2. **Self-herding:** Once we make a first decision (e.g., buying coffee at Starbucks), we line up behind our own past behavior, assuming our first choice was correct and repeating it
3. **Coherent adjustment:** After the arbitrary anchor is set, all subsequent valuations maintain logical internal relationships (more for better wine, less for worse wine), creating the illusion of rational preference

## Applications

- **Pricing strategy:** The first price a customer encounters for a new product category sets their anchor. It should be carefully chosen, not left to chance
- **Brand positioning:** To escape existing anchors (as Starbucks did), make the experience feel categorically different so old prices don't serve as reference points
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** The first number in a negotiation becomes the anchor. Arbitrary coherence means all subsequent numbers will orbit around it
- **Policy:** Demand is not independent of supply -- MSRPs, advertised prices, and promotions (all supply-side) directly shape what consumers believe things are worth
- **Self-awareness:** Question your repeated behaviors. Are they truly preferences, or arbitrary first imprints that you've been coherently following?
