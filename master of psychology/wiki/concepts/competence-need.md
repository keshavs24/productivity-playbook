# Competence (Basic Psychological Need)

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Competence is one of the three basic psychological needs posited by [[self-determination-theory]] (alongside [[autonomy-need|autonomy]] and relatedness). It refers to the experience of effectiveness and mastery -- the sense that one can successfully interact with the environment, exercise and extend one's capacities, and produce desired outcomes. The concept traces to White's (1959) effectance motivation: organisms have an inherent tendency to feel competent and effective in dealing with their environment.

Within [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]], competence satisfaction is a necessary condition for [[intrinsic-motivation]] -- but only when accompanied by autonomy satisfaction. Feeling competent in a controlling context does not enhance intrinsic motivation.

## Key Findings

- **CET Proposition II:** Events that enhance perceived competence enhance intrinsic motivation; events that diminish perceived competence undermine it
- **Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis:** Positive feedback (verbal rewards) significantly enhanced intrinsic motivation for college students (d=0.43), primarily through competence satisfaction
- **Vallerand & Reid (1984); Grouzet et al. (2004); Vansteenkiste & Deci (2003):** Perceived competence mediates the relationship between positive feedback and intrinsic motivation
- **Ryan (1982):** Positive competence feedback in a controlling context did **not** enhance intrinsic motivation -- autonomy must also be present. Competence satisfaction is necessary but not sufficient
- **Danner & Lonky (1981):** Children naturally select activities at optimal challenge level (one step ahead of ability) -- where competence feedback is most informational
- **Negative feedback:** Diminishes perceived competence and reduces intrinsic motivation (Deci & Cascio, 1972; Karniol & Ross, 1977). Severe negative feedback can produce amotivation
- **Optimal challenge:** Intrinsic motivation is a growth function requiring tasks at the leading edge of competence -- easy tasks do not exercise competence, and impossible tasks frustrate it (Deci, 1975)
- **Harter (1974, 1978b):** Children working on optimally challenging tasks displayed greater pleasure than those on very easy or very difficult tasks

## Mechanisms

1. **Effectance:** The inherent satisfaction of producing effects, mastering challenges, and extending capabilities
2. **Optimal challenge:** Competence is most satisfying when tasks are at the zone of proximal development -- difficult enough to stretch but achievable with effort
3. **Feedback integration:** People integrate competence information from task-inherent feedback (puzzle pieces fit, climbing progress) and other-mediated feedback (praise, evaluation). Task-inherent feedback is naturally informational
4. **Competence + Autonomy requirement:** Perceived competence enhances intrinsic motivation only within a context of perceived autonomy. This is why positive feedback in a controlling context fails to enhance, and why performance-contingent rewards (which convey competence but also control) have mixed effects

## Applications

- **Education:** Design activities at optimal challenge levels where students can experience mastery. Provide informational feedback that highlights growth and progress rather than ranking
- **Game design:** Calibrate difficulty to match and slightly exceed player skill (flow channel). Task-inherent feedback (visual/auditory confirmation of success) sustains intrinsic motivation
- **Workplace:** Assign tasks that stretch employee skills. Provide feedback focused on development rather than evaluation
- **Parenting:** Encourage children to attempt challenging tasks. Celebrate effort and progress, not just outcomes. Avoid tasks that are too easy (boring) or too hard (frustrating)

## BPNT Findings on Competence (Ch. 10)

- **Competence vs. [[self-efficacy]]:** The competence need in SDT and Bandura's self-efficacy are complementary but distinct constructs. Self-efficacy concerns *beliefs* about one's capability to execute courses of action. Competence need concerns the *experience* of effectance -- feeling effective in one's interactions. Self-efficacy is domain-specific and cognitive; competence need is a universal nutriment whose satisfaction yields affective and motivational consequences regardless of specific beliefs.
- **Within-person fluctuations:** Daily variations in competence satisfaction independently predict daily well-being (Sheldon, Ryan, & Reis, 1996; Reis et al., 2000)
- **Exercise domain:** Competence satisfaction most strongly mediated the relation between autonomy support and vitality in exercise participants (Vlachopoulos & Karavani, 2009) -- competence is especially salient in domains where physical mastery is central
- **Work settings:** Competence satisfaction on the job predicted both work-related engagement and personal wellness outcomes (Ilardi et al., 1993; Baard et al., 2004)
- **Cross-cultural universality:** Competence need satisfaction predicted unique variance in well-being across Belgium, China, U.S., and Peru, with equivalent effect magnitudes (Chen et al., 2015)

## Open Questions
- How does the need for competence interact with perfectionism? Can the pursuit of competence become controlling (as in [[ego-involvement]])?
- Is there a ceiling effect for competence satisfaction -- do experts need increasingly rare demonstrations of mastery to feel competent?
- How does competence need satisfaction differ for physical vs. cognitive vs. social domains?
- How does the competence need relate to mastery goals (Dweck, 2000; Elliot & McGregor, 2001)? GCT (Ch. 11) suggests mastery goals are more intrinsic and need-satisfying than performance goals.
