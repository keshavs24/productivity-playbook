# Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Organismic Integration Theory (OIT) is the second of SDT's six mini-theories. While [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]] addresses the social-contextual factors that enhance or undermine [[intrinsic-motivation]], OIT addresses the parallel question for [[extrinsic-motivation]]: how do people take in external regulations and progressively transform them into self-regulations? OIT describes the [[internalization]] continuum -- the process by which behaviors initially motivated by external contingencies can become progressively more autonomous through introjection, identification, and integration.

## Key Findings

- OIT specifies four regulatory styles for extrinsic motivation, each representing a different degree of internalization: [[external-regulation]], [[introjected-regulation]], [[identified-regulation]], and [[integrated-regulation]]
- These styles are ordered along a **continuum of relative autonomy** verified empirically through quasi-simplex correlation patterns (Ryan & Connell, 1989)
- The continuum is NOT a developmental stage sequence -- people can enter at any point and transitions can be regressive as well as progressive
- Internalization is facilitated by satisfaction of [[basic-psychological-needs]] -- especially [[relatedness-need|relatedness]] (for initial uptake), [[competence-need|competence]] (for enactment), and [[autonomy-need|autonomy]] (for moving beyond introjection to integration)
- More autonomous forms of internalization predict greater behavioral persistence, higher quality performance, and better psychological health
- The concept of **compartmentalized identifications** explains how people can strongly identify with a value yet defensively isolate it from other self-knowledge

## Five Formal Propositions

1. **Proposition I:** Humans naturally tend to internalize extrinsic motivations endorsed by significant others, but the process can function more or less effectively
2. **Proposition II:** Internalization forms a continuum from controlled to autonomous self-regulation
3. **Proposition III:** Need support (competence, relatedness, autonomy) facilitates internalization and integration; controlling contexts thwart it
4. **Proposition IV:** More autonomous internalization produces greater persistence, higher quality behavior, and more effective performance
5. **Proposition V:** More integrated internalization produces more positive experiences and greater well-being

## Mechanisms

OIT operates through the [[organismic-integration]] process -- the inherent human tendency to assimilate, organize, and integrate experiences. The quality of internalization depends on the social context:
- **Autonomy-supportive contexts** (choice, rationale, acknowledging feelings) produce identified and integrated regulation
- **Controlling contexts** (pressure, conditional regard, surveillance) produce at best introjection, at worst amotivation
- **Relatedness** energizes the uptake; **competence** enables the enactment; **autonomy** enables the full transformation

## Applications

- **Health behavior change:** Autonomy support from physicians predicts autonomous patient motivation and sustained behavior change (Williams et al., 2002; Ng et al., 2012 meta-analysis)
- **Education:** Teacher autonomy support predicts identified/integrated academic regulation and student outcomes (Ryan & Connell, 1989; Grolnick & Ryan, 1989)
- **Parenting:** Parental autonomy support predicts internalization of family values; controlling parenting produces introjection and defiance (Grolnick & Ryan, 1989; Knafo & Assor, 2007)
- **Goal setting:** Self-concordant goals (autonomous) predict sustained effort, attainment, and well-being (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999)
