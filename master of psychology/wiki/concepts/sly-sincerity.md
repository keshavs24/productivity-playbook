# Sly Sincerity

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Sly sincerity is a compliance tactic in which a communicator argues somewhat against their own interests — typically by mentioning a small shortcoming or weakness in their position, product, or candidacy — in order to establish perceived trustworthiness. The admitted drawback is invariably secondary, easily overcome by more significant advantages. By demonstrating honesty on minor issues, the communicator becomes dramatically more believable on the important aspects of their argument. The tactic exploits the second of [[cialdini]]'s two defensive questions against the [[authority-principle]]: "How truthful can we expect the expert to be?"

## Key Findings

- We are more swayed by experts who seem impartial than by those who have something to gain (Eagly, Wood, & Chaiken, 1978)
- This bias toward seemingly impartial sources is found worldwide (McGuinnies & Ward, 1980) and in children as young as second-graders (Mills & Keil, 2005)
- Even knowledgeable authorities will not persuade us until we are satisfied their messages faithfully represent the facts (Van Overwalle & Heylighen, 2006)
- Letters of recommendation produced the **most favorable results** for job candidates when they contained **one unflattering comment** in an otherwise wholly positive set of remarks (Knouse, 1983)
- Companies that take blame for poor outcomes in annual reports have **higher stock prices one year later** than companies that deflect blame (Lee, Peterson, & Tiedens, 2004)
- The tactic is supported by research on one-sided vs. two-sided persuasive messages (Hunt, Domzal, & Kernan, 1981; Settle & Gorden, 1974; Ward & Brenner, 2006)

## Mechanisms

Sly sincerity works through a two-step process:

1. **Establishing trustworthiness**: By voluntarily revealing a weakness or arguing against self-interest on a minor point, the communicator signals that they are honest and not merely trying to manipulate. This passes the "How truthful can we expect the expert to be?" filter.
2. **Leveraging trust for persuasion**: Having established credibility through apparent honesty, the communicator's subsequent claims (which serve their interests) are accepted with less scrutiny. The audience has already classified the communicator as trustworthy.

The key structural element: the admitted weakness must be **secondary** — easily offset by the strengths that follow. The weakness is the investment; the strengths are the return.

## Applications

### Classic Advertising Examples
- **Avis**: "We're number two, but we try harder" — the weakness (second place) is minor; the implication (harder effort, better service) is the persuasive payload
- **L'Oreal**: "We're more expensive but you're worth it" — the weakness (higher price) is reframed as quality evidence

### The Waiter Vincent (from Cialdini's fieldwork)
The most successful waiter Cialdini observed used sly sincerity as his signature move:
- For large parties, when the first person ordered, Vincent would lean in conspiratorially and say: "I'm afraid that is not as good tonight as it normally is. Might I recommend instead the ___ or the ___?"
- He recommended items **slightly less expensive** than the original order
- This simultaneously established him as an expert (authority), an honest advisor (trustworthiness), and a benefactor (triggering [[reciprocation]])
- Result: near-universal assent when he later offered to select wine and persuaded diners to order expensive desserts
- Vincent inflated both his tip percentage and the base charge it was calculated on

### Career and Organizational Use
- Acknowledging a specific failure or weakness in a resume or presentation — then showing what was learned — can produce more favorable outcomes than presenting a flawless image
- Organizations that accept blame for poor outcomes earn more trust (and higher stock prices) than those that deflect

## Open Questions

- What are the boundary conditions? How large can the admitted weakness be before it backfires? Is there research on the optimal weakness-to-strength ratio?
- How does sly sincerity interact with audience sophistication? Do more educated or experienced audiences see through the tactic, or are they equally susceptible?
- Does the tactic lose effectiveness when the audience is explicitly aware of it (as with the "forewarning" defense against persuasion)?
