# Environmental Triggers

**Phase(s):** 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Environmental triggers are stimuli in the surrounding environment that prompt people to think about related products, ideas, or behaviors. Triggers work by making associated thoughts more accessible (top of mind), which in turn makes them more likely to be talked about (tip of tongue). Triggers are the second principle of [[berger]]'s [[stepps-framework]] and are the primary driver of *ongoing* word of mouth, as opposed to [[social-currency]] which primarily drives *immediate* word of mouth.

## Key Findings

- **Cheerios gets more word of mouth than Disney World** because it is more frequently triggered by everyday stimuli (breakfast, cereal aisles, morning routines). Disney World is more interesting but less frequently cued.
- **Interest, novelty, and surprise have no relationship to ongoing word of mouth.** BzzAgent data showed interesting products got more immediate buzz but not more sustained buzz. Triggers are the workhorse of ongoing contagiousness.
- **NASA's Mars Pathfinder mission boosted Mars candy bar sales** — an unintended trigger effect. The Mars company's sales jumped without any change in marketing.
- **French music in a supermarket increased French wine purchases.** German music increased German wine purchases (North, Hargreaves, & McKendrick). Environmental sounds triggered nationality-related thoughts that spilled into purchasing.
- **Dining-hall tray slogans changed eating behavior more than "live healthy" slogans** because trays served as daily triggers. Students rated the tray slogan as *less* appealing but ate 25% more fruits and vegetables.
- **Voting in schools increased support for school-funding initiatives** (Berger, Meredith, & Wheeler). The polling location triggered school-related thoughts that influenced voting.
- **Rebecca Black's "Friday" spiked in YouTube searches every Friday** — the day of the week was a recurring trigger.
- **Kit Kat and coffee linkage lifted sales by 8% within a year**, then by a third overall, growing the brand from $300M to $500M.
- **Negative reviews increased sales of unknown books by 45%** by triggering awareness (Sorensen, Rasmussen, & Berger).
- **More frequently triggered products got 15% more word of mouth** in BzzAgent data.

## Mechanisms

Triggers work through **cognitive accessibility** — the same mechanism underlying the [[availability]] heuristic. At any given moment, only certain thoughts are top of mind. Environmental stimuli (sights, smells, sounds, activities, contexts) shift which thoughts are accessible. Accessible thoughts lead to:
1. **Talking:** People talk about whatever is top of mind during casual conversations
2. **Choosing:** Accessible options are more likely to be selected
3. **Acting:** Triggered behaviors are more likely to be performed

### What Makes an Effective Trigger
1. **Frequency:** How often the trigger stimulus occurs in daily life. Coffee (multiple times daily) > hot chocolate (occasional, seasonal). Weekends (52/year) > holidays (a few/year).
2. **Strength of link:** The exclusivity and strength of the association. "Peanut butter" triggers "jelly" strongly. "Red" triggers too many things to be strong for any one product. Fewer, stronger links beat many weak ones.
3. **Proximity to desired behavior:** The trigger should fire where and when the behavior can happen. An antismoking message at a bar > at a health center. An antisoda PSA showing fat pouring from a can > a bath-mat PSA in a shower (where you can't buy anything).

### Growing the Habitat
Like biological habitats, products and ideas have "habitats" — sets of triggers that cause people to think about them. These can be:
- **Natural:** Mars bars and Mars the planet; hot dogs and barbecues/baseball games
- **Created:** Kit Kat and coffee (linked through a campaign); dining-hall trays and healthy eating (linked through a slogan); Boston Market and dinner (expanded from lunch association, increasing word of mouth by 20%)
- **Parasitic:** Hijacking a competitor's triggers for your own message ("Bob, I've got emphysema" on Marlboro ads)

## Applications

- **Message design:** Consider the context — what environmental cues exist in the target audience's daily life that can be linked to the product or idea?
- **Campaign strategy:** A strong trigger can be more effective than a catchy slogan. The tray slogan was less appealing but more effective than the "live healthy" slogan.
- **Timing:** The more the desired behavior happens after a delay (not at the point of sale), the more important being triggered becomes.
- **Public health:** Place messages where the behavior happens, not where the audience is most receptive. Antismoking messages near bars, not health centers.
- **Negative publicity:** Even bad press can boost sales if it triggers awareness of an otherwise forgotten product.
