# Romeo and Juliet Effect

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Romeo and Juliet effect is the phenomenon whereby external interference in a romantic relationship — particularly parental opposition — intensifies rather than diminishes the partners' romantic feelings for each other. Named after Shakespeare's lovers, whose passion may have been fueled rather than merely undeterred by the families' feud, the effect is a direct application of [[psychological-reactance]] to romantic relationships.

## Key Findings

- **Colorado teenage couples study** (Driscoll, Davis, & Lipetz, 1972): Among 140 teenage couples, parental interference was linked to some relationship problems (partners viewed each other more critically, reported more negative behaviors) but simultaneously made pairs feel **greater love for each other and greater desire for marriage**. As interference intensified over the course of the study, so did the love experience. When interference weakened, romantic feelings actually *cooled*.
- **Reader's Report 7.2** (Cialdini): A 19-year-old woman dated a 27-year-old man she "really wasn't interested in" — until her parents expressed concern about his age. "The more they got on my case about it, the more in love I became." The relationship lasted five months, "about four months longer than it would have lasted if my parents hadn't said anything."
- The effect should not be interpreted as a reason for parents to always accept teenagers' romantic choices. Parents should recognize that teenagers see themselves as young adults who will not respond well to control attempts typical of parent-child relationships. In the arena of mating, adult tools of influence (preference and persuasion) will be more effective than traditional forms of parental control (prohibitions and punishments).
- Heavy-handed restrictions on a young romantic alliance "may well turn it clandestine, torrid, and sad."

## Mechanisms

1. **[[psychological-reactance]]**: Parental opposition restricts the teenager's perceived freedom of romantic choice. This triggers a motivational state driving them to reassert that freedom by committing more deeply to the forbidden relationship.
2. **Desire-merit confusion**: The increased desire triggered by reactance is misattributed to the qualities of the partner and the relationship. The teenager believes they are more in love; they do not recognize that parental interference is inflating their feelings.
3. **Identity assertion**: For teenagers developing an adult identity, resisting parental control in the explicitly adult arena of mating has symbolic significance — it is a declaration of autonomy.

## Applications

- **Parenting**: Avoid heavy-handed prohibitions of teenage romance. Use preference and persuasion rather than prohibition and punishment. Provide guidance from the perspective of an adult with greater experience, rather than attempting to control.
- **Relationship awareness**: Individuals experiencing intensified feelings for a partner in the face of external opposition should consider whether the opposition itself is the cause of the intensity.

## Open Questions

- Does the Romeo and Juliet effect hold across cultures with varying levels of parental authority in mate selection (e.g., societies with arranged marriages)?
- What is the long-term outcome for relationships that were intensified by the Romeo and Juliet effect — do they persist after the external opposition is removed, or do they fade as predicted by reactance theory?
