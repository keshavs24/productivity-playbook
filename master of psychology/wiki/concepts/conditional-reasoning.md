# Conditional Reasoning

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Conditional reasoning is reasoning about "if-then" statements (conditionals). Formal logic prescribes two valid inference rules -- **modus ponens** (if A then B; A is true; therefore B) and **modus tollens** (if A then B; B is false; therefore A is false) -- and identifies two common fallacies: **affirmation of the consequent** and **denial of the antecedent**. Research consistently shows that people handle modus ponens well but struggle with modus tollens and frequently accept the two fallacies.

## Key Findings

- Evans (1993) review: modus ponens accepted ~98% of the time; modus tollens only ~62%; affirmation of the consequent ~42%; denial of the antecedent ~42% ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch10]])
- People interpret conditionals in at least four different ways depending on context: (1) logical, (2) causal, (3) probabilistic, (4) permission-based -- each activating different brain regions and producing different reasoning patterns
- Causal interpretation: when "if A then B" is read causally, denial of the antecedent becomes a reasonable inference rather than a fallacy (Weidenfeld, Oberauer, & Hornig, 2005; Sloman & Lagnado, 2005)
- Real-world knowledge modulates even valid inferences: people reject modus ponens ("If fertilizer, then plants grow quickly") when they can think of other necessary conditions (Cummins et al., 1991)
- Training in formal logic produces almost no transfer: a semester-long logic course improved Wason task performance by only 3% (Cheng, Holyoak, Nisbett, & Oliver, 1986)

## Mechanisms

People naturally interpret "if" in terms of real-world causal and probabilistic relationships rather than as a logical operator. When reasoning about abstract conditionals, parietal brain regions (associated with algebraic processing) are active; when reasoning about meaningful content, left ventral prefrontal and temporal-parietal language regions are active (Goel et al., 2000). This suggests different neural circuits underlie formal vs. content-based reasoning. The difficulty with modus tollens may reflect the greater cognitive demand of reasoning "backward" from the consequent to the antecedent.

## Applications

- **Critical thinking:** Understanding the four inference patterns helps identify common reasoning errors in arguments, persuasion, and everyday logic
- **Causal reasoning in practice:** Recognizing that people interpret conditionals causally explains why logical fallacies feel compelling in real-world contexts -- the "logic" people use is actually sensible causal reasoning
- **Scientific reasoning:** Scientists must learn to apply modus tollens (falsification) deliberately because it does not come naturally
