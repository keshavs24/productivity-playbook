# Implicit Bias

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition
Implicit biases are unconscious attitudes and stereotypes that automatically activate when we encounter others, influencing our judgments and behaviors without conscious awareness or intent. Unlike explicit prejudice, implicit bias operates below the threshold of awareness and often contradicts a person's consciously held egalitarian values. The primary measurement tool is the **Implicit Association Test (IAT)**, developed by Greenwald, McGhee, and Schwartz (1998).

## Key Findings
- When we meet someone, our minds automatically activate stereotypes based on race, sex, and age (Macrae & Bodenhausen, 2001) -- this activation is involuntary
- **Bargh, Chen, and Burrows (1996):** Subliminal exposure to black faces (too fast for conscious awareness) increased aggression on a subsequent unrelated task, consistent with the stereotype of African Americans as aggressive
- **The IAT:** Measures relative speed of associating paired categories (e.g., White + Good vs. Black + Good). Nosek, Banaji, and Greenwald (2002) found ~75% of white Americans who take the IAT show pro-white implicit attitudes
- **Predictive validity:**
  - Rudman and Borgida (1995): Implicit stereotypes predicted discrimination against female job applicants
  - Rudman and Glick (2001): People with strong implicit associations between women and communal traits were more likely to view ambitious women as having poor social skills
  - McConnell and Leibold (2001): Implicit attitudes were highly predictive of nonverbal behaviors toward different groups
  - Asendorpf, Banse, and Muecke (2002): Implicit attitudes predict spontaneous behaviors; explicit attitudes predict deliberate behaviors
- **Implicit bias predicts System 1 behavior:** When people use fast, automatic processing, implicit attitudes drive behavior. When they engage [[system-1-system-2|System 2]] deliberation, explicit attitudes are more influential.
- **Richeson and Shelton (2005):** People whose automatically activated stereotypes are most negative suffer the most discomfort in interracial interactions. The effort of suppressing stereotypes produces measurable cognitive impairments on other tasks (Richeson & Shelton, 2003).
- **Blanton and Jaccard (2006):** The IAT reveals relative strength of implicit attitudes, not absolute attitudes. A pro-white IAT result means slightly more positive feelings toward white people, not necessarily hostility toward black people.
- **Correll et al. (2007):** In a computer simulation, participants instructed to shoot criminals (but not unarmed citizens or police) incorrectly shot more unarmed black men than white men
- Banaji (2001): Calls implicit attitudes "ordinary prejudice" because they are rooted in ordinary mental processes of categorization, perception, memory, and judgment
- There is evidence that societal change can reduce implicit bias: Plant et al. (2009) found implicit attitudes toward black people became less prejudiced after Obama's election

## Mechanisms
Implicit biases arise from:
1. **Categorization:** The brain's natural tendency to sort people into groups
2. **Cultural exposure:** Lifetime exposure to cultural stereotypes creates automatic associations
3. **Memory processes:** Stereotype-consistent information is encoded and retrieved more easily

These biases influence behavior primarily through:
- Shaping what information is immediately accessible in social encounters
- Interacting with situational demands (time pressure, cognitive load) to produce biased snap judgments
- Operating most powerfully when there is insufficient time or motivation for System 2 deliberation

## Applications
- **Hiring and promotion:** Structure interviews and evaluations to reduce reliance on snap judgments; use standardized criteria
- **Law enforcement:** Time pressure and threat perception magnify implicit bias; training on bias awareness and de-escalation may help
- **Self-assessment:** Take the IAT (implicit.harvard.edu) to understand your own implicit associations -- awareness is the first step, though not sufficient on its own
- **Organizational policy:** Create structural accountability rather than relying on individual bias awareness
- **Decision slowing:** When implicit bias is likely relevant, deliberately slow down to engage System 2

## Open Questions
- Can implicit bias be durably reduced through training, or only through structural changes?
- How do implicit biases interact with explicit commitment to fairness in high-stakes decisions?
- What is the causal direction: do implicit biases cause discriminatory behavior, or do they merely correlate with it?
