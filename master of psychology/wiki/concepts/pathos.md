# Pathos

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Pathos is persuasion through the emotions of the audience. According to [[aristotle]] in [[on-rhetoric]], pathos is one of the [[three-modes-of-persuasion]] (alongside [[ethos]] and [[logos]]). It operates "through the hearers when they are led to feel emotion by the speech; for we do not give the same judgment when grieved and rejoicing or when being friendly and hostile" (1.2.5).

Aristotle's inclusion of emotion as a legitimate mode of persuasion is significant. In Book 1, Chapter 1, he initially seems to reject emotional appeals as improper — things "external to the subject" that "warp the jury." But by Chapter 2, he rehabilitates emotion as one of three essential artistic proofs, recognizing that human judgment is inescapably shaped by emotional states.

## Key Findings

- **Emotions are defined as temporary states of mind** that alter judgment and are accompanied by pain and pleasure (2.1.8)
- For each emotion, a speaker must understand three things: (a) the *state of mind* of those who feel it, (b) the *persons* toward whom it is directed, and (c) the *causes/reasons* for it — without all three, one cannot arouse the emotion (2.1.9)
- Aristotle catalogs ten emotion-pairs in Book 2: anger/calmness, friendliness/enmity, fear/confidence, shame/shamelessness, kindliness/unkindliness, pity/indignation, envy/emulation (see [[rhetorical-emotions]])
- Emotions are cognitive phenomena in Aristotle's account — they arise from perceptions and judgments about one's situation, not merely from physiological states
- The practical function: a speaker can put the audience into a state of mind favorable to the desired judgment by using the specific triggers Aristotle identifies for each emotion

## Mechanisms

Pathos works because emotions function as a cognitive frame — they change how the same information is evaluated. An angry audience and a sympathetic audience will reach different judgments on identical evidence. The mechanism is:

1. **Emotional state alters judgment criteria.** A person who is angry applies harsher standards; one who is friendly applies more lenient ones.
2. **Emotions arise from specific cognitive appraisals.** Anger arises from perceived slighting; fear from imagined future harm; pity from witnessing undeserved suffering. A speaker can trigger these appraisals by presenting facts in the right frame.
3. **Temporal proximity matters.** Emotions are stronger when their object seems near — fear requires that danger appear close at hand, not remote (2.5.1).

This mechanism parallels modern research on [[framing-effects]] — the same information, framed differently, produces different judgments because it activates different emotional responses.

## Applications

- A deliberative speaker arouses fear to motivate caution, or confidence to motivate action
- A judicial speaker arouses anger against the opponent (by showing the opponent acted from contempt, spite, or insult) or pity for the client
- An epideictic speaker arouses admiration and emulation
- The speaker should also know how to *calm* emotions — calmness techniques (2.3) are as important as arousal techniques
- Understanding audience character types (young, old, wealthy, powerful) helps predict which emotions are most easily activated

## Open Questions

- Aristotle's analysis of emotions is strikingly cognitive — each emotion involves a judgment or appraisal. This aligns with modern appraisal theories of emotion. How does this compare with the more physiological accounts in [[emotional-intelligence]]?
- The relationship between pathos and ethos is complex: displaying emotion can enhance or damage ethos depending on context. When does emotional expression strengthen credibility vs. undermine it?
