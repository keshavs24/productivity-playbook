# Similarity and Liking

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The similarity-and-liking effect is the robust finding that we like people who are similar to us — whether the similarity is in opinions, personality traits, background, lifestyle, dress, or even name. Because we like similar others, we are more willing to comply with their requests, often in an automatic, unthinking manner. This makes similarity a powerful tool for compliance professionals, who manufacture or highlight similarities to increase liking and thus compliance.

## Key Findings

- **Dress similarity:** When experimenters were dressed the same way as students (hippie or straight), requests for a dime were granted two-thirds of the time; dissimilar dress dropped compliance below half (Emswiller, Deaux, & Willits, 1971)
- **Petition signing:** Antiwar marchers were more likely to sign a similarly dressed requester's petition — and to do so without reading it first (Suedfeld, Bochner, & Matas, 1971). A [[click-whirr]] response
- **Insurance sales:** Customers were more likely to buy from salespeople similar in age, religion, politics, and smoking habits (Evans, 1963; Burger et al., 2004)
- **Name similarity:** Modifying a cover letter so the survey-taker's name resembled the recipient's nearly doubled compliance in two studies (Garner, 2005)
- **Life-and-death decisions:** People rank-ordering kidney patients for treatment chose those whose political party matched their own (Furnham, 1996)
- **Marriage partner choice:** Even minor commonalities affect partner selection (Jones et al., 2004)
- **Mirroring:** Matching body posture, mood, and verbal style produces positive results (Chartrand & Bargh, 1999; Locke & Horowitz, 1990; van Baaren et al., 2003)
- **Underestimation:** We typically underestimate how much similarity affects our liking (Gonzales, Davis, Loney, Lukens, & Junghans, 1983)

## Mechanisms

Similarity produces liking through several possible pathways:

1. **Validation:** Similar others validate our own beliefs and choices, which feels rewarding
2. **Predictability:** Similar people are easier to predict and thus feel safer
3. **Automatic association:** Similarity may trigger an automatic positive response — a [[click-whirr]] reaction

The effect can be manufactured trivially. Car salespeople are trained to scan trade-ins for evidence of customer interests (camping gear, golf balls, out-of-state plates) and then claim matching interests. Sales training programs urge trainees to "mirror and match" the customer's body posture, mood, and verbal style.

Because even small similarities can be effective and a veneer of similarity is easily manufactured, Cialdini advises special caution around requesters who claim to be "just like you."

## Applications

- **Sales:** Car salespeople, insurance agents, and retail staff are trained to find and highlight commonalities
- **Surveys:** Matching the survey-taker's name to the recipient's name nearly doubles response rates
- **Negotiation:** Mirroring posture, mood, and verbal style creates rapport
- **Marketing:** Advertisers select spokespeople who resemble target demographics
- **Social engineering:** Any compliance attempt benefits from establishing perceived similarity

## Open Questions

- What is the minimum threshold of similarity needed to trigger a compliance effect? The name-similarity finding (Garner, 2005) suggests it can be remarkably small
- How does the effect interact with in-group/out-group dynamics? Does similarity only increase liking within one's perceived in-group?
- In an increasingly polarized social landscape, does the similarity effect contribute to echo chambers and tribal thinking?
