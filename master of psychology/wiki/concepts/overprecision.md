# Overprecision

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Overprecision is the tendency to be **too sure that our judgments and decisions are accurate** -- to draw overly narrow confidence intervals and to be too certain that we know the truth. It is the first and most robust of the three forms of overconfidence identified by Bazerman and Moore (the others being [[overestimation]] and [[overplacement]]).

## Key Findings

- When asked for 98% confidence intervals on 10 quantities, people typically bound only 30-70% of the true values ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch2]])
- Alpert and Raiffa (1969/1982): in 1,000 observations, 42.6% of quantities fell outside participants' 90% confidence ranges
- McKenzie, Liersch, and Yaniv (2008): experts in their own domains specified narrower confidence intervals but were equally overprecise -- hit rates did not increase
- Ben-David, Graham, and Harvey (2010): financial officers' 80% confidence intervals for market returns captured actual returns only 33% of the time -- systematically overprecise rather than systematically optimistic or pessimistic
- Mannes and Moore (2012): people act as if they are sure they know the truth, drawing bull's-eyes too small and confidence intervals too narrow, not shifting actions enough in the face of uncertainty
- Henrion and Fischhoff (1986): scientists' confidence intervals for physical constants (speed of light, Planck's constant) were too narrow -- the majority failed to include the true value
- Korean Air flight 801, Flash Airlines Flight 604, Costa Concordia -- overprecise pilots/captains who were "sure" they knew what they were doing contributed to disasters

## Mechanisms

Three causal theories: (1) **Internal dissonance relief** -- people want to resolve the tension of uncertainty and prematurely commit to a single answer (Herzog & Hertwig, 2009 found that asking for a second estimate improved accuracy -- the "crowd within"); (2) **Social rewards for confidence** -- confident people are seen as more persuasive, capable, and are elevated to leadership positions (Sniezek & Van Sol, 2001; Radzevick & Moore, 2011); (3) **Confirmation bias** -- the mind is better at generating supportive evidence than contradictory evidence (Klayman & Ha, 1987), so available evidence bolsters our beliefs. Additionally, we almost never have the experience of believing something we know to be false -- the moment we learn we were wrong, we adopt the new belief, creating the illusion that we are right all the time (Schulz, 2010).

## Applications

- **Forecasting:** Organizations should widen confidence intervals dramatically; use historical base rates of forecast accuracy to calibrate
- **Advice taking:** Overprecision makes us too reluctant to take advice; we give substantially less weight to others' advice than to our own opinions (Minson, Liberman, & Ross, 2009; Yaniv & Kleinberger, 2000)
- **Investing:** Overprecision drives excessive stock trading -- investors think their beliefs are more accurate than others', increasing willingness to trade (Odean, 1998; Barber & Odean, 2000)
- **Hiring:** Managers make overly precise forecasts of candidates' potential based on interviews, despite evidence that interviews poorly predict performance (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998)
- **Negotiation:** Naive realism (Ross & Ward, 1996) -- believing our perspective is the only sensible one -- impedes mutual understanding and agreement
