# Need Satisfaction at Work

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Need satisfaction at work refers to the degree to which the three basic psychological needs posited by [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]] -- autonomy, competence, and relatedness -- are fulfilled in the work environment. SDT identifies need satisfaction as the **universal mediating mechanism** linking organizational practices, managerial styles, and job characteristics to employee outcomes including engagement, performance, well-being, satisfaction, commitment, and turnover.

This concept has been empirically validated across an extraordinary range of work settings: shoe factories, psychiatric sheltered workshops, United Nations agencies, Canadian business school alumni, American banks, nonprofit volunteer organizations, post-communist Bulgarian state-owned companies, police forces, schools, hospitals, and insurance companies.

## Key Findings

### The Core Model
Perceived [[autonomy-supportive-management|autonomy support from managers]] + employee [[causality-orientations-theory|autonomy orientation]] --> satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs --> positive work outcomes (engagement, performance, well-being, satisfaction) and reduced negative outcomes (burnout, exhaustion, turnover)

### Demonstrations Across Settings

- **Ilardi, Leone, Kasser, & Ryan (1993) -- Shoe factory:** Need satisfaction predicted general job satisfaction, satisfaction with specific tasks, self-esteem, and general health symptoms, **even after controlling for pay level and job status**. Both self-report and supervisor ratings confirmed
- **Kasser, Davey, & Ryan (1992) -- Sheltered workshop:** Psychiatric outpatients' satisfaction of competence, autonomy, and relatedness predicted work adjustment (hours worked, pay earned, readiness for competitive employment), whether rated by patients or supervisors
- **Roca & Gagne (2008) -- United Nations:** Competence predicted perceived usefulness; autonomy support and relatedness predicted enjoyment of e-learning; both predicted intentions to continue
- **Richer, Blanchard, & Vallerand (2002) -- Business school alumni (N=500):** Perceived autonomy predicted job satisfaction (positively) and emotional exhaustion (negatively). Turnover intentions predicted actual turnover one year later. Full structural model supported
- **Baard, Deci, & Ryan (2004) -- Bankers:** Both autonomy orientation and perceived autonomy support predicted need satisfaction; need satisfaction predicted work performance and well-being (low anxiety, low physical symptoms, high vitality)
- **Gagne (2003) -- Nonprofit volunteers:** Autonomy orientation + perceived autonomy support predicted need satisfaction and engagement (hours volunteered)
- **Deci, Ryan, Gagne, Leone, Usunov, & Kornazheva (2001) -- Bulgaria and US:** Model was **largely invariant** across these two very different countries and economic systems

### Need Satisfaction Mediates Key Relationships

- **Job demands to exhaustion/depersonalization:** Need satisfaction mediates (Fernet, Austin, Trepanier, & Dussault, 2013)
- **Job resources to engagement:** Need satisfaction mediates (Van den Broeck, Vansteenkiste, De Witte, & Lens, 2008)
- **Low job resources to exhaustion:** Need satisfaction fully mediates (Van den Broeck et al., 2008)
- **Extrinsic vs. intrinsic goal contents to well-being outcomes:** Need satisfaction mediates (Vansteenkiste, Neyrinck, et al., 2007)
- **Transformational leadership to performance:** Need satisfaction mediates (Kovjanic, Schuh, & Jonas, 2013), with competence and relatedness emerging as strongest mediators

### Need Satisfaction vs. Pay

- **Olafsen, Halvari, Forest, & Deci (2015):** Pay level related to distributive justice only. **Pay level did not relate to procedural justice, need satisfaction, or intrinsic motivation.** Autonomy support was strongly related to all three
- **Ilardi et al. (1993):** Need satisfaction predicted outcomes even after controlling for pay and job status

## Mechanisms

1. **Autonomy satisfaction:** Experiencing volition, choice, and endorsement of one's work activities. Supported by [[autonomy-supportive-management]], participative decision making, meaningful rationale
2. **Competence satisfaction:** Feeling effective and capable in work tasks. Supported by optimal challenge, informational feedback, skill development opportunities
3. **Relatedness satisfaction:** Feeling connected, cared for, and significant to colleagues and the organization. Supported by inclusive leadership, team cohesion, and work interdependence

When all three needs are satisfied, employees experience [[autonomous-work-motivation]], which drives [[work-engagement-sdt|engagement]], persistence, creativity, and well-being. When needs are **frustrated** (not merely unsatisfied but actively thwarted), the consequences are particularly negative: burnout, workplace bullying, turnover, and ill-being (Trepanier, Fernet, & Austin, 2013a).

## Applications

- **Job design:** Structure jobs to provide autonomy (how tasks are done), competence feedback (clear progress indicators), and relatedness (collaboration, team membership, contact with beneficiaries)
- **Management development:** Train managers in [[autonomy-supportive-management]] to create need-supportive climates
- **Organizational assessment:** Measure need satisfaction as a leading indicator of engagement, performance, and retention -- it is more predictive than pay satisfaction
- **Change management:** Ensure that organizational changes are implemented in ways that support rather than thwart basic needs
- **Diversity and inclusion:** Address gender and minority disparities in need satisfaction (Baard et al., 2004 found women in banking perceived less autonomy support and relatedness)

## Open Questions

- How does remote/hybrid work affect need satisfaction, particularly relatedness?

- What organizational-level interventions (beyond individual manager training) most effectively improve need satisfaction?
