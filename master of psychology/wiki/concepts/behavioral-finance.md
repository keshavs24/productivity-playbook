# Behavioral Finance

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Behavioral finance is the application of cognitive psychology and decision science to the study of financial markets and investment decisions. It examines how the systematic biases documented in judgment and decision-making research (overconfidence, [[loss-aversion]], [[anchoring-and-adjustment]], [[availability]], etc.) lead individuals and markets to deviate from the predictions of traditional rational economic models. While classical finance assumes rational agents and efficient markets, behavioral finance describes how actual investors behave and why markets exhibit predictable anomalies.

## Key Findings

- **Active fund underperformance:** The Vanguard Index 500 outperforms ~75% of actively managed mutual funds each year. Only 18% of hedge funds outperform benchmarks (Kat & Palaro, 2006). The "two and twenty" fee structure consumes most of any hedge fund advantage. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch10]])
- **Overconfidence and excessive trading (Barber & Odean, 2000):** 66,465 household accounts showed average portfolio turnover of 75% annually. The most active traders earned 6.5 percentage points less than the market. Men traded more than women (77% vs. 53% turnover) and earned worse returns due to overconfidence.
- **Optimistic illusions (Moore et al., 1999):** In investment simulations, participants predicted 8.13% growth per half-year while achieving 5.50%. At the end, they believed they matched the market while underperforming by 8%.
- **[[disposition-effect]] (Odean, 1998):** Investors sell winners too early and hold losers too long, a direct consequence of [[prospect-theory]]
- **Status quo bias in investing (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988; Benartzi & Thaler, 2001):** Investors use naive diversification strategies, rarely adjust allocations, and procrastinate on major financial decisions while being overactive in minor ones

## Mechanisms

Behavioral finance identifies how each major cognitive bias manifests in financial decisions:
1. **[[overconfidence-in-calibration]]** -> excessive trading, daytrading
2. **Optimism** -> inflated return expectations, failure to compare against benchmarks
3. **[[gamblers-fallacy]] / pattern-seeking** -> belief in "hot" funds, denial of regression to the mean
4. **[[anchoring-and-adjustment]]** -> naive diversification based on category offerings; status quo maintenance
5. **[[prospect-theory]] / [[loss-aversion]]** -> disposition effect, risk-seeking with losers

## Applications

- **Personal finance:** Invest in low-cost index funds, formulate a long-term asset allocation plan, and minimize trading activity
- **Organizational investing:** Implement automatic enrollment in retirement plans with sensible defaults (Madrian & Shea, 2001: enrollment jumped from 49% to 86%)
- **Financial advisory:** Advisors should help clients recognize and counteract their biases rather than exploiting them for fees
- **Market design:** Design auction and trading mechanisms that account for predictable bidder biases

## Camerer's Field Evidence (Ch. 16, CVF)

Camerer ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch16-pt-in-the-wild]]) provides systematic field evidence connecting prospect theory to finance:

- **[[equity-premium-puzzle]]:** Stocks returned ~8% more per year than bonds. Benartzi and Thaler (1995/1997) explain this with "myopic loss aversion" -- investors who evaluate portfolios annually and are loss-averse (coefficient ~2.25) find the prospect values of stock and bond returns roughly equal at the observed premium. Barberis, Huang, and Santos (1999) incorporated loss aversion into a general equilibrium model, adding a "house money effect."
- **[[disposition-effect]] field data (Odean):** Brokerage data showed investors held losers a median of 124 days vs. 104 for winners. Unsold losers returned only 5% the next year while sold winners returned 11.6%. The effect disappears in December due to tax-loss selling. Genovese and Meyer found the same pattern in housing sales.
- **Favorite-longshot bias in horse racing:** Jullien and Salanie (1997) used 34,443 races to show cumulative prospect theory fits much better than EU or rank-dependent utility. Loss probability weighting severely overweights small probabilities (pi(0.1) = 0.45).
- **End-of-day effect:** Bettors shift toward longshots late in the day to cover earlier losses -- reflection effect (risk seeking in losses) with daily mental accounting.
