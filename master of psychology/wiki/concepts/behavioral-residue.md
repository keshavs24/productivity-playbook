# Behavioral Residue

**Phase(s):** 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Behavioral residue is the physical traces or remnants that actions and behaviors leave in their wake. While behaviors themselves are often temporary (a purchase, a vote, a brand interaction), behavioral residue persists — creating ongoing observable evidence that continues to generate [[social-proof]] and word of mouth long after the initial action. The concept is central to the "Public" principle of [[berger]]'s [[stepps-framework]]: "Built to show, built to grow."

## Key Findings

- **Livestrong wristbands** created massive behavioral residue. Over 85 million were sold. Yellow was chosen because it stands out against almost any clothing — maximizing observability. As Nike's Scott MacEachern noted: "The nice thing about a wristband is that it lives on. The bike ride doesn't."
- **Hotmail's email signature** ("Get Your Private, Free E-mail from Hotmail") turned every email into an advertisement. Hotmail grew to 8.5+ million subscribers in just over a year and was acquired by Microsoft for $400 million.
- **Apple's white headphones** made iPod usage publicly observable when most devices came with black headphones, creating visible [[social-proof]] for the product.
- **"I Voted" stickers** transformed the private act of voting into a public signal, providing behavioral residue that reminds others to vote.
- **Louboutin's red-lacquered soles** made the brand instantly recognizable even to people who know nothing about fashion.
- **Facebook Like buttons** boosted ABC News traffic by 250% by creating behavioral residue (visible endorsements) that other users could see.

## Mechanisms

Behavioral residue works through three mechanisms:

1. **Ongoing social proof.** The residue continues to signal that people are using/supporting something even when they aren't actively doing so. A Lululemon bag used to carry groceries advertises the brand every day.
2. **Triggering.** Visible residue acts as an [[environmental-triggers|environmental trigger]], reminding people of the product, idea, or behavior.
3. **Facilitating imitation.** If you can't see what others are choosing or doing, you can't imitate them. Behavioral residue makes private choices public and therefore imitable.

### Products That Advertise Themselves
Many products create behavioral residue by being publicly visible during use:
- Apple's logo faces outward when the laptop is open (Jobs flipped it for observability)
- iPod's white headphones stand out in a sea of black
- Louboutin's red soles are visible with every step
- Pringles' unique tube shape, Microsoft's boot-up sound

### Designed Behavioral Residue
When products aren't naturally visible, companies can create residue:
- **Shopping bags:** Tiffany, Victoria's Secret, Lululemon bags are reused as status symbols
- **Giveaway swag:** Conference mugs, pens, T-shirts, stress balls — coffee mugs and gym bags provide more behavioral residue than makeup cases (which are used privately)
- **Digital residue:** Spotify publishing listening activity, Facebook posting "liked" content, email signatures ("Sent from my iPhone")
- **Stickers and badges:** Bumper stickers, laptop stickers, Foursquare badges

### Making the Private Public
Many products, ideas, and behaviors are consumed privately — preferences, beliefs, votes, purchases. Making these visible is a powerful strategy:
- **Movember** took private cancer donations and made them visible through moustaches
- **"I Voted" stickers** took private voting and made it visible
- **Livestrong wristbands** took private charitable donations and made them visible

### When Making Things Public Backfires
Anti-drug PSAs inadvertently made drug use seem common by showing lots of kids being offered drugs. The ads communicated that drug use was widespread, triggering [[social-proof]] in the wrong direction. Similarly, the music industry's warning that "only 37% of music was paid for" essentially told people that piracy is the norm.

**The solution:** When trying to *prevent* behavior, don't make the unwanted behavior more visible. Instead, highlight the desired behavior. Koreen Johannessen reduced binge drinking by 30% by publicizing that *most* students only have one or two drinks — making the true norm (moderation) visible.

Cialdini's Petrified Forest study confirmed this: signs saying "many visitors have removed wood" doubled theft, while signs saying "please don't remove wood to preserve the natural state" reduced it.

## Applications

- **Product design:** Design products that are publicly visible during and after use
- **Brand strategy:** Create distinctive visual markers (colors, shapes, sounds) that make brand usage observable
- **Nonprofit and cause marketing:** Find ways to take abstract, private support and make it physically visible (wristbands, stickers, moustaches)
- **Digital marketing:** Add shareable markers (signatures, badges, sharing widgets) that create digital behavioral residue
- **Behavior change:** When promoting good behavior, make it visible. When preventing bad behavior, make the good behavior (the actual norm) visible instead.
