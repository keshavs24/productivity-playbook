# Change Blindness

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Change blindness is the failure to **notice obvious visual changes in one's physical environment**, even when those changes occur in full view. Unlike [[inattentional-blindness]] (missing unexpected stimuli while focused on a task), change blindness involves failing to detect alterations to existing stimuli (Simons, 2000).

## Key Findings

- Simons, Chabris, Schnur, and Levin (2002): while an experimenter with a basketball asked a pedestrian for directions, a group walked between them and surreptitiously removed the basketball -- most pedestrians did not notice ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch4]])
- Angelone, Levin, and Simons (2003): viewers failed to notice clothing and object changes during camera cuts in video
- Mitroff, Simons, and Franconeri (2002): people had implicit representations of both pre- and post-change states but failed to explicitly notice the change -- they perceived it at some level but screened it from conscious awareness
- **Gradual changes are harder to detect** than sudden ones (Simons & Rensink, 2005) -- this has critical implications for ethical monitoring
- **The "slippery slope" of unethical behavior** (Gino & Bazerman, 2009): when a corporation gradually stretches ethical standards year over year, auditors and employees are less likely to notice than if the same total change occurred suddenly. People are more willing to accept ethical lapses in several small steps than in one large step (Tenbrunsel & Messick, 2004)
- The boiling frog metaphor: gradual ethical degradation escapes notice just as a frog supposedly fails to notice gradually heated water

## Mechanisms

Change blindness occurs because our visual representations of the environment are sparser and less detailed than we assume. We do not maintain a complete, pixel-perfect mental model of our visual field. Instead, we construct a partial representation focused on the objects and features most relevant to our current goals. When changes occur outside this focus -- or gradually enough to avoid triggering a change-detection signal -- they go unnoticed.

## Applications

- **Ethical monitoring:** Recognize that gradual ethical degradation is harder to detect than sudden violations. Design systems that measure absolute ethical standards rather than relying on noticing changes
- **Auditing:** Begin each audit cycle with the self-critical assumption that ethical lapses may have occurred; actively search for them using the confirmation heuristic in reverse
- **Organizational change:** Gradual deterioration in culture, quality, or standards may go unnoticed; periodic "from scratch" assessments can reveal accumulated drift
- **Personal development:** Periodically reassess habits, relationships, and commitments from a fresh baseline rather than relying on noticing gradual changes
