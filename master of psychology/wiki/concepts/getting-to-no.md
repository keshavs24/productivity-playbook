# Getting to No

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Getting to No is [[voss|Chris Voss]]'s principle that "No" is a more valuable word than "Yes" in negotiation. Contra the conventional wisdom (and the title of Fisher and Ury's *Getting to Yes*), Voss argues that skilled negotiators should **seek** "No" early in a negotiation rather than fearing it. "No" is not rejection -- it is a reaffirmation of [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], a declaration of safety, and the true start of negotiation.

The principle rests on the observation that there are three kinds of "Yes":
1. **Counterfeit "yes"** -- the counterpart plans to say "no" but says "yes" as an easier escape route or to keep the conversation going
2. **Confirmation "yes"** -- a reflexive affirmation with no promise of action ("Yes, the sky is blue")
3. **Commitment "yes"** -- a true agreement that leads to action, ending with a signature on the contract

Most "yeses" in negotiation are counterfeit. People worldwide have become masters at giving counterfeit "yes" to avoid confrontation. Pushing hard for "yes" triggers defensiveness and wariness. "No" cuts through to what is real.

## Key Findings

- **Ben Ottenhoff's fundraising experiment:** A "No"-oriented fundraising script ("Do you feel that if things stay the way they are, America's best days are ahead of it?" -- designed to elicit "No") produced a **23% better rate of return** than the traditional "Yes"-pattern script
- **Marti Evelsizer (FBI Pittsburgh):** Facing dismissal, she asked her jealous boss: "Do you want the FBI to be embarrassed?" His "No" put him in the driver's seat; he reversed course and let her keep the position
- **Mark Cuban:** "Every 'No' gets me closer to a 'Yes.'"
- **Jim Camp (*Start with NO*):** Counsels giving your counterpart "the right to veto" from the outset. When people have permission to say "No," the negotiating environment becomes constructive and collaborative almost immediately
- **"Have you given up on this project?"** email -- a one-sentence "No"-oriented provocation that plays on [[loss-aversion]] to force re-engagement from someone who's been ignoring you

## Mechanisms

1. **Autonomy preservation:** "No" satisfies the deep, universal human need for [[autonomy-need|autonomy]]. People need to feel in control. When you give them permission to say "No," emotions calm, decision effectiveness increases, and they can genuinely evaluate your proposal.
2. **Safety and control:** People are driven by two primal urges -- the need to feel safe and the need to feel in control. "No" satisfies both. "Yes" under pressure satisfies neither.
3. **Loss aversion activation:** "No"-oriented questions ("Have you given up on this project?") activate [[loss-aversion]] -- the counterpart feels the threat of losing the relationship or opportunity and is compelled to re-engage to prevent that loss.
4. **Status quo protection:** "No" often means "I am not yet ready to agree" or "I need to maintain the status quo for now." It provides a temporary oasis of control. Change is scary; "No" provides protection from that scariness. Once protected, people can relax and consider possibilities.
5. **Counterintuitive engagement:** Saying "No" often *opens* the discussion and spurs action. People who say "No" feel they've protected themselves and now see an opportunity slipping away.

## Applications

**Practical techniques:**
- **"Is now a bad time to talk?"** -- always better than "Do you have a few minutes to talk?" The former invites "No" (giving safety); the latter pushes for "yes" (triggering wariness)
- **"No"-oriented question design:** Frame questions so that "No" is the honest answer that moves the negotiation forward
- **Mislabeling to force "No":** Say something you know is wrong ("So it seems like you really are eager to leave your job") to compel a corrective "No" that reveals their true position
- **Ask what they don't want:** "Let's talk about what you would say 'No' to"
- **The walk-away:** "Fine, I'm leaving" -- triggers "No, wait!" from people who don't want to be abandoned
- **"No" as diagnostic:** If the other party won't say "No" despite all efforts, you may be dealing with someone who is indecisive, confused, or has a hidden agenda. No "No" = no go.

**After hearing "No":**
- Pause
- Ask solution-based questions: "What about this doesn't work for you?" / "What would you need to make it work?"
- Or label their effect: "It seems like there's something here that bothers you"
- Rethink "No" as one of its alternative meanings: "I am not yet ready," "You are making me uncomfortable," "I don't understand," "I want something else," "I need more information," "I want to talk it over with someone else"
