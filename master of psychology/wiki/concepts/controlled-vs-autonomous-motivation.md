# Controlled vs. Autonomous Motivation

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The autonomy-control continuum is the central dimension used within [[self-determination-theory-framework|SDT]] to differentiate types of motivation. Rather than treating motivation as a unitary phenomenon varying only in amount, SDT proposes that motivations differ fundamentally in type based on the degree to which they are autonomous versus controlled.

**Autonomous motivation:** The person experiences volition -- assents to, concurs with, and is wholly willing to engage in the behavior. Behaviors are experienced as emanating from and an expression of one's *self*. Includes [[intrinsic-motivation]], identified regulation, and integrated regulation.

**Controlled motivation:** The person feels externally or internally pressured or compelled to act. Includes external regulation and introjected regulation.

**Amotivation:** The absence of intentionality and motivation -- passivity, ineffectiveness, or purposelessness.

## Key Findings

- SDT research demonstrates that the *type* of motivation (autonomous vs. controlled) predicts the quality of persistence, performance, and health benefits, not just the *amount* of motivation
- More autonomous forms of motivation yield: greater creativity, superior learning, enhanced well-being, higher quality relationships, better performance
- More controlled forms of motivation yield: more impoverished learning, greater behavioral problems, risk of disengagement/dropout, lower well-being
- The continuum from most controlled to most autonomous: **external regulation** -> **introjected regulation** -> **identified regulation** -> **integrated regulation** -> **[[intrinsic-motivation]]**
- Each form is phenomenologically "ordered" in its degree of autonomy (Ryan & Connell, 1989)
- Neurological processes that subserve autonomous versus controlled motives are increasingly being distinguished (Lee et al., 2012; Murayama et al., 2010)
- Autonomous and controlled motives can coexist simultaneously within the same person for the same activity

## Mechanisms

The continuum reflects degrees of *internalization* -- the process by which external regulations are taken in and transformed:

| Regulation Type | Locus | Phenomenology | Example |
|----------------|-------|---------------|---------|
| External | Fully external | Compliance, coercion | "I study because my parents will punish me if I don't" |
| Introjected | Partially internal | Guilt, shame, ego-involvement, contingent self-esteem | "I study because I'd feel guilty if I didn't" -- connects to [[want-should-conflict]] |
| Identified | Internal | Conscious valuing, personal importance | "I study because education is important to me" |
| Integrated | Fully internal | Congruence with self, values alignment | "I study because learning is central to who I am" |
| Intrinsic | Internal | Interest, enjoyment, inherent satisfaction | "I study because I find the subject fascinating" |

The more autonomous the motivational form, the more the individual has access to the full range of cognitive, affective, and energetic resources -- explaining the functional advantages of autonomous motivation.

## Applications

- **Diagnosing motivation quality:** In any context, one can assess whether people's motivation is predominantly autonomous or controlled, predicting the quality of their engagement and outcomes
- **Shifting from controlled to autonomous:** Social contexts can be redesigned to facilitate internalization -- providing autonomy support, competence support, and relatedness support
- **Understanding backfire effects:** External rewards and pressures can shift motivation from autonomous to controlled, explaining why incentive systems sometimes backfire (connecting to [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]])
