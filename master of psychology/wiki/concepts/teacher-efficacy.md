# Teacher Efficacy

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Teacher efficacy refers to teachers' beliefs in their personal capabilities to motivate and promote learning in their students (Bandura, 1997, Ch.6, pp.240-243). It is a domain-specific construct -- teachers may judge themselves highly efficacious in mathematical or science instruction but much less assured of their efficacy in language instruction. Teacher efficacy encompasses beliefs about ability to teach effectively, manage classrooms, engage students, and enlist parental and community involvement.

## Key Findings

- Gibson & Dembo (1984): Teachers' beliefs in their instructional efficacy partly determine how they structure academic activities in their classrooms and shape students' evaluations of their intellectual capabilities ([[summary-3-11-se-ch6-cognitive-functioning]])
- High-efficacy teachers: devote more classroom time to academic activities, provide students who encounter difficulties with the guidance they need to succeed, and praise their academic accomplishments
- Low-efficacy teachers: spend more time on nonacademic pastimes, readily give up on students who do not get quick results, and criticize them for their failures
- Low instructional efficacy orientation: custodial view of students' motivation, strict regulations, extrinsic inducements, and negative sanctions to get students to study (Woolfolk, Rosoff, & Hoy, 1990)
- Melby (1995): Teachers with low efficacy distrust classroom management ability, are stressed and angered by students' misbehavior, are pessimistic about students' improvability, focus on subject matter rather than student development, and would not choose the teaching profession again
- Ashton & Webb (1986): Students learned much more from teachers imbued with a sense of efficacy than from those beset with self-doubts over the course of an academic year
- Anderson, Greene, & Loewen (1988): Teachers' instructional efficacy is a much stronger predictor of academic attainments of younger students than older students -- early school years are especially formative
- Perceived instructional inefficacy linked to teacher burnout through stress and anger (Chwalisz, Altmaier, & Russell, 1992)
- Teacher efficacy was the best predictor of commitment to the teaching profession; low-efficacy teachers are the most likely to drop out (Glickman & Tamashiro, 1982)
- Strong educational leadership by the principal builds teachers' sense of instructional efficacy (Coladarci, 1992)
- Teacher efficacy should be measured with multifaceted, domain-linked scales (Bandura, 1990b), not global two-item measures

## Mechanisms

Teacher efficacy operates through the same four [[self-efficacy-mediating-processes]] as other forms of self-efficacy:

1. **Cognitive:** High-efficacy teachers view difficult students as reachable and learning problems as surmountable; low-efficacy teachers invoke low student ability as explanation for why students cannot be taught
2. **Motivational:** Efficacy beliefs influence the goals teachers set for their students and the level of challenge they provide. High-efficacy teachers adopt a developmental orientation; low-efficacy teachers adopt a custodial orientation
3. **Affective:** Perceived instructional inefficacy is mediated through stress and anger to teacher burnout -- emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and lack of personal accomplishment
4. **Selection:** Teacher efficacy influences receptivity to new pedagogical approaches, adoption of educational technologies, and willingness to innovate (Olivier, 1985; Jorde-Bloom & Ford, 1988)

Teacher efficacy also creates reciprocal effects on students: high-efficacy teachers construct classroom environments that promote mastery experiences, strengthen students' self-efficacy, and raise academic achievement -- which in turn reinforces teachers' sense of instructional efficacy. The reverse cycle is equally powerful: low-efficacy teachers create environments that erode student efficacy, leading to poor outcomes that further undermine teacher efficacy.

## Applications

- **Teacher development:** Build instructional efficacy through mastery experiences in teaching, not just content knowledge
- **School leadership:** Principals who create a strong academic emphasis and serve as advocates for teachers' instructional efforts enhance the staff's sense of instructional efficacy (Hoy & Woolfolk, 1993)
- **Preventing burnout:** Address perceived instructional inefficacy as the central mediator of burnout; develop problem-focused coping strategies rather than escapist coping
- **Educational reform:** Restructure schools to provide teachers with instructional supports, manageable workloads, and collaborative professional communities that sustain efficacy beliefs
