# One-Text Procedure

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The one-text procedure (also called the single negotiating text) is a mediation technique described in [[getting-to-yes|Getting to Yes]] where a third party prepares a single draft proposal, asks each side to criticize it (not approve it), and iteratively revises the draft until no further improvement can be made. Each party then has only one decision to make: yes or no on the final text. This bypasses the back-and-forth of positional bargaining entirely.

## Key Findings

- **Camp David (1978) is the canonical application.** The United States prepared a draft, asked both Israel and Egypt for criticism, and revised it repeatedly. After 13 days and approximately 23 drafts, President Carter presented the text with his recommendation. Both sides accepted. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]])
- **The procedure works because it separates inventing from deciding.** Each side criticizes but does not commit. The mediator absorbs the political risk of proposing solutions. Neither party has to "give in" to the other — they both respond to a neutral document. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]])
- **The house-building analogy illustrates the mechanism.** A husband and wife with incompatible architectural visions hire an architect who asks about interests (not positions), prepares iterative sketches, asks for criticism, and produces a final plan for a single yes/no decision. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]])
- **You don't need the other side's permission to use it.** Simply prepare a draft and ask for criticism. A third party can take a draft around even if the two sides are not willing to talk to each other directly. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]])
- **Essential for large multilateral negotiations.** 150 nations cannot constructively discuss hundreds of proposals. The one-text procedure simplifies the decision-making process by reducing options to a single text that can be refined. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch7-wont-play]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Bypassing [[reactive-devaluation]]:** Because the proposal comes from a neutral third party rather than from the opposing side, it is less likely to be automatically devalued.
2. **Reducing commitment escalation:** Neither side has to abandon a position — they simply criticize a draft. This avoids the psychological cost of "backing down" ([[commitment-and-consistency]], [[loss-aversion]]).
3. **Separating inventing from deciding:** The mediator invents (proposes); the parties decide (accept or reject the final text). This prevents the premature evaluation that kills creativity.
4. **Limiting the decision space:** Each party ultimately faces a binary choice — yes or no on the final text. This is psychologically easier than evaluating an infinite range of possible concessions.

## Applications

- **International diplomacy:** Camp David Accords, Law of the Sea negotiations, multilateral trade agreements
- **Labor-management disputes:** An arbitrator prepares a single draft contract based on both sides' interests
- **Divorce mediation:** A mediator prepares iterative proposals for division of assets, custody arrangements
- **Business mergers:** An investment banker or lawyer prepares iterative term sheets based on both companies' interests
- **Workplace disputes:** An HR mediator can use the one-text procedure even without formal training
