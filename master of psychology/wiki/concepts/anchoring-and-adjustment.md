# Anchoring and Adjustment

**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Confidence:** 0.8

## Definition

Anchoring and adjustment is a heuristic where people make numerical estimates by **starting from an initial value (the "anchor") and adjusting** from it to reach a final answer. The critical finding: adjustments are almost always **insufficient**, so the final estimate is biased toward the anchor.

## Key Findings

- A **completely random anchor** (a spin of a wheel of fortune) massively influenced estimates of the percentage of African countries in the UN. Subjects anchored at 10 gave a median estimate of 25; those anchored at 65 gave 45. ([[summary-1-01-judgment-under-uncertainty]])
- Incentives for accuracy did not eliminate the effect
- **Conjunctive events are overestimated:** People overestimate the probability that ALL of a series of likely events will occur (product of probabilities < individual probabilities). This leads to overoptimistic project plans.
- **Disjunctive events are underestimated:** People underestimate the probability that AT LEAST ONE of a series of unlikely events will occur. This leads to underestimation of risk in complex systems.
- **[[overconfidence-in-calibration]]:** People set far too narrow confidence intervals. When asked for 98% confidence ranges, the true value fell outside ~30% of the time — a 15x error rate.

## Mechanisms

Anchoring works through two potential pathways (the 1974 paper does not distinguish them, but later research does):

1. **Insufficient adjustment:** People start from the anchor and serially adjust until they reach a plausible value — then stop too soon. The adjustment is effortful and terminates prematurely.
2. **Selective accessibility (proposed in later research):** The anchor makes anchor-consistent information more accessible in memory, biasing the judgment toward the anchor without any conscious "adjustment" process.

The conjunctive/disjunctive bias arises because people anchor on the probability of individual events and insufficiently adjust for the compounding effect of combining many events.

## Applications

- **Negotiation:** First offers serve as anchors. The party who sets the initial number has a powerful advantage — all subsequent discussion adjusts from that point. (Critical for Phase 5 — [[getting-to-yes]], [[negotiation-genius]], [[never-split-the-difference]])
- **Pricing:** Initial prices anchor perceived value. A $100 product marked down to $60 is perceived differently than the same product always priced at $60.
- **Project planning:** The tendency to overestimate conjunctive probabilities = systematic overoptimism about project timelines (the "planning fallacy")
- **Risk assessment:** The tendency to underestimate disjunctive probabilities = systematic underestimation of system failure rates
- **Salary negotiation:** The first number spoken anchors the range. Even knowing about anchoring doesn't fully immunize against it.

## Bazerman & Moore Extensions

Bazerman and Moore ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch3]]) significantly extend the anchoring research with additional studies and mechanisms:

- **Two distinct mechanisms (Epley, 2004):** (1) When an anchor is externally set, it leads to a biased search for anchor-consistent information (Mussweiler & Strack, 1999, 2000, 2001). E.g., a house with a high list price leads you to notice positive features. (2) When someone develops their own anchor, they start from it and insufficiently adjust (Epley & Gilovich, 2001). E.g., estimating when George Washington was elected by adjusting from 1776
- **Paying for accuracy does not reduce anchoring** -- unlike many biases, incentives fail to eliminate the effect
- **Mussweiler and Strack (2000):** Participants anchored on high vs. low car prices were faster at recognizing words associated with expensive vs. cheap cars -- the anchor primes related concepts
- **Extreme price anchors:** Simply exposing someone to an extreme price increases willingness to pay for a product, even if the product is in a different category (Janiszewski, Lichtenstein, & Belyavsky, 2008; Adaval & Wyer, 2011)
- **Arbitrary action anchors:** Participants asked to draw long lines gave higher estimates of the Mississippi River's length than those who drew short lines (Oppenheimer, LeBoeuf, & Brewer, 2008)
- **Professional auditors:** Joyce and Biddle (1981) showed anchoring among practicing Big Four auditors -- fraud incidence estimates were 16.52 vs. 43.11 per 1000 depending on the arbitrary anchor in the question
- **Judicial sentencing:** Judges' sentences are influenced by anchors as irrelevant as a roll of dice (Englich & Mussweiler, 2001; Englich, Mussweiler, & Strack, 2006)
- **Education:** Children tracked by ability at an early age become anchored to expectations; Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) showed teacher expectations (anchors) influence actual student performance
- **Race as anchor:** Duncan (1976) found the same behavior perceived as more aggressive when exhibited by an African American than a European American -- race serves as an anchor for behavioral expectations
- **Salary negotiation:** Pre-MBA salaries anchor post-MBA offers despite marginal relevance; women are less inclined to negotiate aggressively (Bowles, Babcock, & McGinn, 2005), resulting in lower salary anchors
- **Nisbett and Ross (1980):** The anchoring bias itself makes it difficult to convince people to change their decision-making strategies -- existing heuristics are cognitive anchors

## Ariely Extensions (Predictably Irrational)

Ariely's experiments extend anchoring into the concept of [[arbitrary-coherence]] -- the idea that initial prices are arbitrary but become coherent reference points for all future valuations ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch2-supply-demand]]):

- **SSN anchoring experiment (Ariely, Prelec, & Loewenstein):** N=55 MIT MBA students. Last two digits of social security number served as price anchors for 6 products. Students with SSN 80-99 bid **216-346% higher** than those with SSN 00-19. Correlations: 0.32-0.52. Yet relative pricing within categories was coherent (everyone bid more for better wine) -- the anchor was arbitrary but created a coherent price structure.
- **Annoying sounds experiment:** First anchor (10 cents vs. 90 cents) persisted across three subsequent sounds and **resisted replacement by new anchors** -- demonstrating that initial anchors have durable, cross-decision effects.
- **Poetry reading experiment:** Whether an ambiguous experience was anchored as "worth paying for" or "requiring compensation" completely determined subsequent valuation patterns.
- **Housing market anchoring (Simonsohn & Loewenstein):** People who move cities remain anchored to their previous city's housing prices -- spending similar amounts regardless of the new market.
- These findings extend anchoring from a one-shot estimation error to a **persistent force shaping long-term consumption patterns** through [[self-herding]].

## Open Questions
- How strong is anchoring when the anchor is obviously absurd? (Bazerman confirms: still remarkably strong -- even subliminal anchors work per Mussweiler & Englich, 2005)
- Is anchoring primarily a failure of adjustment or a priming/accessibility effect? (Epley, 2004 clarifies: both mechanisms operate, depending on whether the anchor is externally imposed or self-generated)
- Can training or debiasing techniques reduce anchoring? (Evidence is mixed -- awareness helps somewhat but doesn't eliminate the effect)
- How can organizations design systems that minimize the influence of irrelevant anchors on important decisions?
