# Authority Principle

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The authority principle states that people have a deep-seated sense of duty to authority and will comply with the requests of an authority figure, often automatically and without critical evaluation. It is the fifth of [[cialdini]]'s six principles of influence. The principle operates as a [[click-whirr]] shortcut: because obeying legitimate authorities is usually adaptive (they typically possess superior knowledge, wisdom, and power), people develop an automatic deference response that can be triggered even by the mere *symbols* of authority — titles, clothing, and trappings — in the absence of genuine expertise.

## Key Findings

- In [[milgram]]'s obedience experiments, approximately **two-thirds of subjects** delivered what they believed were dangerous, potentially fatal electric shocks to a screaming victim simply because a lab-coated researcher instructed them to continue (Milgram, 1974)
- In the Hofling hospital experiment, **95% of nurses** complied with a phone order from an unknown "doctor" to administer a dangerously excessive dose of an unauthorized drug (Hofling et al., 1966)
- In Bickman's uniform studies, compliance with odd requests nearly **doubled** when the requester wore a security guard's uniform vs. street clothes (Bickman, 1974)
- **3.5 times** as many pedestrians followed a jaywalker in a business suit as one in work clothes (Lefkowitz, Blake, & Mouton, 1955)
- Motorists waited significantly longer before honking at a luxury car stalled at a green light vs. an economy car; **50% never honked at all** at the luxury car (Doob & Gross, 1968)
- In every study, participants **grossly underestimated** the effect of authority pressure on behavior
- Results replicated across Holland, Germany, Spain, Italy, Australia, and Jordan (Meeus & Raaijmakers, 1986) and in modern samples (Burger, in press)

## Mechanisms

The authority principle works through several reinforcing mechanisms:

1. **Socialization**: From birth, humans are trained that obedience to proper authority is right and disobedience is wrong — through parental lessons, education, religious instruction, and legal/military/political systems
2. **Adaptive value**: Authorities typically possess superior knowledge, wisdom, and power. Following their directives is usually beneficial, making automatic deference a generally reliable shortcut
3. **Click, whirr responding**: Because authority-following is so consistently rewarding, it becomes automatic — people react to authority cues without conscious deliberation, responding to symbols rather than substance
4. **Symbol sensitivity**: Three categories of [[authority-symbols]] — titles, clothes, and trappings — can trigger the authority response independently of actual expertise, because in [[click-whirr]] mode people attend to only one feature of a situation

The principle's power is amplified by the fact that people systematically underestimate its influence on their own behavior, removing a key potential defense (awareness).

## Applications

- **Advertising**: Using actors who play authority roles (doctors, scientists) to endorse products — the Robert Young / Sanka commercial exploited the fictional title "M.D."
- **Sales and compliance**: The [[sly-sincerity]] tactic combines authority with apparent trustworthiness — arguing against one's own interests on minor points to become more persuasive on major ones
- **Con artistry**: Fraudsters drape themselves in titles, uniforms, and trappings of authority (e.g., the bank examiner scheme)
- **Organizational design**: Understanding authority pressure is critical for designing systems where subordinates need to challenge superiors (e.g., aviation "Captainitis," hospital medication errors)
- **Defense**: Two diagnostic questions — "Is this authority truly an expert?" and "How truthful can we expect this expert to be?" — can interrupt the automatic deference response

## Open Questions

- How has the rise of the internet and democratized information affected the strength of the authority principle? Does easier access to credentials reduce symbol-based deference?
- To what extent do cultural dimensions (e.g., Hofstede's power distance) moderate the strength of the authority principle beyond the cross-cultural replications already documented?
- What is the interaction between authority pressure and other influence principles when they conflict (e.g., authority says one thing, social proof says another)?
