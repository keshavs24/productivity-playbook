# System 1 and System 2 Thinking

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

System 1 and System 2 refer to two distinct modes of cognitive processing (Stanovich & West, 2000; Kahneman, 2003). **System 1** is fast, automatic, effortless, implicit, and emotional -- the intuitive system used for most everyday decisions. **System 2** is slower, conscious, effortful, explicit, and logical -- the deliberate reasoning system. The distinction provides the foundational framework for understanding why heuristics and biases pervade human judgment.

## Key Findings

- Most life decisions use System 1, and this is often sufficient (e.g., interpreting language, grocery shopping) ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch1]])
- System 2 should govern our most important decisions, but managers are more likely to rely on System 1 when busier, more rushed, and with more on their minds (Chugh, 2004)
- System 2 does not fully override System 1: people cannot overcome the impulse to avoid eating from a container labeled "cyanide" even when they wrote the label themselves (Rozin, Markwith, & Ross, 1990)
- The two systems frequently work in tandem: System 1 provides a quick initial response, then System 2 modifies it -- but System 2 often does not fully adjust
- Biases and errors are much more likely in System 1 thinking; any methodical System 2 process will still use some intuitive System 1 shortcuts
- The Shepard (1990) table illusion demonstrates System 1 visual processing failures that System 2 knowledge cannot override

## Mechanisms

System 1 operates automatically and quickly, with little effort and no sense of voluntary control. It generates impressions, feelings, and inclinations that, when endorsed by System 2, become beliefs, attitudes, and intentions. System 2 allocates attention to effortful mental activities that demand it. The subjective experience of System 2 is associated with agency, choice, and concentration.

The key vulnerability: System 1 continuously generates suggestions for System 2 -- impressions, intuitions, intentions, and feelings. If endorsed by System 2, impressions and intuitions turn into beliefs. System 2 is often lazy, accepting System 1's suggestions with little scrutiny, especially under cognitive load, time pressure, or distraction.

## Applications

- **Managerial decision making:** A key goal is to identify situations where you should move from intuitively compelling System 1 to the more logical System 2 ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch1]])
- **Debiasing:** Understanding which system is generating a judgment helps identify when to slow down and deliberate
- **Decision architecture:** Designing environments that trigger System 2 engagement for high-stakes decisions (e.g., checklists, structured decision protocols)
- **Training:** Making people aware of the System 1/System 2 distinction is itself a debiasing intervention -- it creates a framework for recognizing when intuition may mislead

## Anderson's Cognitive Science Perspective (Ch.10)

Anderson uses the terminology **Type 1** and **Type 2 processes** (Evans, 2007; Stanovich, 2011) rather than System 1/System 2 (Kahneman), and adds important neuroscience and nuance ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch10]]):

- **Brain imaging evidence**: The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is more engaged when Type 1 and Type 2 processes lead to different conclusions (de Neys, Vartanian, & Goel, 2008) -- providing a neural marker of the conflict between the two systems
- **IQ and Type 2 engagement**: Higher IQ correlates with better normative performance on the [[wason-selection-task]] (Newstead et al., 2004), suggesting IQ partially reflects the ability or willingness to engage Type 2 processing
- **Timing evidence**: Quick responses align with Type 1 outputs; slower responses align with Type 2 -- consistent with the fast/slow characterization
- **Critical nuance -- Type 1 is not always wrong**: Anderson emphasizes that in the real world, Type 1 processes can take advantage of the "wisdom of experience" and outperform Type 2 logic. Formal logic often fails to capture the full complexity of real-world situations; Type 1 processes that incorporate this complexity may be more adaptive than strict logical reasoning.
- **Unfalsifiability criticism**: Anderson notes that dual-process theories have been criticized because they can accommodate any result -- irrational behavior is attributed to Type 1 dominance, rational behavior to Type 2 dominance

## Open Questions
- How much can System 2 training reduce System 1 biases in practice? Evidence is mixed.
- Are there individual differences in the default reliance on System 1 vs. System 2? (Stanovich's work on "cognitive miserliness" suggests yes.)
- Does expertise shift judgments from System 2 to System 1 (automaticity), and if so, does this create new vulnerabilities?
- Can dual-process theory be falsified, or does it merely re-describe any finding? (Anderson raises this concern.)
