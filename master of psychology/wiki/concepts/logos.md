# Logos

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Logos is persuasion through logical argument — showing the truth or apparent truth "through the persuasive arguments [logoi] themselves" (1.2.6). According to [[aristotle]] in [[on-rhetoric]], logos is one of the [[three-modes-of-persuasion]] (alongside [[ethos]] and [[pathos]]).

Logos operates through two forms of proof:
1. **[[Enthymeme]]** (rhetorical syllogism) — deductive reasoning from probable premises; the "body" of persuasion
2. **Paradigm** (rhetorical example/induction) — drawing conclusions from parallel cases, whether historical or invented (fables)

## Key Findings

- Aristotle considers the enthymeme the primary tool of logos, calling it the "body" (*soma*) of persuasion (1.1.3)
- Logical proof in rhetoric differs from logical proof in dialectic: rhetoric deals with probabilities (*eikota*) and signs (*semeia*), not certainties (1.2.14-15)
- The materials (premises) of enthymemes come from specific disciplines — politics, ethics, and other subject matter. The formal structure comes from [[topoi]] — common strategies of argument applicable to any subject (1.2.21-22)
- There are twenty-eight common topoi for constructing enthymemes (2.23) and nine fallacious topoi to recognize (2.24) — see [[topoi]]
- The uneducated are actually more persuasive than the educated before crowds — "the educated reason with axioms and universals, the uneducated from what they know and from instances near their experience" (2.22.3)

## Mechanisms

Logos works through the audience's natural capacity to recognize truth and valid reasoning:
1. **Humans have a natural disposition for the true** — "the true and what resembles the true are grasped by the same capacity" (1.1.11)
2. **Enthymemes exploit shared assumptions** — they work because the audience supplies the missing premise from commonly held opinions (*endoxa*)
3. **The audience experiences intellectual satisfaction** — recognizing the logical connection in an enthymeme produces understanding, which is pleasurable (cf. 1.11.21-23)

## Applications

- Use enthymemes as the primary form of proof; supplement with paradigms (examples) as witnesses
- Draw premises from what the specific audience knows and believes — not from abstract universals
- Refutative enthymemes (showing the opponent's position leads to contradiction) are more popular with audiences than demonstrative ones (2.23.30)
- Avoid two common errors: (a) drawing conclusions from too far back (obscurity) and (b) stating what is obvious (tedium) (2.22.3)

## Open Questions

- How does logos interact with the other modes? Aristotle treats them as separate channels, but modern persuasion research suggests they are deeply intertwined — a strong logical argument enhances ethos, and emotional arousal can make arguments seem more compelling.
- The relationship between logos and [[framing-effects]]: Aristotle's topoi are essentially framing strategies — the same facts can be presented as an argument from opposites, from degree, from consequences, etc. How self-aware was Aristotle about the role of framing vs. truth-tracking?
