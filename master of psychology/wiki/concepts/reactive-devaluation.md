# Reactive Devaluation

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Reactive devaluation is the tendency for negotiators to devalue a proposal or concession simply because it was offered by the opposing party. The same proposal is perceived as less attractive when it comes from the other side than when it comes from a neutral source or from one's own side. This bias reinforces the [[fixed-pie-bias]] and can block mutually beneficial agreements.

## Key Findings

- **Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner, and Ross (1990):** Demonstrated that concessions made by an adversary are systematically devalued relative to identical concessions from a neutral third party or from one's own side. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch12]])
- **Curhan, Neale, and Ross (2004):** Had negotiators estimate the value of various possible outcomes before and after a negotiation. Negotiators tended to like a possible outcome MORE after they proposed it in the negotiation and LESS after it was proposed by the other side -- even when the objective content was identical.
- The logic underlying reactive devaluation: "If she is willing to make this concession, the issue must not be very important" -- a reasonable inference in isolation but one that systematically biases evaluation of all proposals from the other side.

## Mechanisms

1. **Suspicion of motives:** If the other side offers something, it must benefit them, which (under [[fixed-pie-bias]] assumptions) means it must hurt you
2. **Endowment-like effects for proposals:** Ideas "owned" by one's own side feel more valuable than identical ideas attributed to the opponent
3. **Confirmation of adversarial framing:** Reactive devaluation reinforces the perception that the negotiation is purely competitive

## Applications

- **Negotiation strategy (Phase 5):** When you have a good proposal, consider having a neutral third party present it rather than presenting it yourself. The same proposal will be evaluated more favorably.
- **Mediation:** A key reason why mediators are valuable -- proposals from a neutral source avoid reactive devaluation
- **Internal advocacy:** When trying to get organizational buy-in for ideas that originated from a rival department, reframe the proposal as jointly developed or independently sourced
- **Self-awareness:** When evaluating a proposal from the other side, ask: "Would I view this differently if it came from my own team?"
