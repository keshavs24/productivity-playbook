# Contribution System

**Phase(s):** 4, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The contribution system is the [[stone-patton-heen]] alternative to blame in the [[what-happened-conversation]]. Instead of asking "Whose fault is it?" (which produces defensiveness, denial, and either/or thinking), the contribution system asks "How did we each contribute to this situation?" It reframes problems as the result of a *system* of interacting actions and choices by both parties, rather than the fault of a single person.

## Key Findings

- **Blame looks backward; contribution looks forward.** Blame fixates on who caused the problem and what punishment they deserve. Contribution analysis focuses on understanding the real causes of the problem and how to prevent recurrence. ([[summary-4-15-dc-ch1-three-conversations]])
- **In situations that give rise to difficult conversations, it is almost always true that what happened is the result of things *both* people did or failed to do.** The problem is that it's much easier to see the other person's contribution from the outside than to see our own from the inside. Parents watching twins fight in the back seat can see both children's contributions; each child can only see the other's.
- **Blame produces predictable defensive reactions.** "Nobody wants to be blamed, especially unfairly, so our energy goes into defending ourselves." When you blame someone, they don't reflect and learn — they prepare their defense. The focus shifts from understanding to self-protection.
- **Even when one person's contribution is much larger, the contribution frame is still more productive.** When competent, sensible people do something stupid, the smartest move is to figure out what kept them from seeing it coming and how to prevent it from happening again — not to assign fault and punishment.

## Mechanisms

The shift from blame to contribution works because:

1. **It reduces defensiveness.** When you say "I'd like to understand how we each contributed to this" rather than "This is your fault," the other person can engage without feeling attacked.
2. **It surfaces hidden causes.** Blame settles on the most visible cause. Contribution analysis reveals the system — including structural factors, missing communication, role confusion, and environmental pressures.
3. **It creates joint ownership.** When both parties see their contribution, both are motivated to change. When only one person is "at fault," only one person is expected to change (and they resist doing so because they feel the blame is unfair).
4. **It models the [[and-stance]].** Your contribution *and* my contribution. Not either/or.

In the Jack-Michael example: Michael contributed by not mentioning the chart error when he reviewed the mock-up that morning, by not communicating how sensitive the revenue figures were, and by expressing frustration through an angry voicemail rather than a conversation. Jack contributed by not double-checking the chart and by agreeing to take on a rush job without adequate time. Both contributions matter.

## Applications

- **Workplace post-mortems:** "How did our process contribute to this failure?" rather than "Who dropped the ball?"
- **Relationship conflicts:** "What did each of us do that led to this argument?" rather than "You started it."
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Understanding the contribution system is essential for moving from positional bargaining to interest-based problem solving. See [[interest-based-negotiation]].
