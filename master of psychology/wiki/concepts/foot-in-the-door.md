# Foot-in-the-Door Technique

**Phase(s):** 1, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The foot-in-the-door technique is a compliance strategy in which a small initial request is made to increase the likelihood of compliance with a larger, related (or even unrelated) subsequent request. The mechanism operates through [[commitment-and-consistency]]: agreeing to the small request changes the person's self-image, making them more willing to comply with the larger request to remain consistent with their new self-concept.

## Key Findings

- **Freedman & Fraser (1966)** — the foundational experiment:
  - Homeowners asked to display a tiny "BE A SAFE DRIVER" sign nearly all agreed. Two weeks later, **76%** agreed to install a massive, ugly "DRIVE CAREFULLY" billboard — vs. only **17%** of those who received no initial request.
  - Even more remarkably, people who first signed a petition for "keeping California beautiful" (a different topic entirely) later agreed to the billboard at roughly **50%** — demonstrating that the mechanism operates through *self-image change*, not topic-specific consistency.
- **Sherman (1980)**: Asking residents to *predict* their volunteering behavior produced a **700% increase** in actual volunteering when later contacted.
- **Howard (1990)**: The "How are you feeling?" preface nearly doubled solicitation compliance (18% to 32%).
- **Trivial first commitments can begin a "momentum of compliance"** leading to behaviors as significant as organ or bone marrow donation (Carducci, Deuser, Bauer, Large, & Ramaekers, 1989; Schwartz, 1970).
- **Burger & Caldwell (2003)**: Even trivial agreements can alter self-concepts and increase compliance with much larger, even unrelated requests.
- **Petrova, Cialdini, & Sills (2007)**: Individualistic (American) students were more than twice as likely to comply with foot-in-the-door as collectivistic (Asian) students (21.6% vs. 9.9%).

## Mechanisms

1. **Self-image shift**: After agreeing to a small request, the person sees themselves differently — as "the kind of person who does this sort of thing" (Freedman & Fraser). This new self-image then drives compliance with larger requests to maintain consistency.
2. **[[commitment-and-consistency]]**: The initial agreement functions as a commitment, triggering the consistency principle. The person acts to maintain alignment between their behavior and their self-concept.
3. **Generalization**: The self-image change is not specific to the original request domain. A person who signs an environmental petition may see themselves as a "public-spirited citizen" and later comply with an unrelated civic request.

## Applications

- **Sales**: Start with a small order to establish someone as a "customer" — further purchases flow naturally from the commitment. *American Salesman*: "The general idea is to pave the way for full-line distribution by starting with a small order."
- **Charitable organizations**: Use progressively escalating commitments — starting with agreeing to be interviewed, then volunteering, then donating.
- **Political campaigns**: Petition signing commits signers to the group's position. The signatures themselves may never be used.
- **Jury selection**: Jo-Ellen Demitrius asks prospective jurors to commit to independence before trial begins.
- **Korean War POW camps**: The Chinese used this as their primary escalation strategy — start with trivial anti-American statements, escalate to essays and broadcasts.
- Contrast with [[rejection-then-retreat]], which works by starting *large* and conceding to a smaller request.

## Open Questions

- What is the optimal size of the initial request? Too small may not trigger self-image change; too large may be refused.
- How does the time delay between initial and subsequent request affect the technique's power?
- Is the technique more or less effective in digital contexts (e.g., email opt-ins, click-through sequences)?
- How does awareness of the technique affect its effectiveness? (Cialdini notes he is now "rarely willing to sign a petition anymore, even for a position I support.")
