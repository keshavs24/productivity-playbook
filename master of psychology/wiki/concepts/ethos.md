# Ethos

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Ethos is persuasion through the character of the speaker as projected in the speech itself. According to [[aristotle]] in [[on-rhetoric]], ethos is one of the [[three-modes-of-persuasion]] (alongside [[pathos]] and [[logos]]). It occurs "whenever the speech is spoken in such a way as to make the speaker worthy of credence" (1.2.4). Aristotle calls it "almost the most authoritative form of persuasion."

Critically, Aristotle limits ethos to what emerges from the speech — not from the speaker's prior reputation. "This should result from the speech, not from a previous opinion that the speaker is a certain kind of person" (1.2.4). This is a narrower definition than modern usage, which includes extrinsic authority.

## Key Findings

- **Three components of ethos** (2.1.5-7): A speaker is persuasive through character when they display:
  1. **Practical wisdom** (*phronesis*) — the speaker seems to know what they are talking about; they form correct opinions
  2. **Virtue** (*arete*) — the speaker seems morally good; they share honest opinions
  3. **Good will** (*eunoia*) — the speaker seems to wish the audience well; they give the best advice
- A speaker who fails on any one of these three will not be persuasive through ethos — they may be knowledgeable but dishonest, honest but incompetent, or competent and honest but indifferent to the audience's interests
- In deliberative rhetoric, ethos is especially important because the audience must trust the speaker's judgment about future outcomes
- The character appropriate to a speech should match the character of the audience — a democratic audience responds to democratic character, an aristocratic audience to aristocratic character (1.8.6)
- Maxims (*gnomae*) contribute to ethos by making the speech "ethical" — they reveal the speaker's deliberate moral choices and values (2.21.16)

## Mechanisms

Ethos works through a cognitive shortcut: when audiences cannot independently verify the truth of what is being argued (which is most of the time), they use the speaker's apparent character as a proxy for the reliability of the argument. If the speaker seems wise, good, and well-intentioned, the audience infers that the argument is more likely to be sound.

This mechanism operates through three parallel assessments:
1. **Competence judgment** (phronesis): "Does this person know what they're talking about?"
2. **Character judgment** (arete): "Is this person honest and morally sound?"
3. **Benevolence judgment** (eunoia): "Does this person have my best interests at heart?"

## Applications

- A speaker builds ethos by demonstrating knowledge of the subject, displaying moral character through word choice and framing (e.g., the reframing techniques in 1.9.28-29), and showing concern for the audience
- Epideictic rhetoric (praise and blame) is closely connected to ethos — knowledge of what is honorable builds the speaker's credibility (1.9.1)
- Adapting the speech to the audience's character (Book 2, chs. 12-17) is a form of ethos management — matching the audience's values signals shared identity

## Open Questions

- How does Aristotle's speech-internal ethos relate to the [[authority-principle]] (Cialdini), where external symbols of authority (titles, uniforms, trappings) are highly persuasive independent of speech content? Modern credibility research suggests both intrinsic (speech-based) and extrinsic (reputation-based) factors contribute.
- To what extent does ethos function as a heuristic similar to [[social-proof]] — where the audience takes the speaker's character as a shortcut for evaluating argument quality?
