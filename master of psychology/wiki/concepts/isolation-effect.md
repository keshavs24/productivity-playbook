# Isolation Effect

**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Confidence:** 0.6

## Definition

The isolation effect is the tendency to **discard components shared by all alternatives** and focus only on the components that distinguish them. Because the same choice can be decomposed into shared and distinctive components in more than one way, this leads to inconsistent preferences depending on how the choice is *presented*.

## Key Findings

- Problem 10: A two-stage game produced different preferences (78% chose certain 3,000) than the equivalent single-stage formulation (Problem 4: 65% chose risky 4,000) — because the sequential framing made the certainty of 3,000 in Stage 2 salient ([[summary-1-02-prospect-theory]])
- Problems 11 & 12: Adding a common bonus to both options, then framing the choice as gains vs. losses, reversed preferences — even though the final wealth positions were identical
- The isolation effect is the mechanism through which **[[framing-effects]]** operate — framing determines which components appear "shared" and get discarded

## Mechanisms

Simplification. The mind reduces cognitive load by canceling shared components and evaluating only differences. This is efficient but context-dependent: the same objective choice, decomposed differently, highlights different components and yields different preferences. The reference point (what counts as the "baseline" to be discarded) is set by the framing, not by the objective situation.

## Applications

- **Framing in negotiation:** How a deal is structured (what's presented as the "given" vs. the "choice") changes what parties focus on and what they prefer
- **Product comparison:** What features are presented as "standard" (shared, ignored) vs. "differentiating" (focused on) shapes purchasing decisions
- **Policy framing:** Whether a tax is framed as a "bonus lost" or a "penalty imposed" changes public reaction despite identical financial impact
