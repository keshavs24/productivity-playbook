# Extrinsic Aspirations

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Extrinsic aspirations are life goals focused on obtaining external indicators of worth, contingent outcomes, or socially comparative advantages. As defined within [[goal-contents-theory|GCT]], the three categories of extrinsic aspirations are:

1. **Wealth** (financial success) -- accumulating money, possessions, and material resources
2. **Fame** (social recognition) -- being well-known, admired, and famous
3. **Image** (attractiveness/appearance) -- looking physically attractive, having the "right" appearance

These goals are termed "extrinsic" because they are focused on instrumental outcomes, contingent approval, or social comparison rather than on inherently satisfying activities. Their pursuit and attainment tend to be only indirectly related to [[basic-psychological-needs|basic need satisfaction]] and often involve processes (social comparison, contingent self-worth, objectification of self and others) that actively frustrate needs. They are contrasted with [[intrinsic-aspirations]] (personal growth, close relationships, community contribution, health).

## Key Findings

- **Consistent negative associations with well-being:** Placing high [[relative-aspirations|relative importance]] on extrinsic vs. intrinsic aspirations predicts lower self-actualization, lower vitality, more depression, more anxiety, more physical symptoms, and more narcissism (Kasser & Ryan, 1993, 1996)
- **Risk behaviors:** Strong relative extrinsic aspirations predicted more high-risk behaviors in U.S. high school students -- tobacco, alcohol, marijuana (Williams et al., 2000), more drug use and television watching in college students (Kasser & Ryan, 2001)
- **Relationship quality:** High extrinsic aspirations associated with more conflicted, less satisfying intimate relationships; less empathy; more Machiavellian attitudes; more instrumental use of friends (Kasser & Ryan, 2001; Sheldon & Kasser, 1995; Khanna & Kasser, 1999)
- **Social and environmental harm:** Extrinsic orientation predicted overharvesting of common resources (Sheldon & McGregor, 2000), larger environmental footprint (Brown & Kasser, 2005), more racial/ethnic prejudice and social dominance (Duriez et al., 2007), and more competition when resources were scarce (Sheldon et al., 2000)
- **Attainment effects:** Attaining extrinsic goals yields no well-being gains or actively predicts ill-being (Sheldon & Kasser, 1998; Niemiec et al., 2009). In older adults, extrinsic goal attainment was unrelated to psychological health and contributed positively to despair (Van Hiel & Vansteenkiste, 2009). High-paid lawyers in money-focused firms showed more negative affect and more alcohol consumption than lower-paid public-service lawyers (Sheldon & Krieger, 2014a).
- **Materialism meta-analysis:** Dittmar, Bond, Hurst, & Kasser (2014) -- 753 effect sizes, 259 samples: materialism yields d = -0.24 on well-being, mediated by low basic need satisfaction
- **Match hypothesis fails:** Extrinsic aspirations predict lower well-being even in environments that ostensibly endorse them -- business school (Kasser & Ahuvia, 2002), prisons (Kasser, 1996)
- **Vicious cycle:** Loneliness (relatedness frustration) predicts materialism, and materialism predicts greater loneliness (Pieters, 2013). Failure to attain intrinsic aspirations predicts compensatory extrinsic behavior like compulsive buying (Otero-Lopez & Villardefrancos, 2015).

## Mechanisms

1. **Compensatory origins:** Extrinsic aspirations frequently develop as compensatory responses to [[need-frustration|need thwarting]] during development. Cold, controlling, authoritarian parenting predicts children placing high importance on wealth (Kasser et al., 1995). Williams et al. (1999): low parental autonomy support predicted stronger extrinsic aspirations.
2. **Need-frustrating pursuit:** The process of pursuing extrinsic goals often compromises autonomy (social comparison, contingent self-worth pressures) and relatedness (instrumental relationships, objectification of others). Even when extrinsic goals are attained, they yield at most competence satisfaction -- which alone is insufficient for well-being.
3. **Controlled regulation:** Extrinsic goals are more often regulated through introjection and external pressure (ego involvement, contingent self-esteem) rather than integrated, autonomous motivation. This controlled regulation depletes vitality and undermines wellness.
4. **Cultural amplification:** Advertising and consumer culture actively promote extrinsic aspirations (Kasser, Kanner, Cohn, & Ryan, 2007). The "American Dream" narrative equates success with wealth, fame, and image, normalizing goals that empirically undermine well-being.

## Applications

- **Critical media literacy:** Help individuals (especially adolescents) recognize and resist the pervasive cultural promotion of extrinsic aspirations through advertising and social media
- **Goal hierarchy analysis:** When extrinsic goals are unavoidable (e.g., earning a living), examine whether they serve intrinsic superordinate goals (family support, community) or have become ends in themselves
- **Intervention:** Materialistic adolescents given interventions to decrease materialism showed increased self-esteem (Kasser et al., 2014). Mindfulness training reduces financial desire discrepancy and shifts toward intrinsic values (Brown et al., 2009).
