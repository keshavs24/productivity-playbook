# Self-Serving Bias in Negotiation

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Self-serving bias in negotiation is the tendency for each party in a dispute to sincerely believe that their preferred outcome is the "fair" one. This is not strategic manipulation -- negotiators genuinely interpret evidence, select fairness criteria, and weight arguments in ways that favor their own position, all without conscious awareness of the distortion. The result is that even when both parties want to be fair, their different definitions of fairness create genuine impasses.

## Key Findings

- **Thompson and Loewenstein (1992):** In a simulated labor dispute, self-serving biased attention to available information affected each party's perceptions of a fair settlement. The magnitude of the bias predicted the length of the resulting strike -- stronger bias meant longer strikes. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch12]])
- **Babcock, Loewenstein, Issacharoff, and Camerer (1995):** Participants assigned as plaintiffs or defendants in a lawsuit made self-serving predictions of the judge's ruling. Plaintiffs predicted significantly higher awards than defendants. The degree of discrepancy predicted whether pairs settled or went to (costly) trial. Neither financial incentives for accuracy nor essay-writing about the other side's perspective reduced the bias. The mechanism operates at the level of evidence interpretation -- participants viewed arguments supporting their assigned position as more convincing.
- **Babcock and Loewenstein (1997):** Rewarding accurate prediction of the judge's ruling with cash had no effect on the self-serving bias
- **Loewenstein and Moore (2004):** When roles were assigned AFTER reading the evidence, the bias was substantially reduced -- demonstrating that the bias works by distorting the encoding of information, not just the recall
- **Wade-Benzoni, Tenbrunsel, and Bazerman (1996):** In a fishery commons dilemma simulation, parties who harvested more exhibited stronger self-serving fairness biases. Discussion among parties reduced the magnitude of the bias.
- **Climate change negotiations:** The U.S.-China impasse illustrates self-serving fairness at the national level -- each country sincerely believes the other bears more responsibility.

## Mechanisms

1. **Selective attention:** People attend more to information that supports their preferred outcome (Babcock et al., 1995)
2. **Criteria selection:** Multiple legitimate fairness criteria exist (equality, equity, need, market rate). People unconsciously select whichever criterion favors them ([[self-serving-bias-in-decisions]])
3. **Evidence interpretation:** The same evidence is interpreted differently depending on which role one occupies -- arguments supporting one's own position appear objectively stronger
4. **Pre-conscious encoding:** The bias operates during initial information processing, not just at the recall stage (Loewenstein & Moore, 2004)

## Applications

- **Negotiation awareness (Phase 5):** Expect that your counterpart sincerely believes their position is fair. They are not lying -- they are biased. And so are you.
- **Perspective assignment timing:** In organizational disputes, have decision-makers review all evidence BEFORE assigning roles or positions to reduce encoding bias
- **Communication strategies:** Asking questions, seeking tradeoffs, and making concessions can reduce self-serving bias by exposing each party to the other's perspective
- **Dispute resolution:** Mediators should help each party recognize that their fairness perception is likely biased, rather than assuming one side is being strategic
- **Common resource management:** Self-serving biases make self-policing unrealistic in commons dilemmas. Systemic enforcement mechanisms are needed.
