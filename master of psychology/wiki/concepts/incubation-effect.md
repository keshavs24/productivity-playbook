# Incubation Effect

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The incubation effect is the phenomenon in which stepping away from an unsolved problem for a period of time improves the likelihood of solving it upon return. Contrary to popular belief, this is NOT because the subconscious continues working on the problem during the break. Rather, the break allows **inappropriate strategies and set effects to be forgotten**, enabling the solver to take a fresh approach.

## Key Findings

- Silveira (1971) tested the cheap-necklace problem: 55% solved it with continuous work (30 min), 64% with a half-hour break inserted, and 85% with a 4-hour break ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch8]])
- Critically, participants did NOT come back after breaks with pre-formed solutions -- they started over from scratch, suggesting the break did not involve unconscious problem solving
- Smith and Blakenship (1989, 1991) directly tested the "forgetting inappropriate strategies" hypothesis. Participants given deliberately misleading hints (inducing fixation) benefited MORE from incubation than control participants. Those who had incubation periods were more likely to have forgotten the misleading hint.
- The mathematician Henri Poincare (1929) reported multiple instances of breakthroughs coming after periods of rest, including his discovery about Fuchsian functions while stepping onto a bus during a geology excursion

## Mechanisms

During initial attempts, problem solvers activate certain knowledge structures and commit to certain approaches ([[set-effect]]). If these are inappropriate, the solver is stuck -- continuously reactivating the wrong strategies. Going away from the problem allows the activation of these inappropriate knowledge structures to decay. Upon returning, the solver is more likely to activate different (potentially correct) approaches. The incubation effect is thus the flip side of the [[set-effect]] -- it works because set effects are temporary and fade with time.

## Applications

- When stuck on a difficult problem, deliberately take a break -- the longer the better (within reason). This is not procrastination; it is a cognitively grounded strategy.
- The benefit comes from doing something DIFFERENT during the break, not from sleeping on it (though sleep may also help via memory consolidation)
- Be aware that the fresh approach after a break may not feel like insight -- you will likely restart your search, not receive a sudden revelation
- Combine with explicit "Don't be blind" self-instructions to maximize the chance of finding new approaches
