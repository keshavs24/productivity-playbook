# Commitment and Consistency

**Phase(s):** 1, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.6

## Definition

The principle of commitment and consistency states that once a person makes a choice or takes a stand, they encounter personal and interpersonal pressures to behave consistently with that commitment. This drive to be (and appear) consistent is one of the six fundamental principles of social influence identified by [[cialdini]]. It operates as a [[click-whirr]] mechanism — once a commitment triggers the consistency tape, subsequent behavior follows automatically, often without conscious deliberation.

## Key Findings

- **Post-decisional confidence boost**: Racetrack bettors become much more confident of their horse's chances immediately after placing bets (Knox & Inkster, 1968). Voters believe more strongly their candidate will win after casting a ballot (Regan & Kilduff, 1988).
- **Behavioral transformation through trivial commitments**: In the NYC beach theft experiment, a simple "please watch my things" request transformed 4/20 bystander interveners into 19/20 (Moriarty, 1975).
- **Prediction-based commitment**: Asking people to predict their behavior (e.g., volunteering for charity) produced a 700% increase in actual compliance (Sherman, 1980).
- **"How are you feeling?" technique**: Prefacing a solicitation with this question doubled compliance rates from 18% to 32% (Howard, 1990).
- **Four factors that maximize commitment effectiveness**: Commitments are most powerful when they are [[active-commitment|active]], [[public-commitment|public]], [[effortful-commitment|effortful]], and perceived as [[inner-choice|internally motivated]].
- **Commitments "grow their own legs"**: People generate new reasons to justify commitments, so decisions persist even after original reasons are removed (the mechanism behind the [[lowball-technique]]).

## Mechanisms

1. **Cognitive shortcut**: Like other [[click-whirr]] responses, consistency offers an efficient way to navigate complex decisions. Once a stand is taken, we don't need to re-analyze — we simply act consistently.
2. **Self-image alignment**: Commitments change how we see ourselves. After agreeing to a small request, a person may come to view themselves as "the kind of person who does this sort of thing" (Freedman & Fraser, 1966), making larger consistent requests easier to accept.
3. **Social desirability of consistency**: Inconsistency is culturally stigmatized as confused, two-faced, or mentally ill (Allgeier et al., 1979; Asch, 1946). Consistency is associated with rationality, strength, and honesty.
4. **Fortress against thought**: Consistency can serve as a psychological fortress — a place to hide from unwelcome conclusions. People sometimes commit *faster* when confronted with evidence against their choice, to avoid having to think.
5. **Self-generated justification**: Once committed, people build new supporting reasons for their decision. These "legs" sustain the commitment independently of the original reason, making it extraordinarily resilient.

## Applications

- **Sales**: The [[foot-in-the-door]] technique, the [[lowball-technique]], written commitments to prevent cancellations, testimonial contests
- **Fundraising**: The "How are you feeling?" opening, prediction-based volunteering (Sherman, 1980)
- **Negotiation**: Extracting verbal commitments ("Will you call us if you change your plans?") to increase follow-through (Gordon Sinclair's restaurant — no-shows dropped from 30% to 10%)
- **Persuasion/Indoctrination**: The Chinese POW "lenient policy" — start small, escalate through writing, public declaration, and essay contests
- **Personal behavior change**: Public commitments for weight loss, smoking cessation; writing down goals (Amway approach)
- **Defense**: Listen for "stomach signs" (visceral discomfort) and use the "heart-of-hearts" test — "Knowing what I know now, would I make the same choice?"

## Bazerman & Moore Extensions (Chapter 8)

Bazerman and Moore ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch8]]) reveal the dark side of consistency pressure in decision making:

- **[[escalation-of-commitment]]:** Consistency pressure drives managers to continue failing courses of action. Staw (1976): decision-makers who made the initial investment allocated significantly more to a failing division than those who inherited the decision. The mechanism is self-justification -- consistency with prior choices.
- **Impression management:** Society rewards consistency and punishes "flip-flopping." Staw and Ross (1980): consistent administrators are perceived as better leaders. The Kerry vs. Bush 2004 dynamic illustrates this -- Kerry's position changes were framed as weakness, Bush's stubbornness as strength.
- **Ross & Staw (1986):** People are more likely to be rewarded for escalating commitment than for changing course, creating organizational incentives for irrational consistency.
- **Organizational remedy:** Reward decision QUALITY at each stage rather than consistency. Separate initial decision-makers from subsequent evaluators. Evaluate the process, not just the outcome (Staw & Ross, 1987).
- This connects Cialdini's interpersonal influence perspective to Bazerman's managerial decision-making perspective: the same consistency drive that makes compliance techniques work also traps managers in failing strategies.

## Open Questions

- How does commitment and consistency interact with [[loss-aversion]]? Does the sunk-cost aspect of effortful commitment tap into loss-aversion mechanisms? Bazerman's Ch.8 confirms: yes, quitting is framed as a sure loss, and [[prospect-theory]] risk-seeking in losses drives escalation.
- What is the boundary between adaptive consistency (valuable shortcut) and exploitable consistency (foolish fortress)? Can we develop better heuristics for distinguishing the two?
- How do digital-age commitments (social media posts, online reviews, public profiles) amplify or alter the consistency effect compared to traditional commitments?
- Does the age-related increase in preference for consistency (Brown, Asher, & Cialdini, 2005) reflect wisdom (honoring commitments) or vulnerability (rigidity)?
