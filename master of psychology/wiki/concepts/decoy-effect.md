# Decoy Effect (Asymmetric Dominance)

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The decoy effect (also called asymmetric dominance) occurs when adding a third option that is clearly inferior to one of two existing options -- but not easily comparable to the other -- **shifts preference toward the option that dominates the decoy**. The decoy is never chosen itself but systematically changes which of the other options people prefer.

Formally: Given options A and B that differ on two attributes, adding option -A (similar to A but slightly worse) makes A look better not just relative to -A, but relative to B as well.

## Key Findings

- **Ariely's Economist subscription experiment ([[summary-2-S1-ariely-ch1]]):** Three options -- Internet $59, Print $125, Print+Internet $125. With the decoy (print-only at $125): 84% chose print+Internet. Without it: only 32% chose print+Internet. A completely unchosen option shifted 52% of preferences
- **Attractiveness experiment:** When students saw person A, a decoy version of A (-A, slightly less attractive), and person B, they chose A 75% of the time. Without the decoy, preference was roughly equal
- **Bread maker effect:** Williams-Sonoma's $275 bread maker sold poorly until they introduced a larger, more expensive model. The original became the "sensible choice" and sales took off
- **Restaurant menus:** Adding an expensive entree no one buys still boosts revenue because customers order the second most expensive dish
- **Television sales:** Displaying three TVs at $690, $850, and $1,480 drives customers to the $850 middle option

## Mechanisms

1. **Comparison asymmetry:** When two options differ on orthogonal attributes (A is better on quality, B on price), choosing is hard. The decoy (-A) makes A **easy to compare** with -A, and the conclusion that A > -A naturally extends to A > B
2. **Relative evaluation:** We lack an absolute value meter. The decoy creates a local reference point that makes the target option shine
3. **Cognitive ease:** The decoy reduces the effort of comparison. Choosing A over -A is effortless; this sense of confidence bleeds into the overall decision

## Applications

- **Pricing strategy:** Offer three tiers where the middle option is the target. The premium tier serves as a decoy, making the middle tier look like great value
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Present your preferred option alongside an inferior variant of it, plus the counterparty's alternative. Your preferred option gains relative attractiveness
- **Menu design:** High-priced items serve as decoys that drive sales of the second-tier items
- **Personal decisions:** Be aware when a "bad" option is included that may be pushing you toward a specific choice. Ask: "Would I make the same decision if this third option didn't exist?"

## Open Questions
- How large does the asymmetry need to be for the decoy to work? Does a barely inferior decoy work as well as a clearly inferior one?
- Does awareness of the decoy effect reduce its power? (Ariely's MIT MBAs were presumably smart yet still influenced)
- How does the decoy effect interact with expertise in a domain?
