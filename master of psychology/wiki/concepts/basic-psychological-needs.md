# Basic Psychological Needs

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Within [[self-determination-theory-framework|SDT]], basic psychological needs are defined as *nutrients that are essential for growth, integrity, and well-being*. They are the psychological analogue of physiological needs (oxygen, water, nutrition). SDT posits exactly three basic psychological needs: [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], [[competence-need|competence]], and [[relatedness-need|relatedness]]. Their satisfaction is universally essential for optimal human functioning, regardless of developmental period, cultural setting, or personal values.

Needs in SDT are defined functionally by the consequences of their satisfaction versus frustration: satisfaction reliably produces growth, integrity, and well-being; frustration reliably produces stagnation, fragmentation, and ill-being. They are *objective* phenomena -- their functional effects operate whether or not the individual consciously values or pursues them (analogous to vitamin C deficiency causing scurvy regardless of dietary preferences).

## Key Findings

- The three needs were initially identified functionally because they integrated results across experiments on [[intrinsic-motivation]] and internalization of [[extrinsic-motivation]]
- Need satisfaction is strongly linked with vitality; need-frustration predicts motivational depletion (Ryan & Deci, 2008a)
- Both between-person differences in need satisfaction and within-person fluctuations in need dynamics predict changes in mood, mental health states, and even physical symptoms (Reis, Sheldon, Gable, Roscoe, & Ryan, 2000; Ryan, Bernstein, & Brown, 2010)
- Need satisfaction predicts wellness; need thwarting explains psychopathology and negative physical health outcomes
- Need frustration produces substitute and compensatory motives (materialism, status seeking, power) that do not truly satisfy basic needs (Kasser, Ryan, Zax, & Sameroff, 1995)
- Parents' use of conditional regard creates conflict between autonomy and relatedness needs, resulting in psychological disturbances (Assor, Roth, & Deci, 2004)
- Baumeister and Leary (1995) proposed nine standards for identifying a basic need; all apply to SDT's three needs. SDT adds additional standards when formally explicating BPNT (Chapter 10).
- SDT's needs differ critically from Murray's (1938) need theory: Murray posited 20+ primary needs (including needs for aggression, exhibition, dominance) which SDT views as preferences or compensatory motives rather than true needs. SDT uses a restrictive, functional definition yielding only three.
- SDT's needs also differ from Hull's (1943) physiological drives: basic psychological needs do not require deficiencies to motivate action (they are growth needs, not deficit needs)

## Mechanisms

The three needs function as essential nutriments for the psychological organism:

1. **[[autonomy-need|Autonomy]]** -- feeling volitional and self-endorsed in one's actions. Not independence. People can be autonomously dependent, independent, or interdependent.

2. **[[competence-need|Competence]]** -- feeling effective in one's interactions with the social environment, experiencing opportunities for exercise and expression of capacities and talents.

3. **[[relatedness-need|Relatedness]]** -- feeling cared about and caring for others; experiencing belonging and connection. Satisfied both in being cared for *and* in caring for others.

When all three needs are supported, the individual shows:
- Robust [[intrinsic-motivation]]
- Full internalization and integration of [[extrinsic-motivation|extrinsic regulations]]
- Vitality, well-being, and psychological health

When any need is frustrated, the individual shows:
- Lowered vitality and loss of volition
- Greater fragmentation and diminished well-being
- Defensive and compensatory behaviors (aggression, greed, power-seeking)
- Psychopathology (obsessive-compulsive patterns, introjective depression, conduct disorders, antisocial personality)

## Applications

- **Needs provide a diagnostic framework:** For any domain (classroom, workplace, family, sports team), one can assess the degree to which the environment supports or thwarts each need, yielding specific prescriptions for improvement
- **Needs bridge psychology and social policy:** The concept of basic psychological needs provides a bridge between individual psychology and broader sociological, economic, and historical systems
- **Clinical application:** Many forms of psychopathology are understood as resulting from developmental deprivations of basic needs (Chapter 16)

## BPNT Formalization (Ch. 10)

Chapter 10 formally introduces [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]] (BPNT) as the fourth SDT mini-theory, with eight propositions:

- **Proposition Ia:** Three needs are essential for optimal development; frustration produces ill-being
- **Proposition Ib:** Need satisfactions vary within persons over time, contexts, and interactions -- fluctuations predict wellness fluctuations at every level of analysis
- **Proposition II:** [[autonomy-support]] facilitates satisfaction of *all three* needs, not just autonomy
- **Proposition III:** Effects operate regardless of whether individuals value or desire the needs (distinguishing BPNT from expectancy-value theories)
- **Proposition IV:** The three needs tend to positively relate; **balance** across needs matters for well-being (Sheldon & Niemiec, 2006 -- four studies)
- **Proposition V:** Deficit needs (security, self-esteem) become salient under threat but do not promote flourishing
- **Proposition VI:** Subjective vitality reflects need satisfaction vs. thwarting; controlling states deplete vitality, need satisfaction enhances it
- **Proposition VII:** Nature exposure enhances vitality, mediated partly by need satisfaction
- **Proposition VIII:** Mindfulness facilitates autonomy, self-regulation, and need satisfaction

BPNT critically distinguishes [[need-satisfaction-vs-frustration|need satisfaction from need frustration]] as partially independent processes: satisfaction predicts growth and wellness via a bright pathway; frustration predicts psychopathology and ill-being via a dark pathway. These are not mere opposites (Bartholomew et al., 2011).

SDT applies six stringent criteria to reject candidate needs: meaning (viewed as an outcome, not a need), self-esteem (an outcome; contingent self-esteem indicates introjection), and security/safety (deficit needs that lack growth properties). See [[summary-3-10-sdt-ch10-bpnt]] for full detail.

## Open Questions

- Are there additional basic psychological needs beyond the three? SDT has found little evidence for any beyond autonomy, competence, and relatedness, but the question remains open. SDT applies six criteria (functional specificity, explanatory necessity, additional variance, growth-not-deficit, appropriate variable type, universality) that candidates like meaning, self-esteem, and security fail to meet.
- How do the three needs interact dynamically? Can surplus satisfaction of one compensate for deficiency in another? Balance across needs matters (Sheldon & Niemiec, 2006), but the main effect of overall satisfaction is larger than the balance effect.
- How do basic psychological needs relate to Maslow's hierarchy? SDT explicitly rejects hierarchical ordering -- all three needs are essential simultaneously.
- What are the evolutionary origins of each need? Recent work by Ryan & Hawley (2016) examines this.
- How does the [[need-satisfaction-vs-frustration|satisfaction vs. frustration distinction]] affect measurement? Instruments should assess both separately rather than using a single bipolar scale.
