# SDT Compensation Design

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

SDT compensation design is the application of [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]] and [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]] principles to the structure of pay, rewards, and incentives in the workplace. The core insight is that compensation has a **functional significance** -- it is experienced by employees as either informational (affirming competence, communicating value) or controlling (pressuring behavior, contingent on compliance) -- and it is this functional significance, not the dollar amount, that determines the motivational impact.

SDT challenges the dominant assumptions of principal-agent theory and expectancy-valence theory that more contingent pay always produces more effort. Instead, the way pay is structured, administered, and communicated differentially affects autonomy, competence, and relatedness need satisfaction, which in turn affects work quality, engagement, and retention.

## Key Findings

### The Four Principles of SDT-Informed Compensation

1. **Pay should support rather than undermine psychological incentives.** Making the controlling aspects of pay salient (emphasizing the contingency between behavior and pay) is likely to undermine [[autonomous-work-motivation]] and have negative consequences for engagement, performance, and wellness
2. **Performance-contingent compensation has high risk of being experienced as controlling.** The Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis found performance-contingent rewards to be the most detrimental contingency. Field studies confirm: Kuvaas (2006), Kuvaas et al. (2016). Frey & Osterloh (2005) argue fixed pay is more effective
3. **Pay must be equitable** -- high in distributive and procedural justice (Colquitt & Greenberg, 2003). Multiple inputs determine equity: education, training, tenure, quality of work, teamwork -- not just output metrics. Effective managers determine equity and communicate it in a need-supportive way
4. **Managers supporting basic psychological needs is more important and effective in motivating performance than using pay as a motivator.** Autonomy support predicts need satisfaction, intrinsic motivation, and procedural justice; pay level does not (Olafsen et al., 2015)

### Field Evidence on Pay Structures

- **Olafsen, Halvari, Forest, & Deci (2015):** Pay level related to distributive justice only. **Pay level did not relate to procedural justice, need satisfaction, or intrinsic motivation.** Autonomy support predicted all three
- **Kuvaas et al. (2016) -- Norwegian insurance companies:**
  - Base-pay (not contingent on performance): positively predicted autonomous motivation, unrelated to controlled motivation
  - Pay-for-performance plans: **negatively predicted autonomous motivation, positively predicted controlled motivation**
  - Autonomous motivation: strongly predicted effort, negatively predicted desire to leave
  - Controlled motivation: weakly predicted effort, positively predicted desire to leave
  - **Net result:** Performance-contingent pay led to less work effort and greater turnover intentions
- **Harrison, Virick, & William (1996):** Sales employees on pure commission -- three-quarters left within a year. Extremely high turnover for both high and low performers
- **Kuvaas, Dysvik, & Buch (2014):** Extrinsic motivation (working for money) and intrinsic motivation (working for interest) were **negatively correlated** in gas stations and financial organizations -- field-level [[undermining-effect]]

### The Undermining Effect in Organizational Settings

- **Gubler, Larkin, & Pierce (2016) -- "Motivational spillovers":** An attendance award program at an industrial laundry plant:
  - Direct positive effect on targeted attendance
  - Strategically gamed by workers
  - No lasting internalization
  - **Negatively affected previously excellent attenders** (reduced punctuality during ineligibility)
  - **"Crowded out" performance in non-targeted tasks** -- previously above-average employees lost efficiency in daily laundry tasks
  - The awards program **hurt the best employees most**
- **Frey (1993):** Undermining can "spill over" -- if rewards undermine autonomy in one work domain, the individual may generalize an extrinsic focus and lower autonomy across other domains

### When Rewards Do Not Undermine

- **Task-noncontingent** rewards (Deci, 1972a): Not detrimental
- **Unexpected** rewards (Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett, 1973): Not detrimental
- **Non-salient** rewards (Ross, 1975): Not detrimental
- **Performance-contingent rewards in an autonomy-supportive context** (Ryan, Mims, & Koestner, 1983): Can actually **enhance** intrinsic motivation by conveying competence informationally
- **Reward choice** (Caza, McCarter, & Northcraft, 2015): Giving employees meaningful choice over compensation components enhances productivity (but only when options are attractive)

### Task Type Matters

- **Cerasoli, Nicklin, & Ford (2014) meta-analysis:** Intrinsic motivation is a stronger predictor of performance on **heuristic tasks** (where quality matters); extrinsic rewards are better predictors on **algorithmic tasks** (where quantity matters)
- **Weibel, Rost, & Osterloh (2010) meta-analysis (public sector):** Pay-for-performance improved performance on simple tasks but **impaired performance on more complex tasks**
- **Implication:** The more complex, creative, or judgment-dependent the work, the more dangerous performance-contingent pay becomes

### CEO Pay

- CEO stock-option bonuses are **outcome-focused rewards** -- they reinforce any route to the target (stock price), fueling shortcuts with "considerable collateral damage"
- Research results on CEO outcome-focused pay are negative or mixed (Cooper, Gulen, & Rau, 2014; Hou, Priem, & Goranova, 2017)
- **Enron** exemplifies how outcome-focused rewards can incentivize deception and destruction (Ryan & Brown, 2005)

## Mechanisms

1. **Functional significance:** Every compensation event is interpreted as informational or controlling. This interpretation -- not the dollar amount -- is the proximal determinant of motivational impact
2. **Autonomy thwarting:** Making pay contingent on specific behaviors shifts the perceived locus of causality from internal to external, undermining autonomy
3. **Competence signaling:** Pay can affirm competence (informational) or pressure behavior (controlling). The same amount of money can do either, depending on how it is administered
4. **Relatedness disruption:** Performance incentives to individuals or groups can disrupt relatedness and teamwork, especially when people compete internally for bonuses or recognition
5. **Equity perception:** Unfair or opaque pay damages all three needs; equitable pay communicated supportively protects them

## Applications

### SDT-Aligned Compensation Practices
- **Favor base pay over performance-contingent pay** for complex, heuristic work
- **Administer rewards in an autonomy-supportive manner** -- acknowledge competence, avoid using pay as a controlling lever
- **Ensure equity and transparency** -- unfair pay damages motivation regardless of amount
- **Offer reward choice** -- let employees choose aspects of their compensation package
- **Provide rationale** for compensation decisions to support internalization
- **Decouple pay from micro-behaviors** -- avoid tying compensation to specific task behaviors; instead link to broad contributions
- **Use pay to signal organizational values** -- the careful offer of rewards can convey that the organization values certain activities (Kuvaas, Buch, Gagne, Dysvik, & Forest, 2017)
- **Beware motivational spillovers** -- reward programs targeting one behavior can undermine motivation for other behaviors

### What NOT to Do
- Do not use performance-contingent bonuses as the primary motivator for complex work
- Do not make pay differentials highly salient or competitive
- Do not assume that increasing pay will increase motivation -- it often will not (Olafsen et al., 2015)
- Do not design "outcome-focused" incentives that reward any path to a metric (the Enron problem)
