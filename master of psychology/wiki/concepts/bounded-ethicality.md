# Bounded Ethicality

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition
Bounded ethicality refers to the systematic and predictable psychological processes that lead people to engage in ethically questionable behaviors that are inconsistent with their own preferred ethics (Chugh, Bazerman, & Banaji, 2005). Unlike deliberate corruption, bounded ethicality operates below conscious awareness -- managers develop protective cognitions that lead them to harmful behaviors they would condemn upon reflection.

The term parallels Simon's [[bounded-rationality]]: just as cognitive limitations prevent fully rational decisions, ethical limitations prevent fully ethical behavior -- even among well-intentioned people.

## Key Findings
- Most unethical behavior occurs without the actor's conscious intent or awareness -- the traditional ethics focus on deliberate wrongdoing misses the larger problem
- Chugh (2004): The fast-paced demands of managerial life exacerbate bounded ethicality because they demand [[system-1-system-2|System 1]] speed and decisiveness, which allows biases to operate unchecked
- Supreme Court Justice Scalia denied his duck-hunting trip with Vice President Cheney could bias his judgment in Cheney v. U.S. District Court -- illustrating how even the most analytically sophisticated individuals believe themselves immune
- High self-regard keeps people from monitoring their own behavior, making bounded ethicality more likely (Banaji et al., 2003)
- Ethics training that focuses only on explicit unethical behavior is insufficient; it must address unconscious biases to be effective (Tenbrunsel & Messick, 2004)

## Mechanisms
Bounded ethicality manifests through six documented channels:
1. **[[overclaiming-credit]]** -- Systematically overestimating one's own contributions
2. **[[in-group-favoritism]]** -- Favoring similar others, which functionally discriminates against out-groups
3. **[[implicit-bias]]** -- Unconscious stereotypes that influence behavior despite conscious egalitarian beliefs
4. **[[indirectly-unethical-behavior]]** -- Enabling harmful outcomes through intermediaries or willful ignorance
5. **Sacred value conflicts** -- Emotional reactions to value tradeoffs that override rational analysis
6. **[[conflicts-of-interest]]** -- Inability to provide objective judgment when personal incentives are at stake

The common thread: [[system-1-system-2|System 1]] thinking allows these biases to develop without triggering the conscious ethical monitoring that System 2 would provide.

## Applications
- **Organizational design:** Structure systems to reduce reliance on individual ethical vigilance -- institutional controls beat individual willpower
- **Self-awareness:** Accept that you are not immune to bounded ethicality; monitor decision contexts where conflicts exist
- **Slow down:** When ethical dimensions are present, engage [[system-1-system-2|System 2]] thinking deliberately
- **Perspective-taking:** Actively consider how decisions affect out-groups, not just in-groups
- **Policy over disclosure:** Disclosure of conflicts is insufficient and can backfire; structural separation of conflicting roles is more effective
