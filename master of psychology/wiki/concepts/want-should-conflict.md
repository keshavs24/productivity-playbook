# Want/Should Conflict

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The want/should conflict describes the internal tension between two motivational systems: the **"want" self** (impulsive, emotional, present-focused, System 1) that seeks immediate gratification, and the **"should" self** (reflective, rational, future-focused, System 2) that pursues long-term interests. Most self-control failures stem from the "want" self overpowering the "should" self, particularly in the present moment.

## Key Findings

- **Schelling (1984):** People behave like two individuals -- "one who wants clear lungs and long life and another who adores tobacco." This explains the paradox of simultaneous industries for smoking AND quitting, dieting AND obesity, drugs AND rehab. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch7]])
- **McClure et al. (2004):** Different brain regions activate for immediate vs. delayed rewards. System 1 (amygdala, basal ganglia) handles immediate/emotional responses. System 2 (prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate) handles deliberative thought. Immediate rewards activate emotional centers; delayed rewards activate the rational prefrontal cortex.
- **Shiv, Loewenstein, Bechara, Damasio, & Damasio (2005):** People with damage to emotional brain regions sometimes make MORE rational decisions -- they select options with higher expected value because they are less frightened by potential losses.
- **Milkman, Rogers, & Bazerman (2007) movie rental study:** When ordering DVDs for future delivery, people chose "should" movies (documentaries). When movies arrived, they watched "want" movies (comedies). "Should" movies stayed unplayed significantly longer than "want" movies.
- **Rogers, Milkman, & Bazerman (2007) grocery study:** Longer lead time between order and delivery = more vegetables ("should" items). Shorter lead time = more ice cream ("want" items).
- **Rogers & Bazerman (2008):** Support for "should" policies like gas taxes goes up significantly when implementation is in the future rather than immediate.
- **Khan & Dhar (2006, 2007):** The "should" self makes New Year's resolutions; the "want" self breaks them one at a time. The "should" self is weaker when tired or distracted (Danziger, Levav, & AvnaimPesso, 2011).

## Mechanisms

The want/should distinction maps onto multiple established frameworks:
- **System 1 / System 2** (Stanovich & West, 2000; Kahneman, 2003): "Want" = System 1, "should" = System 2
- **Hyperbolic discounting** (Laibson, 1994): The "want" self overweights the present, producing [[hyperbolic-discounting]]
- **Separate vs. joint evaluation:** The "want" self dominates separate evaluation (one option at a time, affect-driven). The "should" self dominates joint evaluation (comparison triggers analytical thinking) -- explaining [[preference-reversal]]

Reconciliation strategies:
1. **Pre-commitment** (Thaler & Shefrin, 1981): The "should" self as planner constrains the "want" self as doer (Ulysses and the Sirens, Antabuse)
2. **Alignment** (Raiffa, 1968): Communication between the two selves to find what the "want" self can reveal about genuine needs
3. **Internal negotiation:** Treat the two selves as negotiating parties. Agreements must be Pareto efficient, within the "want" self's bargaining zone, and honor reasonable limits

## Applications

- **Productivity:** Design systems that make "should" behaviors easier and "want" temptations harder to access (commitment devices, habit stacking)
- **Policy design:** Implement "should" policies with future effective dates to gain support while emotions about present costs are weaker
- **Diet/health:** Pre-plan meals (future = "should" dominates) rather than deciding in the moment (present = "want" dominates)
- **Financial planning:** Automate savings (pre-commitment device) to prevent the "want" self from spending

## Ariely Extensions (Predictably Irrational, Chs. 6-7)

Ariely provides powerful experimental evidence for the want/should conflict:

- **Hot-cold empathy gap (Ch. 6):** The "should" self (cold state) cannot predict the behavior of the "want" self (hot state). In sexual arousal experiments, participants' willingness to engage in risky/immoral behavior increased 72-136% compared to their cold-state predictions. "Prevention, protection, conservatism, and morality disappeared completely from the radar screen." ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch6-arousal]])
- **Procrastination as want/should conflict (Ch. 7):** Ariely's three-class experiment showed that students recognized the conflict (voluntarily setting early deadlines) but couldn't resolve it optimally. Externally imposed constraints (dictatorial deadlines) outperformed self-imposed ones. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch7-procrastination]])
- **Reward pairing strategy:** Ariely paired his "should" behavior (interferon injections) with his "want" reward (watching movies), creating a positive association that helped him complete a painful medical protocol that every other participant abandoned. This is a practical implementation of recruiting the "want" self for "should" goals.
- **Variable reinforcement and the "want" self:** Email checking operates as a Skinner-box variable-ratio reinforcement schedule -- the "want" self is addicted to the unpredictable reward, overriding the "should" self's preference for focused work.

## Phase 3 Connections: SDT Perspective

The want/should conflict maps onto several SDT constructs:

- **[[intrinsic-aspirations]] vs. [[extrinsic-aspirations]]:** The "want" self may pursue extrinsic gratification (wealth, fame, image) while the "should" self endorses intrinsic values (personal growth, community). However, [[goal-contents-theory|GCT]] reframes this: intrinsic goals, when truly integrated, are both wanted *and* endorsed -- they are autonomously pursued. Extrinsic goals are more often the product of [[need-frustration|need thwarting]] and controlled motivation ([[introjected-regulation]]). The real conflict is between controlled extrinsic pursuits and autonomous intrinsic ones.
- **Controlled vs. autonomous self-regulation:** SDT distinguishes self-*control* (introjected, pressured "shoulds") from self-*regulation* (autonomous, integrated). Self-control depletes vitality (Moller, Deci, & Ryan, 2006); autonomous self-regulation does not. This suggests the want/should framing may be overly dichotomous -- the goal is not to strengthen the "should" self but to integrate the "want" and "should" selves into an autonomous whole.
- **Ego depletion reinterpreted:** [[basic-psychological-needs-theory|BPNT]] challenges the strength model underlying the want/should conflict. Controlled regulation (pressured compliance with "shoulds") depletes vitality; autonomous regulation (pursuing genuinely endorsed goals) can maintain or enhance it. True choice does not deplete; compelled choice does.

## Open Questions
- Is the "want"/"should" distinction neurally clean, or is it more of a continuum?
- Can the "want" self be recruited for long-term goals through gamification and intrinsic reward design? (Ariely's reward-pairing strategy suggests yes)
- How do cultural norms about self-control (e.g., collectivistic vs. individualistic) affect the balance?
- Does SDT's integration model (making "shoulds" into genuine "wants" through internalization) provide a more effective long-term resolution than precommitment devices that merely constrain the "want" self?
