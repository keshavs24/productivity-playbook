# Commodity Theory

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Commodity theory is a framework for understanding persuasion developed by psychologists Timothy Brock (1968) and Howard Fromkin (Fromkin & Brock, 1971). Its central claim is that exclusive information — information perceived as scarce or restricted in availability — is more persuasive than widely available information. The theory extends the [[scarcity-principle]] beyond physical commodities to the realm of knowledge, messages, and communications.

## Key Findings

- **Information need not be censored to gain persuasive power — it need only be scarce.** A piece of information is found more persuasive when we think we can't get it elsewhere.
- **The Knishinsky beef study (1982)** — the most striking empirical demonstration: A beef-importing company's salespeople called buyers in three conditions:
  1. Standard sales pitch → baseline orders
  2. Standard pitch + beef supply will be scarce → **2x** baseline orders
  3. Standard pitch + beef will be scarce + "this information came from exclusive contacts" → **6x** baseline orders
- The "scarcity double-whammy": when both the *product* and the *information about the product* are framed as scarce, the effect is dramatically amplified
- The information provided was always true (there was an impending foreign beef shortage, and it had come through exclusive contacts), but the framing of its exclusivity was the active ingredient

## Mechanisms

1. **Scarcity heuristic applied to information**: Just as scarce goods are assumed to be more valuable, scarce information is assumed to be more accurate, important, or actionable
2. **Insider status**: Being told information is exclusive creates a sense of privileged access, activating both the [[scarcity-principle]] and social identity (being in the "know")
3. **Reduced counter-arguing**: Exclusive information may bypass the critical scrutiny applied to widely available claims, because the recipient feels specially trusted

## Applications

- **Sales and marketing**: Frame product information as exclusive, insider knowledge rather than mass-market messaging
- **Negotiation**: Sharing "confidential" information with a counterpart can increase its persuasive impact
- **Defense**: When information is presented as exclusive or restricted, ask whether the exclusivity adds to the information's *truth value* or merely to its *perceived value*

## Open Questions

- How does commodity theory interact with the modern information environment, where "exclusive" information rapidly becomes public through social media? Does the window of perceived exclusivity shrink, and does this reduce persuasive power?
- Is there a threshold of exclusivity below which the effect reverses (i.e., information so exclusive that it seems implausible or suspicious)?
