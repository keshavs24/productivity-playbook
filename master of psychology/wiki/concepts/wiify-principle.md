# WIIFY Principle

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

WIIFY -- pronounced "whiff-ee" -- stands for "What's In It For You" and captures the principle that effective communication must make the audience's self-interest explicit and tangible. Coined by Jerry Weissman (a former TV producer who coaches CEOs on presentations), WIIFY should be a central aspect of every speech, pitch, or appeal. The principle goes beyond merely mentioning benefits -- it requires framing them in concrete, personal terms that the audience can immediately visualize and feel. However, as the [[heath-brothers]] demonstrate in [[made-to-stick]], pure self-interest is only part of the motivational picture; identity-based appeals and appeals to higher levels of Maslow's hierarchy can be equally or more powerful.

## Key Findings

- **John Caples's mail-order advertising:** The greatest direct-mail copywriter of all time emphasized that self-interest should appear in every headline. His most famous headline -- "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano... But When I Started to Play!" -- has been imitated for decades. Caples's key insight: companies emphasize features when they should emphasize benefits. People do not buy quarter-inch drill bits; they buy quarter-inch holes so they can hang their children's pictures
- **Tempe cable TV study (1982):** Homeowners received two versions of cable TV benefits. Group 1 heard abstract, third-person benefits ("a person can plan in advance to enjoy events offered"). Group 2 was asked to imagine themselves using cable ("Take a moment and imagine how CATV will provide you with..."). Group 1 subscribed at 20% (same as the neighborhood average). Group 2 subscribed at 47%. The research paper was subtitled "Does Imagining Make It So?" -- yes, it does
- **The tangibility finding:** It is the tangibility of the benefit, not its magnitude, that drives interest. The cable TV study offered modest benefits (avoiding babysitter costs), but because people could easily imagine themselves enjoying them, the benefits became compelling. You do not have to promise riches; it is enough to promise reasonable benefits that people can easily imagine themselves enjoying
- **Caples's rule:** Keep the "self" in self-interest. "Don't say, 'People will enjoy a sense of security when they use Goodyear Tires.' Say, 'You enjoy a sense of security when you use Goodyear Tires.'" The shift from third person to second person makes the benefit personal and immediate

## Mechanisms

1. **Self-referencing effect:** When information is processed in relation to the self ("you" rather than "people"), it is encoded more deeply and remembered better -- a well-established finding in memory research
2. **Visualization and simulation:** Asking someone to imagine themselves benefiting from something triggers [[story-as-simulation]] mechanisms -- the same brain regions that would be activated by actual experience. This is why the cable TV "imagine" condition was more than twice as effective
3. **Benefit of the benefit:** Most communicators stop at the feature or even the first-level benefit. WIIFY requires drilling down to the ultimate personal payoff: not "quarter-inch drill bits" (feature), not "quarter-inch holes" (benefit), but "hanging your children's pictures" (benefit of the benefit)
4. **Loss of self-referencing in abstract language:** Abstract language strips away the personal connection. "Broader entertainment and informational service" does not activate self-referencing; "imagine spending your time at home, with your family, alone, or with your friends" does

## Applications

- **Presentations and speeches:** Jerry Weissman coaches CEOs to make WIIFY central, not buried. Even if the audience can connect the dots between a feature and its benefit, they may not have time to do so before the speaker moves on
- **Teaching:** "How are we ever going to use this?" is the WIIFY question. If algebra made students better at video games, no teacher would hesitate to say so. The best answer to "why study algebra?" is not abstract ("provides methods for moving from the specific to the general") but personal and tangible ("math is mental weight training -- you lift weights so you can carry your groceries and your grandchildren")
- **Marketing:** Emphasize benefits, not features. Frame benefits in second person. Drill down to the benefit of the benefit
- **Fundraising:** Imagine yourself as a sponsor of Rokia -- "you've got a picture of her on your desk, next to your kids' pictures. You've traded letters three times" -- this combines WIIFY with the [[mother-teresa-principle]]

### WIIFY Is Not Enough: Identity and Maslow's Upper Floors

- **The popcorn popper disaster:** A marketer offered firefighters free popcorn poppers for reviewing a fire safety film. The firefighters were furious -- the offer violated their identity as people who care about safety for its own sake, not for bribes
- **Donald Kinder's research:** Self-interest has a "trifling" effect on political views. People vote based on group interest and identity, not personal gain
- **James March's two decision-making models:** (1) Consequences model -- rational cost-benefit analysis, (2) Identity model -- "Who am I? What kind of situation is this? What do people like me do?" People often decide based on identity, not calculation
- **"Don't Mess with Texas":** Appealed to Texan identity (real Texans do not litter), not self-interest. Litter decreased 72% in five years
- **Maslow's Basement error:** People are personally motivated by Esteem and Learning (upper floors of Maslow), but they predict others are motivated by Security and Physical needs (basement). Floyd Lee, the Baghdad mess hall cook, appealed to Transcendence: "I am in charge of morale, not food." This was far more motivating than any self-interest appeal could have been
- **Lesson:** Self-interest is a reliable but limited palette. Always consider identity-based appeals and higher Maslow levels as alternatives or supplements

## Open Questions

- How do WIIFY and identity-based appeals interact when they point in different directions (e.g., when the self-interested choice conflicts with the identity-consistent choice)?
- Does the effectiveness of WIIFY vary by culture -- particularly in collectivist cultures where explicit self-interest appeals may be less socially acceptable?
- Is the Tempe cable TV finding replicable in digital marketing contexts, where people are already bombarded with personalized "imagine yourself" messaging?
