# Active Commitment

**Phase(s):** 1, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Active commitment refers to the principle that behavioral, action-based commitments (doing something, writing something, saying something) are far more effective at shaping self-image and future behavior than passive commitments (merely deciding in one's mind, or failing to opt out). The act of *doing* provides evidence to oneself about one's own beliefs and values.

## Key Findings

- **Active vs. passive volunteering**: Students who actively opted in to an AIDS education project (filling out a form stating they wanted to participate) showed up at **74%**, while those who passively opted in (by not filling out a form saying they didn't want to participate) showed up at much lower rates. Active volunteers also explained their decisions in terms of personal values and traits (Allison & Messick, 1988; Fazio, Sherman, & Herr, 1982; Cioffi & Garner, 1996).
- **Written commitments are especially powerful**: Writing provides physical evidence of the act, is harder to deny than verbal statements, and can be shown to others (Jones & Harris, 1967; Gawronski, 2003).
- **Self-perception theory (Bem, 1972)**: People use their own behavior as primary evidence of their beliefs and attitudes. Actions speak louder than intentions — to ourselves as well as to others.
- **Chinese POW writing tactics**: The Chinese always pushed prisoners to *write* their statements, even if it meant copying from notebooks. Written commitments served double duty — internal self-perception and external persuasion of others.
- **Business applications**: Amway's "write it down" goal-setting; having customers fill out their own sales agreements to reduce cancellations; testimonial contest essays.

## Mechanisms

1. **Self-perception**: We observe our own actions and infer our attitudes from them (Bem, 1972; Vallacher & Wegner, 1985). An active commitment provides clear behavioral evidence of "who I am."
2. **Irrevocability**: An action taken cannot be undone the way a thought can be revised. Writing creates a permanent record.
3. **Social proof of intent**: Written and spoken commitments are observable by others, who then assume the commitments reflect genuine beliefs — even when the commitments were coerced (Jones & Harris, 1967).
4. **Cognitive consistency pressure**: Having acted, the person adjusts beliefs and attitudes to align with the action, avoiding the dissonance of acting contrary to one's beliefs.

## Applications

- **Sales**: Get the customer to fill out the order form themselves, not the salesperson.
- **Goal-setting**: Write goals down — "There is something magical about writing things down" (Amway).
- **Behavior change**: Active opt-in procedures produce more lasting commitment than passive (default) enrollment.
- **Indoctrination**: The Chinese POW program demonstrates the dark-side application — forcing written statements that progressively shift self-image.
