# Myopic Loss Aversion

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Myopic loss aversion is the combination of two behavioral tendencies: (1) **[[loss-aversion]]** -- losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good, and (2) **narrow bracketing** -- the tendency to evaluate outcomes frequently and separately rather than aggregating them over longer time horizons. The interaction of these two tendencies leads people to be excessively conservative in their risk-taking, particularly in financial contexts.

## Key Findings

- **Explains the equity premium puzzle (Benartzi & Thaler, 1995):** Stocks have historically outperformed bonds by ~6% per year, yet investors demand this enormous premium. If investors evaluate their portfolios daily, they observe frequent small losses (stocks decline about 46% of trading days). Loss aversion makes these daily losses painful. Annual evaluation produces fewer loss observations and more willingness to hold stocks. ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch14-mental-accounting-matters]])
- **Evaluation frequency matters:** The attractiveness of a risky investment depends on the time horizon of evaluation. The same stock portfolio is attractive with annual evaluation but unattractive with daily evaluation, purely due to how losses are experienced.
- **Samuelson's colleague paradox:** A colleague rejected a single 50-50 bet to win $200 or lose $100 (loss aversion) but was willing to accept 100 such bets. Narrow bracketing prevents the aggregation that would make the single bet attractive as part of a portfolio.

## Mechanisms

1. **[[loss-aversion]]** makes any individual loss disproportionately painful
2. **Narrow bracketing** ([[mental-accounting]]) means each observation period is evaluated as a separate mental account
3. **Frequent evaluation** maximizes the number of observations of losses, since risky assets fluctuate frequently
4. The combination produces a demand for a large risk premium to compensate for the pain of observing frequent losses

If investors evaluated their stock returns less frequently (annually rather than daily), they would observe gains more often than losses (stocks rise in most years), and the demanded risk premium would be much lower.

## Applications

- **Investment behavior:** Investors who check their portfolios less often tend to hold more stocks and earn higher returns
- **Retirement savings design:** Default investment options and evaluation frequency in 401(k) plans affect asset allocation
- **Organizational decision-making:** Managers who evaluate projects frequently may reject positive-expected-value projects because short-term losses are painful
- **Personal productivity:** Evaluating progress too frequently can make setbacks feel like losses and discourage persistence
