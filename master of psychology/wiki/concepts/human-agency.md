# Human Agency

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Human agency is the capacity to exercise control over one's own functioning and over environmental events through intentional action. Agency refers to acts done intentionally -- the power to originate actions for given purposes. It must be distinguished from the mechanical power to make things happen: a person who smashes a set of precariously displayed dishes upon being tripped by another shopper would not be considered the agent of the event (Davidson, 1971).

A key distinction: one must separate the personal production of action for an intended outcome from the effects that carrying out that course of action actually produces. Many actions are performed in the belief that they will bring about a desired outcome, but they actually produce outcomes that were neither intended nor wanted. The killing of Polonius by Hamlet illustrates this -- the action was intentional but the wrong victim was the consequence. Agency is about intentional origination of action, not about whether consequences match intentions.

## Key Findings

- Beliefs of personal efficacy constitute the key factor of human agency -- if people believe they have no power to produce results, they will not attempt to make things happen ([[summary-3-11-se-ch1-theoretical-perspectives]])
- Agency operates within [[reciprocal-determinism]], not in a causal vacuum -- personal agency functions within a broad network of sociostructural influences
- Social cognitive theory rejects the dualistic view of self as agent and self as object -- the self is simultaneously agent and object in self-reflection and self-influence
- Thought processes are emergent brain activities that exert determinative influence -- they are not ontologically reducible to lower-level neural events even though they are realized in neural systems (emergent interactive agency)
- Agency extends from individual to collective: collective efficacy is an emergent group-level attribute, not simply the sum of individual efficacy beliefs

## Mechanisms

Human agency operates through several core capabilities:

1. **Intentionality:** The power to originate actions for given purposes
2. **Forethought:** The ability to anticipate likely consequences of actions and set goals
3. **Self-reactiveness:** The capacity to construct appropriate courses of action and to motivate and regulate their execution through self-regulatory processes
4. **Self-reflectiveness:** The ability to reflect on one's own functioning, evaluate the adequacy of one's thoughts and actions, and make corrective adjustments

Agency can be exercised in three forms:
- **Direct personal agency:** Mobilizing one's own skills and resources to produce desired outcomes
- **Proxy agency:** Getting those who wield influence and power to act on one's behalf (when personal control is not possible or is relinquished)
- **Collective agency:** Acting together through shared belief in collective capabilities to produce desired outcomes

## Applications

- **Empowerment:** Building human agency requires developing personal efficacy, not just providing resources -- people must believe they can use those resources effectively
- **Avoiding proxy dependence:** Low self-efficacy fosters reliance on proxy control, which further reduces opportunities to build skills -- a self-reinforcing cycle of disempowerment
- **Collective action:** Collective efficacy is necessary for social change -- inveterate self-doubters are not easily organized into a collectively efficacious force
- **Education:** People are proactive, aspiring organisms who have a hand in shaping their own lives -- not onlooking hosts of brain mechanisms orchestrated by environmental events
