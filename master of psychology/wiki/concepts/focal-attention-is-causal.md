# Focal Attention Is Causal

**Phase(s):** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The what's-focal-is-presumed-causal effect is the human tendency to assign causal status to whatever is most perceptually salient at the moment of judgment. Beyond merely being deemed *important* (the [[focusing-illusion]]), focal elements gain standing as *causes* — as answers to the question "Why?" This effect is automatic, cross-cultural, resistant to correction, and operates in domains ranging from lottery number selection to criminal justice.

## Key Findings

- **Taylor's seating studies**: Whichever discussant's face was more visible was judged to have "had more influence" and been "more causal" — even when conversations were scripted so neither dominated. The effect was automatic, cross-gender, persistent across delays, and appeared in both live and videotaped interactions.
- **Lassiter's interrogation camera studies**: A camera aimed at the suspect (standard police practice) leads observers — including judges and law enforcement — to assign greater guilt. A side-angle camera eliminates the bias. A camera aimed at the interrogator reverses it.
- **Tylenol lottery numbers**: After the 1982 Chicago cyanide poisonings, the production lot numbers (2880, 1910) were bet at such unprecedented rates that three states halted wagering. Despite being associated with *death*, the numbers' focal prominence made people perceive them as having "causal properties — to have the ability to make events occur."
- **Oberholzer-Gee line-cutting**: Money appeared to be the cause of compliance (more money = more compliance), but almost no one accepted the money. The hidden cause — the norm of social responsibility — was less visible and therefore underestimated.
- **Romance of leadership**: CEOs are assigned outsized credit/blame for organizational outcomes because they are visually prominent. Other factors (workforce quality, business systems, market conditions) have greater impact but are less focal.

## Mechanisms

The effect operates through a normally adaptive heuristic: we typically direct attention to the genuine causes of events around us. The brain therefore learns to equate "I'm paying attention to this" with "this is probably a cause." Communicators can exploit this by making a selected factor salient, thereby loading it with perceived causal power.

Two practical consequences for influence:
1. **Importance**: Focal elements are judged as more important
2. **Causality**: Focal elements are judged as more causal
Together, these make audiences more *receptive* to considering a focal argument — the biggest challenge in persuasion is often not building a strong case but getting audiences to devote time and energy to considering its merits.

## Applications

- **Criminal justice**: Require equal-angle camera placement in interrogation recordings to prevent camera-angle bias from producing false guilt attributions
- **Leadership attribution**: Recognize that a leader's visible position inflates their perceived causal role; use structured decision-making to correct for this bias
- **Persuasion**: Draw attention to the most favorable feature of your offer, knowing that focal attention will automatically load it with both importance and causal power
- **Self-defense**: If ever in a police interrogation, find the camera and move your chair so both faces appear equally in the recording
