# Extensionality

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Extensionality is Kenneth Arrow's (1982) term for the **invariance principle** in rational choice theory: the requirement that different representations of the same choice problem should yield the same preference. Two characterizations that a decision-maker would, on reflection, view as alternative descriptions of the same problem should lead to the same choice -- even without the benefit of such reflection.

Extensionality is so fundamental to rational choice that it is typically **tacitly assumed** in the characterization of options rather than explicitly stated as a testable axiom. Decision models that describe objects of choice as random variables all assume that alternative representations of the same random variables should be treated alike.

## Key Findings

- Arrow (1982) formalized this as a named principle, highlighting its foundational role
- Tversky and Kahneman (1986) demonstrated that extensionality is **systematically violated** through [[framing-effects]], making it the most normatively compelling but descriptively invalid axiom of rational choice ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch12-rational-choice-framing]])
- A related concept, consequentialism (Hammond 1985), requires that choices depend only on consequences, not on the process or path by which they are reached

## Mechanisms

Extensionality fails because humans do not spontaneously reduce problems to their canonical form. Instead, they evaluate problems **as presented**, and the presentation determines the reference point, which determines the coding of outcomes as gains or losses, which (via [[prospect-theory]]) determines preferences. The computational requirements for recognizing equivalent representations are often beyond unaided cognitive capacity.
