# Organismic Integration

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Organismic integration is the inherent developmental process by which living entities, under supportive conditions, progress toward increased differentiation and integration (Jacob, 1973; Kauffmann, 2000; Maturana & Varela, 1992; Mayr, 1982). In SDT, it refers to the natural tendency of human beings to actively assimilate events and experiences, integrate them into existing psychological structures, and elaborate those structures in the direction of organized complexity and unified functioning.

This tendency is evident from infancy in exploratory urges, social interest, and the striving to assimilate and integrate experiences within social groups. Through transformations across the lifespan, self-organization remains central to healthy functioning (Cicchetti, 2006; Ryan, Kuhl & Deci, 1997; Vansteenkiste & Ryan, 2013).

## Key Findings

- The principle is consistent with cognitive-developmental theory (Piaget, 1971; Werner, 1948), humanistic psychology (Goldstein, 1939; Rogers, 1963), and psychodynamic approaches (Freud, 1923; Loevinger, 1976)
- The integrative propensity is *conditional* -- it requires social and environmental support for [[basic-psychological-needs]] satisfaction. Without need support, fragmentation and defense occur instead of integration.
- Integration manifests in two core processes: [[intrinsic-motivation]] (the propensity to explore, manipulate, and understand) and *internalization* (the propensity to assimilate social norms and regulations into self-regulation)
- When integration fails, the result is inner division, alienation, fragmentation, and controlled or defensive functioning

## Mechanisms

Organismic integration operates through:
1. **Intrinsic motivation** -- spontaneous exploration and engagement with the environment, producing assimilation and elaboration of psychological structures
2. **Internalization** -- taking in and transforming external social regulations into self-regulations
3. **Integration** -- the most complete form of internalization, where values and regulations are fully assimilated into one's coherent sense of self

The process requires satisfaction of [[basic-psychological-needs]]:
- [[autonomy-need|Autonomy]] support facilitates volitional engagement
- [[competence-need|Competence]] support provides the optimal challenge for growth
- [[relatedness-need|Relatedness]] support provides the social connection that fuels internalization

## Applications

- Understanding organismic integration helps explain why controlling environments produce fragmentation and why supportive environments produce coherent, integrated personality functioning
- Clinical implications: psychopathology can be understood as failures of integration (Chapter 16)
- Educational implications: learning is itself an integrative process requiring need support
