# What Happened? Conversation

**Phase(s):** 4, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The "What Happened?" Conversation is the first and most prominent of the three conversations in the [[three-conversations-framework]]. It concerns disagreement about facts, events, intentions, and responsibility — who said what, who's right, who meant what, who's to blame. This is where most time in difficult conversations is spent, and it contains three predictable errors that distort understanding.

## Key Findings

### The Three Errors

**1. The Truth Assumption** — The foundational error: "I am right, you are wrong." We build our entire stance on the assumption that our version of events is correct. But difficult conversations are almost never about objective facts — they are about conflicting perceptions, interpretations, and values. The question is not "What's true?" but "What's important?" Two people can agree on every fact and still disagree about meaning, significance, and what should be done. ([[summary-4-15-dc-ch1-three-conversations]])

The antidote: shift from proving you are right to exploring how each person is making sense of the situation. Offer your views as perceptions and values, not as "the truth."

**2. The Intention Invention** — We assume we know the other person's intentions, but intentions are invisible. We invent them from the impact of their behavior on us, and we almost always assume the worst. This maps directly onto the [[fundamental-attribution-error]]. The antidote: recognize the gap between impact and intent, hold your assumptions as hypotheses, and ask rather than assert. See [[intent-impact-gap]]. ([[summary-4-15-dc-ch3-intent-and-impact]])

**3. The Blame Frame** — We focus on who's to blame, which produces defensiveness, denial, and either/or thinking. Blame looks backward at who caused the problem; [[contribution-system]] analysis looks forward at how to prevent recurrence. The antidote: replace the blame question ("Whose fault is it?") with the contribution question ("How did we each contribute to this situation?"). ([[summary-4-15-dc-ch1-three-conversations]])

### Why Our Stories Diverge

Chapter 2 identifies three systematic reasons people construct different stories about the same events ([[summary-4-15-dc-ch2-explore-stories]]):

1. **Different information** — We notice different things (selective attention shaped by identity and interests) and we each have private access to our own internal experience. Jack knows what it felt like to stay up all night; Michael doesn't. Michael knows his client blew up that morning; Jack doesn't.
2. **Different interpretations** — Past experiences create different emotional filters (Caroline's Depression-era frugality). Implicit rules create invisible frameworks for judgment (Thelma: "lateness is unprofessional" vs. Ollie: "obsessing about lateness is unprofessional").
3. **Self-serving conclusions** — Raiffa's experiment showed that buyers and sellers given identical facts valued a company 30% apart, each unconsciously biasing toward their interest.

## Mechanisms

The What Happened? Conversation goes wrong because of a cascade:
1. We construct a story based on partial information, shaped by our interpretations and self-interest
2. We assume our story is the objective truth
3. We assume the other person's conflicting story reflects their character flaws (they're selfish, irrational, controlling)
4. We argue our conclusions at each other rather than exploring the information and interpretations that produced them
5. The argument reinforces each person's story rather than creating understanding

The exit from this cascade is the move from certainty to curiosity — asking "What don't I know?" rather than asserting "Here's what I know."

## Applications

- **Before a difficult conversation:** Map out the three errors. What is your Truth Assumption? What intentions are you attributing? Who are you blaming? Then prepare to hold each as a hypothesis rather than a certainty.
- **During the conversation:** When you catch yourself arguing about who's right, shift to exploring what each person's story is and why it makes sense from their vantage point.
- **The And Stance:** Both stories can be valid simultaneously. You don't have to choose between yours and theirs. See [[and-stance]].
