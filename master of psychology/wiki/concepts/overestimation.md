# Overestimation

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Overestimation is the common tendency to think we are **better, smarter, faster, more capable, more attractive, or more popular** than we actually are. It is the second form of overconfidence identified by Bazerman and Moore, alongside [[overprecision]] and [[overplacement]]. Overestimation leads us to overestimate what we will accomplish, how much control we have, and how rosy our future will be.

## Key Findings

- **Self-enhancement:** People are motivated to view themselves positively (Dunning, 2005); we evaluate ourselves more favorably on desirable traits (Alicke, 1985); we even prefer the letters in our own names over other letters (Nuttin, 1985, 1987). These effects operate at System 1 speed and weaken under deliberate System 2 thinking (Koole, Dijksterhuis, & van Knippenberg, 2001) ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch2]])
- **Illusion of control:** Overestimating how much control we have (S.C. Thompson, 1999). Superficial signs of control (choosing lottery numbers) lead to belief in controlling uncontrollable events (Langer, 1975). Paradox: when people have actual control, they tend to underestimate it, leading to fatalistic attitudes
- **Planning fallacy:** Overestimating speed and underestimating cost of project completion (Buehler, Griffin, & Ross, 1994). Flyvbjerg (2003): major infrastructure projects worldwide systematically underestimate cost and duration. Boston's Big Dig: $2.5B budget became $14.5B, completed 9 years late. Doctoral students dramatically overestimate the probability of finishing dissertations within a year. Simple tasks may actually be overestimated in time when complications are few (Boltz, Kupperman, & Dunne, 1998)
- **Optimistic biases:** Unrealistic optimism (Sharot, Riccardi, Raio, & Phelps, 2007). Not universal -- defensive pessimism (Norem, 2002) and the "moment of truth" effect (Gilovich, Kerr, & Medvec, 1993) show people sometimes become more pessimistic as feedback approaches
- **Endowment effect connection:** Liking our own characteristics may explain why simply owning an object increases its subjective value (Morewedge, Shu, Gilbert, & Wilson, 2009)

## Mechanisms

Overestimation is partly motivational (it feels good to believe in ourselves) and partly cognitive (we have more information about our own efforts, intentions, and plans than about the obstacles, competitors, and chance events that will affect outcomes). The planning fallacy specifically arises from focusing on the plan itself (inside view) rather than distributional information about similar projects (outside view). Self-enhancement is strongest under System 1 automatic processing and weakens when System 2 engages (Koole et al., 2001).

## Applications

- **Project management:** Use reference-class forecasting (the "outside view") -- base estimates on similar past projects rather than analyzing the specific plan
- **Strategic planning:** Budget for overestimation by applying a systematic correction factor based on historical accuracy
- **Personal development:** Recognize the illusion of control and focus energy on genuinely controllable factors
- **Leadership:** Balance confidence (needed for motivation and persuasion) with realistic self-assessment
