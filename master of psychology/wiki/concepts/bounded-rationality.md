# Bounded Rationality

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Bounded rationality is the concept, proposed by Nobel laureate Herbert Simon (March & Simon, 1958; Simon, 1957), that individual judgment is **bounded in its rationality** -- we cannot make perfectly rational decisions because of cognitive, informational, and temporal constraints. Rather than optimizing (finding the best possible solution), we **satisfice** -- searching until we find a solution that is "good enough."

## Key Findings

- The rational model of decision making assumes people (1) perfectly define problems, (2) identify all criteria, (3) accurately weigh criteria, (4) know all alternatives, (5) accurately assess each alternative, and (6) accurately compute the optimal choice. People rarely achieve any of these ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch1]])
- Time and cost constraints limit the quantity and quality of available information
- Decision makers retain only a relatively small amount of information in usable memory
- Intelligence limitations and perceptual errors constrain the ability to "calculate" the optimal choice from the universe of available alternatives
- Together, these limitations prevent us from making the optimal decisions assumed by the rational model -- we forgo the best solution in favor of one that is acceptable or reasonable
- Thaler (2000) extended the framework beyond cognition: (1) **bounded willpower** -- giving greater weight to present concerns over future, leading to failures like inadequate retirement saving; (2) **bounded self-interest** -- caring about others' outcomes contrary to the self-interested economic actor assumption
- Bazerman and Chugh (2005) added [[bounded-awareness]] -- the tendency to overlook obvious and important information
- The complete framework: bounded rationality, bounded willpower, bounded self-interest, bounded awareness, and bounded ethicality

## Mechanisms

Bounded rationality arises from fundamental limitations of the human cognitive system: limited working memory capacity, limited attention, limited processing speed, and the inability to consider all possible alternatives simultaneously. The mind compensates by using heuristics -- simplifying strategies that reduce complex judgments to manageable operations. These heuristics are often effective but can lead to systematic, predictable errors (the subject of the [[heuristics-and-biases-program]]).

Satisficing is the behavioral consequence: rather than examining all possible alternatives to find the best, we search sequentially and stop as soon as we find an option that exceeds our aspiration level. The aspiration level itself may be set suboptimally.

## Applications

- **Organizational design:** Since individuals satisfice, organizations should design systems that make the satisficing outcome as close to optimal as possible (checklists, decision protocols, defaults)
- **Strategy:** Recognizing that competitors also have bounded rationality creates opportunities for those who invest in overcoming their own bounds
- **Negotiation:** Understanding that both sides satisfice opens space for creating value that neither party initially considered
- **Self-improvement:** The prescription from [[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch1]] is to identify where your rationality is bounded and develop specific strategies to expand those bounds

## Open Questions
- Is satisficing sometimes the rational strategy? (Gigerenzer and colleagues argue that simple heuristics can be "ecologically rational" -- better than optimization in uncertain environments.)
- How can organizations systematically reduce the bounds on their members' rationality?
