# Emotional Intelligence

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Emotional intelligence (EI) is "the subset of social intelligence that involves the ability to monitor one's own and others' feelings and emotions, to discriminate among them and to use this information to guide one's thinking and actions" (Salovey & Mayer, 1990). It encompasses three interrelated sets of mental abilities: (1) appraising and expressing emotion in self and others, (2) regulating emotion in self and others, and (3) utilizing emotion to solve problems, plan flexibly, think creatively, redirect attention, and motivate behavior.

EI is positioned within the broader intelligence tradition — not as a contradiction in terms, but as a legitimate subset of social intelligence (Thorndike) and closely related to Gardner's personal intelligences (intrapersonal + interpersonal). The construct rests on a functionalist view of emotion: emotions are not disorganized disruptions of rationality but organized, adaptive responses that cross physiological, cognitive, motivational, and experiential subsystems.

## Key Findings

- **EI is not a single ability but a family of interrelated processes.** The three branches (appraisal/expression, regulation, utilization) need not intercorrelate for the framework to be useful — just as attention, memory, and metacognition are independent components of cognition (Salovey & Mayer, 1990).
- **Individual differences in EI components are measurable** via existing scales: alexithymia measures (verbal appraisal deficits), nonverbal sending/receiving accuracy tests, empathy scales, meta-mood scales, and affect-cognition tasks.
- **Positive mood enhances creative problem solving.** Isen et al. demonstrated that positive mood facilitates performance on remote associates, Duncker's candle task, categorization, and integration of information.
- **Mood swings serve a cognitive function.** Individuals with stronger mood swings generate more varied future scenarios, enabling more flexible planning.
- **Empathy development involves both innate and learned components:** primary circular reactions (infants crying in response to other infants' cries) and classical empathic conditioning (learning emotional responses through observation).
- **EI deficits map onto clinical conditions:** alexithymia (inability to identify/express emotions), depression (reduced emotional expressiveness), sociopathy (over-regulation of others' emotions for selfish ends).
- **The emotionally intelligent person is not simply "nice"** — EI involves tolerating painful emotions in service of growth, exercising emotional restraint for greater objectives, and sometimes prioritizing long-term well-being over immediate comfort.

## Mechanisms

**Emotional appraisal and expression** operates through verbal introspection, nonverbal channels (facial expression, body language, tone), and empathic resonance. The perceptual system initiates EI when affect-laden information enters awareness. Accurate appraisal enables appropriate response selection.

**Emotion regulation** involves a meta-cognitive regulatory system: meta-experiences of mood monitor, evaluate, and sometimes act to change emotional states. Regulation strategies include choosing associates, seeking self-affirming information, helping others (negative state relief), reappraising negative moods as controllable, and using conscious mechanisms to counteract automatic negative associations. The asymmetry between mood maintenance (easier) and mood repair (harder) parallels [[loss-aversion]] in prospect theory.

**Emotion utilization** works through four channels:
1. Mood-driven variation in future estimates enables flexible scenario planning
2. Positive affect broadens associative networks, facilitating creative connections
3. Strong emotions interrupt ongoing processing and redirect attention to higher-priority concerns
4. Emotional states (anxiety, confidence, positive attitude) modulate persistence and preparation

## Applications

- **Mental health:** Identifying and addressing EI deficits as clinical targets (alexithymia treatment, emotional skills training)
- **Leadership:** Understanding charisma as emotional regulation of followers; emotionally intelligent impression management
- **Education:** Teaching emotional appraisal, expression, and regulation skills
- **Interpersonal relationships:** Using empathy and emotion regulation for prosocial outcomes
- **Persuasion and influence:** Emotionally intelligent communicators can elicit targeted emotional responses in audiences (connects to [[cialdini]]'s influence principles, especially liking and social proof)
- **Decision-making:** Recognizing how mood states influence judgment and using that awareness to improve decisions (connects to [[heuristics-and-biases-program]])

## Open Questions

- **Do the three branches intercorrelate empirically?** The authors argue they need not, but the degree of empirical overlap is an open question for measurement development.
- **Is EI domain-general or domain-specific?** Do individuals who excel at self-appraisal also excel at regulating others' emotions, or are these separable skills?
- **How trainable is EI?** The authors suggest it may be rooted in learnable underlying skills, but intervention evidence is not yet presented.
- **Where is the line between emotionally intelligent influence and manipulation?** The framework is value-neutral on this point.
- **How does culture moderate EI?** The paper does not address cultural variation, but given that the [[fundamental-attribution-error]] varies cross-culturally (per 1-03), emotional norms and competencies likely do as well.
