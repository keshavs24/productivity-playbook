# Feedback Model (Bradford & Robin)

**Phase(s):** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The feedback model in [[bradford-robin|Bradford and Robin's]] *[[connect]]* treats feedback as a relationship-strengthening tool oriented toward growth, not punishment. "Feedback is the breakfast of champions" (Chapter 7 title). The model frames feedback as an act of care: when you help someone understand the impact of their behavior, you are showing commitment to both the relationship and the person's development. This is hallmark #6 of [[exceptional-relationships]] — commitment to each other's growth and development. The model is developed across two chapters: Chapter 7 (why feedback matters) and Chapter 8 (challenges in using feedback effectively).

## Key Findings

- Challenging someone can be a powerful way of supporting them, yet few people feel confident they can do it well. The fear of giving feedback — and the fear of receiving it — is one of the primary barriers to relationship deepening.
- Feedback delivered in the spirit of helping the other person understand the *impact* of their behavior is fundamentally different from criticism, judgment, or evaluation. The former builds relationships; the latter damages them.
- In [[exceptional-relationships]], feedback is easier to give and receive because both parties trust that the intention is growth, not attack. This trust is built through the [[interpersonal-vulnerability]] cycle — repeated disclosures met with acceptance.
- Feedback and conflict are "features, not bugs" of exceptional relationships. A relationship where both parties avoid all feedback is one where important information is being withheld, preventing the growth that defines exceptional connections.
- The model connects closely to the [[pinch-crunch-model]]: raising a pinch early is a specific instance of growth-oriented feedback. If feedback is normalized in a relationship, pinches are addressed before they become crunches.
- Chapter 8 addresses the common challenges that prevent effective feedback: fear of hurting the other person, fear of retaliation, lack of skill in framing feedback constructively, defensiveness when receiving feedback, and the tendency to confuse feedback with evaluation.

## Mechanisms

Effective feedback in the Bradford-Robin model follows key principles:

1. **Intent matters:** Feedback should be motivated by care for the other person's growth and for the relationship, not by a desire to win, punish, or demonstrate superiority
2. **Focus on impact, not intent:** Describe the impact of the other person's behavior on you ("When you did X, I felt Y") rather than attributing intent ("You were trying to undermine me")
3. **Specificity over generalization:** Describe specific behaviors and specific impacts, not character assessments or sweeping generalizations ("always," "never")
4. **Timeliness:** Give feedback close to the triggering event, not weeks or months later. This connects to the [[pinch-crunch-model]] principle of addressing irritants early.
5. **Receptivity to reciprocal feedback:** Be willing to hear how your behavior impacts others. Feedback is a two-way street; demanding the right to give feedback without being open to receiving it creates a power imbalance.
6. **Curiosity over certainty:** Approach feedback conversations with genuine curiosity about the other person's perspective, not with a predetermined conclusion

The model positions feedback as a form of [[interpersonal-vulnerability]] — offering honest feedback is risky (the other person might react badly), and receiving it is vulnerable (it challenges your self-image). Both acts require a [[learning-mindset]].

## Applications

- **Reframe feedback as care:** Before giving feedback, check your intent. If you are motivated by genuine concern for the person and the relationship, proceed. If you are motivated by frustration, punishment, or ego, pause and recalibrate.
- **Practice the impact formula:** "When you [specific behavior], I felt/experienced [specific impact]." This separates observation from interpretation and avoids triggering defensiveness.
- **Normalize feedback in key relationships:** Explicitly discuss with important people in your life that feedback is welcome and expected. Create a shared understanding that feedback is a sign of investment in the relationship, not a sign of dissatisfaction.
- **Build receiving skills:** When receiving feedback, resist the defensive impulse. Ask clarifying questions. Thank the person for the risk they took. Reflect before responding.
- **Link to the six hallmarks:** Use feedback conversations as opportunities to practice hallmarks 4 (honesty), 5 (productive conflict), and 6 (mutual growth commitment) simultaneously.

## Open Questions

- How does the Bradford-Robin feedback model compare to other feedback frameworks (e.g., SBI — Situation-Behavior-Impact; Radical Candor)? The core principles appear similar, but the relational context may be distinctive.
- What role does [[emotional-intelligence]] play as a moderator? People with higher EI may be better at both delivering and receiving feedback effectively.
- The model assumes that both parties in a relationship share the goal of growth. How should feedback be handled when one party is invested in growth and the other is not?
