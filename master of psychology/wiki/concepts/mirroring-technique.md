# Mirroring Technique

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

In [[voss|Chris Voss]]'s negotiation framework, mirroring (also called isopraxism) is the practice of repeating the last one to three words (or the critical one to three words) of what someone has just said. Unlike broader conceptions of mirroring that include body language, posture, and vocal patterns, the FBI's version focuses exclusively on **the words themselves** -- not the tone, accent, or body language.

Mirroring follows a basic biological principle: we fear what's different and are drawn to what's similar. A mirror signals to another person's unconscious: "You and I -- we're alike." It is "the art of insinuating similarity" and facilitates bonding, trust, and rapport. Voss calls it "the closest one gets to a Jedi mind trick" in the FBI's hostage negotiation skill set.

## Key Findings

- **Wiseman waiter study:** Psychologist Richard Wiseman compared two groups of waiters. Those who mirrored customers' orders (repeating them back verbatim) received **70% higher average tips** than those who used positive reinforcement ("Great!", "Sure!", "No problem!"). Mirroring outperformed active positivity as a rapport-building tool.
- **Chase Manhattan robbery (1993):** Voss mirrored bank robber Chris Watts -- "We chased your driver away?" -- triggering a cascade of damaging admissions about an unknown accomplice and the getaway driver. Later, mirroring "No? You said 'okay'" caused Watts to lose composure and begin blurting information.
- **Workplace drive-by boss:** A student's boss demanded two paper copies of thousands of documents. She mirrored at each step ("Two copies?", "Anywhere?"), causing the boss to self-correct over two exchanges. A day later he emailed: "The two digital backups will be fine." A week of unnecessary work eliminated.

## Mechanisms

1. **Isopraxism (imitation instinct):** Mirroring exploits a deep neurobehavior in which humans (and other animals) copy each other to comfort one another. It is generally unconscious -- we rarely notice when it's happening -- but it signals bonding, synchrony, and trust.
2. **Elaboration trigger:** By repeating back key words, you trigger the other person's instinct to elaborate and clarify. They will never restate it exactly the same way -- they will add detail, nuance, and new information. This is why mirroring is superior to "What do you mean by that?" (which incites irritation).
3. **Time and safety:** Mirroring buys time to regroup, keeps people talking, reveals strategy, and sustains the process of connecting. It slows the conversation down naturally without creating awkward pauses.
4. **Inconsistency detection:** "When another person's tone of voice or body language is inconsistent with his words, a good mirror can be particularly useful" -- it surfaces the real message behind the contradictory signals.

## Applications

**The four-step mirroring process:**
1. Use the [[late-night-fm-dj-voice]]
2. Start with "I'm sorry..."
3. Mirror (repeat the last 1-3 critical words, in an inquisitive tone)
4. Silence -- at least four seconds to let the mirror work its magic on your counterpart
5. Repeat as needed

**When to use:**
- To disagree without being disagreeable -- it lets you confront without confrontation
- To gather more information -- people elaborate when mirrored
- To buy time when you're not sure how to respond
- To signal that you're listening and engaged
- To surface inconsistencies between words and tone/body language
- To keep a counterpart talking (information extraction)

**The intention behind most mirrors:** "Please, help me understand." Every time you mirror, they will reword what they've said -- providing new angles, new information, new opportunities.
