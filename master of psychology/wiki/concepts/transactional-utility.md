# Transactional Utility

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Transactional utility is the perceived quality of a deal, evaluated by comparing the price paid against what the item "should" cost. It is distinct from acquisition utility (the objective value of the commodity). People evaluate purchases based on both: the value of the item AND the quality of the deal. A "good deal" creates positive transactional utility; a "rip-off" creates negative transactional utility -- even when the acquisition utility is identical.

## Key Findings

- **Thaler (1985) beach beer scenario:** Identical beer drunk on the beach. Median WTP from a fancy resort hotel: $2.65. Median WTP from a small, rundown grocery store: $1.50. Same product, same consumption context, but expected "fair price" differs by vendor. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch6]])
- **Mouse vs. laptop (Problems 9 & 10):** People demand a larger absolute discount on a $2,000 laptop than on a $50 mouse to justify a 20-minute drive. The relative percentage matters more than the absolute savings, despite the rational comparison being "savings vs. value of 20 minutes." A $40 savings on a laptop (2%) feels small; the same $40 on a mouse (80%) feels enormous.
- Transactional utility explains "too good a deal to pass up" (buying unneeded items) and "rip-off" avoidance (refusing to buy needed items at inflated prices)

## Mechanisms

Transactional utility arises from [[reference-dependence]] applied to prices rather than outcomes. The "fair price" or expected price serves as the reference point. Paying more feels like a loss; paying less feels like a gain. The reference price is determined by context (vendor type, historical prices, competitor prices), not by the intrinsic value of the good.

This connects to [[mental-accounting]]: the "deal" is evaluated in its own mental account, separate from the value of the item itself.

## Applications

- **Pricing strategy:** Maintaining high reference prices (list price, MSRP) makes discounts feel like positive transactional utility
- **Retail context:** Identical products can command different prices in different retail contexts (airport vs. supermarket) because expected "fair prices" differ
- **Negotiation:** Anchor high to set the reference price, making subsequent offers feel like better "deals"
