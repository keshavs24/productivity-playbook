# Nonverbal Communication

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.6

## Definition

Nonverbal communication refers to how people communicate intentionally or unintentionally without words. Channels include facial expressions, tone of voice, gestures, body position and movement, eye gaze, touch, and personal space. Nonverbal cues are a primary source of information in social perception and a core component of [[emotional-perception]] in the Salovey/Mayer emotional intelligence framework.

## Key Findings

### Facial Expressions and Ekman's Universals

- **Darwin (1872)** proposed that facial expressions are universal, evolutionary vestiges of once-useful physiological reactions
- **Ekman & Friesen (1971)** confirmed universality with the South Fore tribe in New Guinea -- a preliterate people with no Western contact could match facial expressions to emotional stories with the same accuracy as Western subjects
- **Six universal emotions:** anger, happiness, surprise, fear, disgust, sadness (with possible additions: pride, contempt, anxiety, shame, embarrassment)
- **Functional design (Susskind et al., 2008):** The fear face *enhances* sensory input (wider eyes, more air intake); the disgust face *reduces* it -- these expressions are not arbitrary but serve adaptive physiological functions

### Affect Blends

- **Affect blends** occur when one part of the face registers one emotion while another part registers a different emotion -- making accurate decoding significantly harder
- This complicates the assumption that emotions are discrete, clean signals; real-world emotional expression is often mixed and ambiguous

### Emblems vs. Illustrators

- **Emblems:** Gestures with well-defined, culturally specific meanings (OK sign, thumbs up, peace sign). These function like words -- they have agreed-upon semantic content. Critically, emblems are NOT universal and can cause cross-cultural miscommunication (the "OK" gesture is obscene in some cultures)
- **Illustrators:** Gestures that accompany speech and help convey meaning (hand movements while explaining, pointing) -- more universal but less precisely defined than emblems
- **Display rules (Ekman):** Culturally determined norms about which emotional expressions are appropriate in which contexts. Japanese norms suppress negative expressions in social settings; American norms suppress male grief and crying. The underlying emotion may be universal; its acceptable expression is culturally regulated.

### Context Effects

- The same facial expression has different implications depending on eye gaze direction (Adams & Kleck, 2003) and surrounding people's expressions (Masuda et al., 2008)
- **Cultural differences in reading context:** Japanese participants interpreted emotion holistically (attending to surrounding faces); Americans focused on the individual face alone (analytic style). Eye-tracking confirmed Japanese scanned the background more.
- Personal space norms vary dramatically across cultures (Hall, 1969)

### Nonverbal Perception as Emotional Intelligence

Salovey and Mayer position nonverbal perception as a core component of [[emotional-perception]] -- the first branch of [[emotional-intelligence]]. Key findings from the EI literature:
- Nonverbal perception is measured by instruments including the Profile of Nonverbal Sensitivity (PONS), the Affect Sensitivity Test, and the Communication of Affect Receiving Ability Test (CARAT)
- Women are generally better at recognizing facial emotions (except anger perception)
- CARAT scores are higher among artists than scientists
- Buck concluded nonverbal receiving ability "is not a unidimensional construct" -- different channels (face, voice, body) may be processed independently
- Nonverbal expressiveness (sending accuracy) correlates with [[empathy]] and inversely with depression

## Mechanisms

1. **Evolutionary heritage:** Core facial expressions evolved as physiological responses (fear widens eyes to take in more information; disgust narrows them to block contaminants) and were later co-opted as social signals
2. **Automatic processing:** Nonverbal cues are processed rapidly and largely outside conscious awareness, feeding into [[thin-slicing]] -- impressions form in under 100 milliseconds
3. **Cultural learning:** While basic expressions are universal, display rules, emblems, and context-reading strategies are culturally acquired -- not inborn but learned (paralleling the cultural acquisition of [[fundamental-attribution-error]] tendencies)
4. **Multi-channel integration:** Perceivers combine information from face, voice, body, and context -- but not always accurately, especially across cultural boundaries

## Applications

- **First impressions:** Nonverbal cues are the primary data for [[thin-slicing]] -- the rapid impression formation that occurs before any verbal exchange
- **Emotional intelligence development:** Training nonverbal perception is training the appraisal branch of [[emotional-intelligence]]
- **Cross-cultural competence:** Understanding display rules and emblem differences prevents miscommunication in intercultural settings
- **Deception detection:** While people overestimate their ability to detect lies from nonverbal cues, micro-expressions and affect blends can reveal concealed emotions

- To what extent can nonverbal perception accuracy be trained, and does training transfer across cultures?
- The EI paper notes that nonverbal receiving ability is not unidimensional -- what are the distinct sub-skills and how independently do they develop?
