# Inner Choice

**Phase(s):** 1, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Inner choice refers to the principle that commitments are most effective at producing lasting attitude and behavior change when they are perceived as internally motivated — that is, freely chosen without strong external pressure (rewards or threats). When people believe they acted of their own volition, they accept inner responsibility for the behavior and adjust their self-image accordingly. When strong external justifications are present, people attribute their behavior to the external cause and experience no self-image change.

## Key Findings

- **Freedman's forbidden toy experiment (1965)**: Boys told "It is wrong to play with the robot" with a strong threat (punishment) or mild threat (no punishment) both complied equally in the moment (21/22). Six weeks later, **77%** of the strong-threat boys played with the robot, but only **33%** of the mild-threat boys did. The mild-threat boys had internalized the choice.
- **Chinese POW essay contests**: The Chinese deliberately kept prizes small (a few cigarettes, some fruit) despite having much larger rewards available. Large rewards would have given prisoners a rationalization ("I only wrote it for the prize"). Small prizes forced them to *own* what they wrote.
- **Fraternity hazing vs. community service**: Fraternities refuse to substitute community service for hazing because service provides an external justification for the suffering. Hazing forces pledges to own their commitment to the group.
- **Social science principle**: "We accept inner responsibility for a behavior when we think we have chosen to perform it in the absence of strong outside pressure" (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Higgins, Lee, Kwon, & Trope, 1995; Lepper & Greene, 1978).
- Large material rewards may *reduce* inner responsibility, causing subsequent reluctance to perform the act without the reward (the overjustification effect).

## Mechanisms

1. **Self-attribution**: Without external justification, people attribute their behavior to their own values, preferences, and traits (Cioffi & Garner, 1996). "I must have done it because I believe in it."
2. **Minimal sufficiency principle**: The least amount of external pressure that produces the desired behavior is the most effective for producing lasting change. Greater pressure provides an "out" — an alternative explanation for the behavior.
3. **Self-image alignment**: When external causes are absent, the behavior becomes evidence of "who I am." This self-image change then drives future behavior through [[commitment-and-consistency]].

## Applications

- **Parenting**: Use reasons "just sufficient" to produce desired behavior but not so strong they provide external explanation. A simple "I hope you won't do it" may produce more lasting behavior change than "I'll punish you severely."
- **Management**: Avoid heavy incentives or threats for behaviors you want employees to internalize. Create conditions where people feel they choose the behavior freely.
- **Behavior change**: Ensure people see their new behavior as stemming from their own choice, not from external contingencies. This is why the Iowa energy conservation study succeeded — when the publicity incentive was removed, the homeowners could fully own their conservation identity.
- **Compliance resistance**: Recognition that strong external pressure produces only temporary compliance (Samuel Butler: "He who agrees against his will / Is of the same opinion still").

## Open Questions

- How does inner choice interact with the other commitment factors (active, public, effortful)? Are they additive, multiplicative, or is inner choice truly "more important than the other three combined" as Cialdini suggests?
- Can inner choice be engineered without deception? The Iowa study technically deceived participants about publicity — is there an ethical version?
- How does awareness of the minimal sufficiency principle change its effectiveness for parents and managers?
