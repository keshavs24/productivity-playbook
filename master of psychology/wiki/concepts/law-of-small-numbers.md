# Law of Small Numbers

**Phase(s):** 1, 2
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The "law of small numbers" is the erroneous belief that **small samples should closely mirror the properties of the population** from which they are drawn — essentially, expecting the law of large numbers to apply to small samples. People intuitively believe that even tiny samples are representative.

## Key Findings

- The hospital problem: subjects judged that a large hospital (~45 births/day) and a small hospital (~15 births/day) would equally often have days with >60% boys. The correct answer is the small hospital, because small samples have higher variance. Most subjects said "about the same." ([[summary-1-01-judgment-under-uncertainty]])
- "The notion that sampling variance decreases in proportion to sample size is apparently not part of people's repertoire of intuitions." — Tversky & Kahneman
- This bias is closely related to [[gamblers-fallacy]]: both stem from expecting small sequences to be representative of the generating process.

## Mechanisms

People treat every sample as a faithful miniature of the population. If a coin is fair, they expect short sequences to have roughly 50/50 heads and tails. The concept of sampling variability — that small samples are noisy — is not intuitive. The [[representativeness]] heuristic drives this: a small sample that mirrors the population "looks right"; one that deviates looks surprising.

## Applications

- **Business decisions:** Drawing conclusions from small pilot studies or limited customer feedback as if the sample were definitive
- **Research:** Underpowered studies that happen to show an effect are treated as conclusive; the role of sampling error is underappreciated
- **Sports:** "Hot hand" beliefs and streak interpretations from tiny samples of shots/games
- **Investing:** Judging a fund manager's skill from a few quarters of above-average returns
