# Self-Serving Attribution

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Self-serving attributions are explanations for one's own outcomes that **credit internal, dispositional factors for successes** ("I'm talented") **and blame external, situational factors for failures** ("the test was unfair"). The asymmetry protects self-esteem.

## Key Findings

- Athletes and coaches: 80% of attributions for wins were internal; losses attributed to external causes (Lau & Russell, 1980) ([[summary-1-03-social-psychology-ch4]])
- Less experienced athletes make stronger self-serving attributions; experienced athletes are more balanced
- Solo athletes show stronger self-serving bias than team athletes
- Three drivers: (1) self-esteem protection, (2) impression management (Goffman), (3) differential information access — you know your own effort level better than an observer does
- **Culturally moderated:** Meta-analysis of 266 studies (Mezulis et al., 2004) found self-serving bias strongest in Western individualistic cultures; weaker in some Asian cultures where self-criticism serves group harmony

## Applications

- **Leadership:** Being aware of self-serving attributions in yourself (taking too much credit, deflecting too much blame) and in reports (same pattern) improves feedback quality and organizational learning
- **Negotiation:** Both parties in a negotiation tend to attribute good outcomes to their own skill and poor outcomes to the other side's unfairness — creating post-negotiation resentment even after successful deals
