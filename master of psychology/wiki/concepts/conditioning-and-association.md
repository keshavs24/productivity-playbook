# Conditioning and Association

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The principle of association states that an innocent connection with either good or bad things will influence how people feel about us (Lott & Lott, 1965). People naturally transfer their emotional reactions from one thing to another merely because the two are linked in experience or perception. This principle — rooted in Pavlovian conditioning — is one of the five major factors driving the [[liking-principle]].

The principle operates bidirectionally: positive associations enhance liking (celebrity endorsements, good food, pleasant surroundings), while negative associations diminish it (bad news, bad weather, losing sports teams). Critically, the association need not be causal — mere proximity or connection is sufficient.

## Key Findings

- **Weatherman anecdote:** A TV weatherman received hate mail and death threats when weather was bad, despite merely reporting it. Cialdini explained this through the ancient Persian messengers — bearers of military defeat were summarily slain
- **Bad news infects the teller:** A natural human tendency to dislike the person who brings unpleasant information, even when they did not cause it (Manis, Cornell, & Moore, 1974)
- **Communicating good vs. bad news — Rosen & Tesser (1970):** Students spontaneously connected themselves to good news ("You got a phone call with great news") but distanced from bad news ("You got a phone call"), demonstrating intuitive understanding of the principle
- **Guilt by association — Miller, Campbell, Twedt, & O'Connell (1966):** People assume we have the same personality traits as our friends
- **Cars and attractive models — Smith & Engel (1968):** Men rated a car as faster, more appealing, and better-designed when a seductive model was in the ad — then denied the model had influenced them
- **Credit cards and spending — Feinberg (1986):** MasterCard insignias in a room increased spending by 29% on catalog items and boosted charity donations from 33% to 87%. Diners tipped more in the presence of credit card insignias even when paying cash (McCall & Belmont, 1996). The effect only works for people with a positive credit card history (Feinberg, 1990)
- **Luncheon technique — Razran (1938, 1940):** Subjects became fonder of political statements they encountered while eating; putrid odors produced the opposite effect. Subjects could not recall which statements were shown during eating — the conditioning was unconscious. Subliminal odors also affect likeability judgments of faces (Li et al., 2007)
- **Cultural trend exploitation:** Moon shot allusions in the 1960s; Olympic sponsorships; the "natural" trend in the 1970s. Mars Candy Bar sales jumped after the Mars Pathfinder landing despite no connection to the planet (White, 1997). SALE signs increase purchases through past association with savings, even when no actual discount exists (Naylor et al., 2006)
- **Celebrity endorsements:** Athletes and entertainers are paid to create positive associations — relevant or irrelevant to the product
- **[[basking-in-reflected-glory]] — Cialdini et al. (1976):** After wins, students used "We beat Houston"; after losses, "They lost to Missouri." Belgian homeowners left lawn signs up longer after their party won (Boen et al., 2002)

## Mechanisms

The mechanism traces directly to Pavlovian classical conditioning:

1. **Pavlov's demonstration:** A neutral stimulus (bell) paired with an unconditioned stimulus (food) eventually produces a conditioned response (salivation to the bell alone)
2. **Razran's insight:** Food produces many responses beyond salivation — including a positive feeling. This positive feeling can be transferred to anything closely associated with food
3. **Generalization:** Any pleasant (or unpleasant) stimulus can serve as the unconditioned stimulus. The principle extends to attractive models, celebrities, good weather, pleasant music, and any other positive experience
4. **Unconscious operation:** The conditioning typically occurs without awareness, making it a [[click-whirr]] response

People also actively manage their own associations:
- **BIRGing (basking in reflected glory):** Connecting oneself to winners
- **CORFing (cutting off reflected failure):** Distancing from losers
- Those who BIRG most tend to have poor self-concepts, seeking prestige from external associations rather than personal attainments

## Applications

- **Advertising:** Models, celebrities, pleasant music, beautiful scenery — all create positive associations with products
- **Political fundraising:** Speeches and appeals during or after meals, never before (the luncheon technique)
- **Branding:** Olympic sponsorships, cultural trend associations, product placement
- **Personal impression management:** Name-dropping, highlighting connections to successful people or institutions
- **Sales environments:** Pleasant music, comfortable seating, coffee/snacks — all create positive associations with the salesperson and the deal

## Open Questions

- How durable are associations formed through brief exposure? Do they decay quickly or persist?
- To what extent can negative associations be overridden by positive ones (or vice versa)?
- The credit card findings (Feinberg, 1986, 1990) suggest personal history moderates the association. How broadly does this apply — do all associations require a compatible personal history to be effective?
- In an era of influencer marketing, has awareness of association tactics increased enough to reduce their effectiveness?
