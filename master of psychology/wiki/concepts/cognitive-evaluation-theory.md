# Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET)

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET) is the first of SDT's six mini-theories, focused exclusively on [[intrinsic-motivation]] -- the inherent human propensity to explore, learn, and master one's environment for the satisfaction inherent in the activity itself. CET specifies how social-contextual factors (rewards, feedback, evaluations, deadlines, competition, interpersonal climates) either enhance or undermine intrinsic motivation.

CET's core claim: Intrinsic motivation is an inherent human characteristic that may flourish or wither as a function of ambient social conditions. Events are not inherently controlling or informational -- it is their **[[informational-vs-controlling|functional significance]]** (the psychological meaning the person assigns to them) that determines their motivational impact.

## Key Findings

### The Five Formal Propositions

1. **Proposition I (Autonomy):** External events affect intrinsic motivation through their impact on perceived locus of causality (PLOC). Events fostering external PLOC undermine intrinsic motivation; events fostering internal PLOC enhance it
2. **Proposition II (Competence):** Events affect intrinsic motivation through their impact on perceived competence. Greater perceived competence enhances intrinsic motivation; diminished perceived competence undermines it
3. **Proposition III (Functional Significance):** Every event has informational, controlling, and amotivating aspects. The relative salience of these determines the event's effect on intrinsic motivation
4. **Proposition IV (Interpersonal Climate):** Interpersonal contexts vary from controlling to autonomy-supportive to amotivating, shaping the functional significance of events within them. [[autonomy-support|Autonomy-supportive]] environments most facilitate intrinsic motivation
5. **Proposition V (Intrapersonal Events):** Internal events (e.g., [[ego-involvement]], self-pressuring) can also be informational, controlling, or amotivating, with parallel effects on intrinsic motivation

### Major Empirical Findings

- **[[undermining-effect]]:** Tangible, expected, contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (Deci, 1971; Lepper et al., 1973; meta-analysis of 128 studies, Deci et al., 1999)
- **Positive feedback enhances:** Verbal rewards/positive feedback enhance intrinsic motivation for college students (d=0.43) but not children (d=0.11), who may experience praise as controlling
- **Controlling events undermine:** Threats of punishment, evaluations, surveillance, deadlines, imposed goals, and controlling competition all tend to undermine intrinsic motivation
- **Supportive events enhance:** Choice, optimal challenge, and informational feedback enhance intrinsic motivation
- **Climate moderates:** The same event can enhance or undermine intrinsic motivation depending on the interpersonal climate in which it occurs
- **Ego involvement undermines:** Internal self-evaluative pressure (ego involvement) undermines intrinsic motivation even without external controls (Ryan, 1982)
- **Performance effects:** Intrinsic motivation produces better conceptual learning, creativity, and performance on complex/heuristic tasks; controlling conditions produce more rote memorization and rigid problem-solving

## Mechanisms

1. **Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC):** Events shift the experience of behavior from "I want to" (internal PLOC) to "I have to" (external PLOC). Intrinsic motivation requires internal PLOC
2. **Competence Satisfaction:** When events convey information about competence in an autonomy-supportive context, intrinsic motivation is enhanced. When they signal incompetence, it is undermined
3. **Need Thwarting:** Controlling events thwart the [[autonomy-need]]; amotivating events thwart [[competence-need]]. Both reduce intrinsic motivation
4. **Neurological:** fMRI studies show that contingent rewards decrease intrinsic value of task success in brain reward networks (anterior striatum, midbrain) and that intrinsic vs. controlled motivation recruit distinct neural pathways (AIC vs. PCC)

## Applications

- **Education:** Autonomy-supportive teaching predicts higher student intrinsic motivation, competence, and self-esteem within weeks. Controlling teaching (evaluations, grades, surveillance) undermines these. Limit-setting can be autonomy-supportive if it minimizes controlling language, acknowledges feelings, and provides rationale
- **Parenting:** Autonomy-supportive parenting predicts children's intrinsic motivation and exploration. Controlling parenting (contingent rewards, conditional regard) undermines it
- **Workplace:** Autonomy-supportive management predicts employee engagement and job satisfaction. Controlling management leads to alienation and emphasis on extrinsic factors
- **Game design:** Small, informational rewards that acknowledge effort without controlling behavior can maintain intrinsic motivation (video game design principle)
- **Feedback delivery:** Positive feedback should be informational (acknowledging competence) rather than controlling ("you did what you should"). Negative feedback should be constructive and autonomy-supportive
