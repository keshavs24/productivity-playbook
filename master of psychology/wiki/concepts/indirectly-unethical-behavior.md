# Indirectly Unethical Behavior

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition
The tendency to judge harmful actions as less unethical when harm is caused indirectly (through intermediaries, delegation, or willful ignorance) rather than directly, even when the indirect route produces equal or greater harm. A manifestation of [[bounded-ethicality]] in which people exploit psychological distance from consequences to behave selfishly while maintaining a positive self-image.

## Key Findings
- **Pharmaceutical scenario (Paharia, Kassam, Greene, & Bazerman, 2007):** Participants judged a company raising its own drug price from $3 to $9 as MORE unethical than selling the rights to a smaller firm that raised it to $15. The indirect route caused more harm but was perceived as less wrong. However, when participants compared both options directly, they correctly identified the $15 outcome as worse.
- **Real-world case -- Merck/Ovation (2005):** Merck sold rights to cancer drug Mustargen to Ovation Pharmaceuticals (a firm specializing in buying slow-selling medicines). Ovation raised the price roughly ten times. Merck avoided the public backlash of raising the price itself.
- **Willful ignorance (Dana, Weber, & Kuang, 2007):** In a modified [[dictator-game]], the dictator could click a box to see consequences for the other player. Half chose not to click, then chose the selfish option. Remaining ignorant of consequences enabled selfish choices.
- **Recipients were more forgiving** of dictators who chose not to learn the consequences than of those who knew and chose selfishly anyway -- suggesting people intuitively understand the self-protective function of willful ignorance.
- **Silent exit (Dana, Cain, & Dawes, 2006):** One-third of dictators chose to exit silently with $9 (recipient gets nothing and never knows) rather than play the standard game where they could take $10 and give $0 openly. People prefer secret selfishness over transparent selfishness.
- **The "[[preference-reversal]]" structure:** Indirect harm is judged differently in separate vs. joint evaluation (consistent with [[want-should-conflict]] patterns from Chapter 5). In separate evaluation, people judge each route in isolation; in joint comparison, the actual harm becomes salient.

## Mechanisms
- **Psychological distance:** Adding intermediaries creates distance between actor and harm, reducing emotional engagement and moral responsibility
- **Self-image protection:** People want to see themselves as ethical. Indirect routes provide plausible deniability ("I didn't raise the price; I just sold the rights").
- **Information avoidance:** When learning the consequences of an action would create moral discomfort, people choose not to learn -- and this avoidance itself enables harmful choices
- **Accountability diffusion:** When multiple actors are involved, each can attribute responsibility to others

## Applications
- **Supply chain ethics:** Organizations that outsource harmful practices (labor conditions, environmental damage) to subcontractors are engaging in indirectly unethical behavior
- **Delegation awareness:** When delegating decisions that affect others, recognize that creating distance from consequences does not eliminate moral responsibility
- **Information systems:** Design organizational processes that make it difficult for decision-makers to remain ignorant of the consequences of their choices
- **Self-monitoring:** When you find yourself choosing not to know the full impact of your decisions, treat that impulse itself as a warning sign
