# Fairness as a Constraint on Profit Seeking

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Fairness as a constraint on profit seeking is the empirically documented phenomenon that community standards of fairness limit the pricing, wage-setting, and rent-setting behavior of firms in ways that deviate from the predictions of standard economic theory. The central theoretical construct is the **principle of dual entitlement**: transactors (customers, employees, tenants) are entitled to the terms of the **reference transaction**, and firms are entitled to their **reference profit**. A firm violates fairness norms when it increases its own profit by imposing a loss on its transactors relative to the reference transaction.

## Key Findings

### The Dual Entitlement Framework

The principle of dual entitlement, proposed by [[kahneman|Kahneman]], Knetsch, and [[thaler|Thaler]] (1986), summarizes the rules governing community judgments of fairness in market transactions:

1. **Transactors are entitled to the terms of the reference transaction.** The reference transaction is typically the most recent price, wage, or rent in an ongoing relationship, or the prevailing competitive terms for new relationships.
2. **Firms are entitled to their reference profit.** A positive reference profit serves as the baseline for evaluating firm actions.
3. **It is acceptable for firms to protect reference profit at the transactor's expense** (e.g., raising prices when costs rise), but **unacceptable to increase profit by exploiting transactors** (e.g., raising prices when demand surges without a cost increase).

### The Core Asymmetry: Cost Increases vs. Demand Increases

The most striking finding is the asymmetry in how different occasions for price or wage changes are judged:

- **Passing on cost increases:** Broadly acceptable (75-80% approval). The firm's reference profit is threatened; protecting it at the transactor's expense is fair.
- **Exploiting demand increases:** Broadly unacceptable (63-82% disapproval). The firm's reference profit is not threatened; capturing the increased surplus is seen as imposing an unjustified loss on transactors.
- **Retaining cost decreases:** Mixed (47-53% disapproval). The transactor's reference terms are not worsened, so the firm may keep gains from cost reductions -- though opinion is divided.

This asymmetry is not predicted by standard economic theory, which treats supply shifts and demand shifts symmetrically.

### Reference Transactions and Adaptation

The reference transaction is not necessarily "just" -- it is simply *normal*. Psychological adaptation means any stable state of affairs tends to be accepted as a legitimate baseline. This has important dynamic implications:

- An initially unfair practice (e.g., charging above list price for a popular car) may gradually become the new reference transaction as it spreads and becomes normalized.
- Entitlements are transactor-specific: a new employee does not inherit a predecessor's entitlement to a higher wage.
- Entitlements are transaction-specific: changing the line of business can reset the reference wage.

### Framing Effects in Fairness

Fairness judgments are susceptible to the same [[framing-effects]] that govern individual choice:

- **Nominal wage cuts** (coded as losses) are judged far more harshly than **below-inflation raises** (coded as gains), even when the real impact is identical -- a manifestation of [[money-illusion]]
- **Price surcharges** (coded as losses relative to list price) are judged more harshly than **elimination of discounts** (ambiguously coded)
- **Wage cuts** (losses) are judged more harshly than **bonus elimination** (reduction of gains)

These patterns directly parallel the gain/loss asymmetries documented in [[prospect-theory]] and [[loss-aversion]].

## Mechanisms

The concept rests on three psychological mechanisms:

1. **[[reference-dependence]]:** All fairness evaluations are made relative to a reference transaction, not in absolute terms. There is no "objectively fair" price -- only departures from the established baseline.
2. **[[loss-aversion]]:** Actions that impose losses on transactors (relative to the reference) are weighted more heavily than actions that reduce possible gains. This is why cost pass-throughs are acceptable (the firm averts a loss) but demand exploitation is not (the firm achieves a gain by imposing a loss).
3. **Naive accounting:** People use intuitive accounting rules (e.g., FIFO for inventory, cost assignment to specific goods) that diverge from economic rationality. Raising the price of existing stock when replacement cost rises is judged unfair because people evaluate the action against the historical cost, not the opportunity cost.

## Applications

- **Pricing strategy:** Firms benefit from framing price increases as cost pass-throughs rather than profit-seeking moves. Discounts are preferable to low list prices because eliminating a discount is less offensive than imposing a surcharge.
- **Compensation design:** Structuring pay as base salary plus bonus gives firms more flexibility to reduce total compensation during downturns (eliminating a bonus is more acceptable than cutting base pay).
- **Market design:** The opposition to auctions for consumer goods, jobs, and services explains why firms use posted prices, waiting lists, and rationing rather than price-clearing mechanisms in customer and labor markets.
- **Regulation and public policy:** Public perceptions of fairness can drive support for anti-gouging legislation, rent control, and minimum wage laws. Edward Zajac inferred general rules of fairness from public reactions to regulated utility behavior.

## Open Questions

- **Cultural variation:** The original data came from Toronto and Vancouver. How stable are these fairness norms across cultures, particularly in societies with different market traditions?
- **Temporal stability:** Have these norms shifted in the decades since the 1984-85 surveys? The rise of dynamic pricing (Uber surge pricing, airline yield management, algorithmic pricing) may be normalizing demand-based pricing.
- **Firm-side behavior:** The paper documents public perceptions but does not directly measure firm behavior. How closely do actual pricing decisions conform to the dual entitlement model?
- **Online vs. offline markets:** Do fairness constraints operate differently in anonymous online transactions where reputation and relationship effects are weaker?
