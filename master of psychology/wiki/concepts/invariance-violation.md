# Invariance Violation

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

An invariance violation occurs when different descriptions or representations of the **same choice problem** lead to **different preferences**. The invariance principle (also called [[extensionality]] by Arrow, 1982) requires that logically equivalent formulations of a decision yield identical choices. It is considered the most basic and normatively compelling axiom of rational choice theory -- so fundamental that it is typically assumed in the characterization of options rather than stated as a testable axiom.

Invariance violations are systematic, not random: the direction of preference reversal is predictable from the framing of outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point.

## Key Findings

- Tversky and Kahneman (1986) demonstrated that invariance violations are pervasive and that they occur even with experienced decision-makers and significant stakes ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch12-rational-choice-framing]])
- The Asian Disease Problem: "200 saved" vs. "400 die" -- identical outcomes, opposite risk preferences
- Concurrent decision problems: evaluating gain and loss decisions in isolation produces dominated portfolios (73% chose the dominated option, only 3% the dominant one)
- Medical framing: 72 physicians showed systematic invariance violations in treatment decisions for tumor patients

## Mechanisms

Invariance violations arise from the interaction of [[framing-effects]] with [[prospect-theory]]'s value function. The frame sets the [[reference-dependence|reference point]], which determines what counts as a gain vs. a loss. [[loss-aversion]] and the [[reflection-effect]] then produce different risk preferences depending on the coding. Since people cannot spontaneously compute the canonical representation of a problem, they are prisoners of whatever frame is presented.

## Applications

- **Policy design:** The way policies are framed (lives saved vs. lives lost, jobs created vs. jobs eliminated) determines public support
- **Medical communication:** How treatment outcomes are described to patients predictably shifts their choices
- **Organizational decisions:** Sequential isolated framing of business decisions produces systematically inferior aggregate outcomes
- **Negotiation:** Framing proposals as gains or losses shifts counterpart risk preferences
