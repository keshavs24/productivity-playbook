# Problem-Solution-Benefit

**Phase(s):** 7
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Problem-Solution-Benefit is a three-part persuasive communication structure used for pitching ideas, selling products, advocating for change, or convincing others to take action. The speaker first defines a **problem** or pain point the audience shares, then presents a feasible **solution**, and finally describes the **benefits** that adoption of the solution will yield.

## Key Findings

- Salespeople, entrepreneurs, and marketers commonly follow this structure when pitching. Rev. Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech, largely delivered spontaneously, follows the Problem-Solution-Benefit formula ([[summary-7-26-tfts-ch5]])
- The structure is particularly effective for persuasion because it follows the audience's natural thought process: "What's wrong?" --> "How do we fix it?" --> "What do we gain?" ([[summary-7-26-tfts-app3]])
- Elon Musk's Tesla Powerwall pitch exemplified the structure: (Problem) global warming from fossil fuels + barriers to solar transition, (Solution) the Powerwall battery, (Benefit) clean energy independence ([[summary-7-26-tfts-app3]])
- The order can be flexibly rearranged: if strong audience resistance is anticipated, leading with the Benefit can be more effective ([[summary-7-26-tfts-app3]])

## Mechanisms

Problem-Solution-Benefit leverages several psychological principles:

1. **Pain point activation:** Defining the problem creates urgency and emotional engagement. People are more motivated to act when they feel a problem personally.
2. **Solution framing:** Presenting a feasible solution after the pain point provides relief and creates an association between the speaker and the resolution.
3. **Benefit articulation:** Ranking and specifying benefits anchors the audience on gains rather than costs, counteracting [[loss-aversion]] tendencies.
4. **Logical narrative:** The three-part sequence creates a satisfying story arc -- tension (problem), action (solution), resolution (benefit) -- which aids memory and processing.

## Applications

- **Pitching to investors or stakeholders:** Define the market problem, present your product/service as the solution, detail the benefits of investing
- **Persuading in meetings:** Frame a proposed change as solving a recognized problem, then lay out benefits
- **Selling products:** TV commercials commonly follow this structure -- problem, product demo, happy customer
- **Personal persuasion:** Abrahams' 12-year-old son used it to convince his parents to buy an electric guitar

### Bonus Structure Variant (for business pitches)
Complete four sentence starters: "What if you could..." / "So that..." / "For example..." / "And that's not all..."

### Tips for Effectiveness
1. Use **analogies** to make each step concrete and relatable
2. Show **consistency** with prior actions ("foot in the door")
3. Frame benefits in **positive terms** (75% success rate, not 25% failure rate)
4. **Address obstacles** proactively -- acknowledge barriers and offer solutions
5. Leave **intentional imperfection** -- Baba Shiv argues overly polished pitches invite criticism; a small flaw gives the audience something to improve, increasing their buy-in
