# Internalization in Education

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Internalization in education refers to the process by which students take in and progressively integrate the value and regulation of academic activities that are not inherently interesting or intrinsically motivating. Within [[self-determination-theory]], this is the application of [[extrinsic-motivation|organismic integration theory (OIT)]] to the educational domain.

Most school tasks are not inherently fascinating to students. Developing a personal value for schoolwork -- seeing it as important, relevant, and worth doing for its own sake -- is crucial for long-term academic success. Through internalization, students can become autonomously motivated to learn material they do not find inherently interesting but in which they find value. The degree of internalization determines whether students' extrinsic motivation is experienced as controlled (external regulation, introjection) or autonomous (identification, integration).

The internalization continuum in education:
1. **External regulation:** Studying only when monitored, for rewards, or to avoid punishment
2. **Introjected regulation:** Studying to avoid guilt or shame, to maintain self-worth contingent on grades
3. **Identified regulation:** Studying because the student personally values the learning and recognizes its importance
4. **Integrated regulation:** Studying because learning is fully assimilated with the student's values, goals, and sense of self

## Key Findings

### Autonomous vs. Controlled Academic Motivation

- **Yamauchi & Tanaka (1998):** Japanese elementary students. Identified and intrinsic regulation predicted greater school value, deeper approaches to learning, and less work avoidance. External regulation and introjection did not predict positive outcomes.
- **Ryan & Connell (1989):** U.S. elementary students with more autonomous motivations showed more positive effort and adjustment. This study established the self-regulation questionnaire approach for measuring internalization in schools.
- A large literature spanning cultures, age groups, and measurement instruments shows a consistent pattern: autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation predict better engagement, learning, and adjustment than controlled forms (Burton et al., 2006; Chatzisarantis et al., 2003; Grolnick et al., 1991; Guay et al., 2010).

### What Facilitates Internalization in Schools

**Autonomy support from teachers:**
- **Jang, Reeve, & Deci (2010); Skinner & Belmont (1993):** Teachers' autonomy support positively predicted more internalized motivation, which in turn predicted a host of positive educational outcomes.
- **Hardre & Reeve (2003):** Rural American high schools -- perceived teacher autonomy support predicted students' autonomous motivation and perceived competence, which predicted performance and intention to stay in school.
- **Reeve, Jang, Hardre, & Omura (2002):** Providing an autonomy-supportive rationale for why a learning activity matters led college students to more fully internalize the regulation. Extended findings of Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, & Leone (1994).

**Structure (when autonomy-supportive):**
- **Vansteenkiste, Sierens, et al. (2012):** High autonomy support + clear structure = high autonomous motivation, deep learning, effective self-regulation strategies, low anxiety. Structure without autonomy support is ineffective.
- **Jang et al. (2010):** Structure contributed to behavioral engagement beyond autonomy support alone, but autonomy support was the stronger predictor.

**Relatedness:**
- Students are more likely to internalize values and practices of those with whom they feel connected. The need for relatedness is especially important for internalization to even begin (OIT; Ch. 8 of SDT).

**Black & Deci (2000):** Organic chemistry students. Autonomy-supportive instructors facilitated increases in autonomous self-regulation over the semester. Students with higher autonomous motivation earned higher grades even after controlling for ability (GPA, SAT). The course performance was importantly determined by the students' autonomous motivation, not just ability.

### Post-Secondary and Beyond

- **Litalien, Ludtke, Parker, & Trautwein (2013):** Students' autonomous motivation for goal pursuits at high school graduation predicted psychological well-being 2 years later, regardless of post-high-school path (university, vocational, noneducational).
- **Levesque, Zuehlke, Stanek, & Ryan (2004):** In both American and German college students, perceptions of autonomy support predicted autonomous motivation and perceived competence, which predicted well-being.

### Cross-Cultural Consistency

The pattern of autonomous motivation predicting better engagement, learning, and well-being has been replicated across Western and Eastern cultures, including individualistic and collectivistic societies. This supports SDT's claim that basic psychological needs are universal, even though the contents of what is internalized may differ across cultures.

## Mechanisms

1. **Relatedness as entry point:** Students first internalize values and behaviors of those with whom they feel connected -- teachers, parents, peers. Without a sense of relatedness, internalization does not begin.
2. **Competence support:** Students are more ready to internalize behaviors they can efficaciously enact and values they can comprehend. When demands exceed competence, internalization stalls.
3. **Autonomy support as integration catalyst:** For internalization to proceed beyond introjection to identified and integrated regulation, the need for autonomy must be supported. Autonomy support allows students to explore, understand, and personally endorse the value of academic work.
4. **Rationale provision:** Explaining the "why" behind requirements helps students connect academic tasks to their own values and goals, facilitating identified regulation.
5. **Choice and perspective-taking:** When teachers offer choices and take students' perspectives, students experience academic regulation as more volitional, promoting deeper internalization.

## Applications

- **Teaching practice:** Provide rationale for assignments and requirements; connect material to students' lives and values; offer choices in how to demonstrate learning; minimize controlling language
- **Curriculum design:** Help students see the personal relevance and intrinsic value of subject matter, not just its instrumental value for grades or career
- **Assessment:** Use formative, informational assessment that supports competence awareness rather than controlling, normative grading
- **School climate:** Foster a sense of community and belonging (relatedness) alongside autonomy support and competence-building structure
- **Parent involvement:** Parents and teachers who provide need-supportive environments both contribute to students' internalization (effects shift from parents being more influential in elementary years to teachers being more influential in secondary years; Deci et al., 1992)
