# Investigative Negotiation

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Investigative negotiation is both a mind-set and a methodology developed by [[malhotra|Deepak Malhotra]] and [[bazerman|Max Bazerman]] in [[negotiation-genius|Negotiation Genius]]. It is the approach of treating every negotiation as a detective would treat a crime scene — the goal is to learn as much as possible about the situation and the people involved before, during, and after the negotiation. Rather than focusing on making a persuasive pitch ("selling"), investigative negotiators focus on discovering the other side's interests, priorities, needs, and constraints — even when the other party is reluctant or unwilling to share this information.

## Key Findings

- **The Chris exclusivity story:** A Fortune 500 executive asked one simple question — "why?" — and resolved an impasse his entire team had failed to resolve for weeks. The supplier refused exclusivity not for financial reasons but to honor a promise to sell 250 lbs/year to his cousin. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch3-investigative]])
- **Most of the investigative agreements described in the book required neither party to make a substantive concession.** In the exclusivity case, the cab ride case, and the vote-trading case, both parties got what they wanted by discovering that their interests were actually compatible despite incompatible demands. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch3-investigative]])
- **Investigative negotiators never stop learning** — not even when the deal is lost. When Linda lost a deal, she called to ask *why*, discovered the client valued features she had stripped out, submitted a revised offer, and won the client over. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch3-investigative]])

## The Seven Principles

1. **Don't just ask what — ask *why*** — focus on *why* people want what they want, not just *what* they want
2. **Seek to reconcile interests, not demands** — demands may be incompatible, but underlying interests are often reconcilable
3. **Create common ground with uncommon allies** — value-creating opportunities exist even between competitors (co-opetition, per Brandenburger & Nalebuff)
4. **Interpret demands as opportunities** — every demand reveals information about the other party's interests; ask "What can I learn from this demand?"
5. **Don't dismiss anything as 'their problem'** — the other party's constraints diminish value for both parties; solving their problem can create value for you
6. **Don't let negotiations end with a rejection of your offer** — end with "yes" or an explanation of "why not"; "why not" is as important as "why"
7. **Understand the difference between selling and negotiating** — selling = arguing the merits of your case; negotiating = also focusing on the other side's perspective

## Five Strategies for Eliciting Information from Reticent Negotiators

1. **Build trust and share information** — speak their language, increase ties, build trust outside deal contexts
2. **Ask questions — especially if surprised or skeptical** — use indirect questions; the important thing is knowing *how* to ask
3. **Give away some information** — leverage the norm of [[reciprocity-principle|reciprocity]]; share *relative* priorities incrementally
4. **Negotiate multiple issues simultaneously** — forces the other party to implicitly reveal priorities
5. **Make multiple offers simultaneously** — two offers of equal value to you that differ in structure; their preference reveals their priorities

## Mechanisms

Investigative negotiation works by overcoming the [[fixed-pie-bias]] — the default assumption that the other party's interests are diametrically opposed to yours. By actively investigating interests rather than accepting demands at face value, negotiators discover that:

1. **Compatible issues exist** — sometimes both parties want the same thing but assume they don't (Thompson's incompatibility bias research: 39% of negotiators failed to agree even when they wanted the same outcome)
2. **Trade-offs are possible** — parties have different priorities across issues, enabling logrolling
3. **Creative solutions exist** — constraints are often specific and solvable once understood (as in the shipping cost example with HomeStuff/Kogs)

## Applications

- **Breaking impasses:** When negotiations stall on positions, shift to investigating *why* each side wants what they want
- **Job negotiations:** Ask indirect questions about the employer's constraints, priorities, and flexibility
- **Vendor negotiations:** Investigate the other side's cost structure, delivery constraints, and alternative customers
- **Dispute resolution:** Ask why the other party is behaving "irrationally" — they may have hidden constraints or hidden interests
