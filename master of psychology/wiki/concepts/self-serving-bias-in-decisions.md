# Self-Serving Bias in Decisions

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Self-serving bias in decisions is the systematic tendency to unconsciously interpret information, select fairness criteria, and perceive situations in ways that favor one's own interests. Unlike deliberate self-interest, self-serving reasoning operates below awareness: people genuinely believe their self-serving interpretations are fair and objective.

## Key Findings

- **U.S. News survey:** 85% said opponents should pay legal costs when they lose, but only 44% agreed they should pay when they themselves lose -- identical principle, opposite application depending on self-interest ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch7]])
- **Hastorf & Cantril (1954):** Princeton and Dartmouth students watched the same football film. Each group saw the opposing team as more aggressive and penalizable. They literally "saw a different game."
- **Dawson, Gilovich, & Regan (2002):** People apply different evidentiary standards based on desirability. Favorable information triggers "Can I believe this?" (low bar). Unfavorable information triggers "Must I believe this?" (high bar).
- **Ditto & Lopez (1992):** When evidence suggested a friendly coworker was smarter, people stopped searching quickly. When evidence favored an unfriendly coworker, they kept searching for contradictory evidence.
- **Balcetis & Dunning (2006):** Self-serving bias operates at the level of perception itself. Participants hoping to see a farm animal to avoid a veggie smoothie literally could not perceive an ambiguous image as a seal -- motivated perception at a pre-conscious level.
- **Pronin et al. (2004):** People believe they are less biased than others, even when shown evidence of their own bias. This creates a "bias blind spot" ([[bias-blind-spot]]).
- **Messick & Sentis (1983):** People first determine their preferred outcome based on self-interest, then select fairness criteria to justify it -- the selection of "fair" rules is itself self-serving.
- **Moore, Tetlock, Tanlu, & Bazerman (2006):** Auditors, CEOs, doctors, and politicians rationalize self-serving behavior while believing they are acting fairly. The most effective self-deception is unconscious (von Hippel & Trivers, 2011).

## Mechanisms

Self-serving bias in decisions combines:
1. **Confirmation bias:** Selectively seeking and weighting evidence that supports desired conclusions
2. **Motivated reasoning:** Asymmetric evidentiary standards ("Can I?" vs. "Must I?")
3. **Motivated perception:** Pre-conscious filtering of sensory information to match desires
4. **Post-hoc rationalization:** Selecting fairness criteria that happen to support the preferred outcome

The bias is particularly dangerous because it is unconscious. People sincerely believe in their own objectivity, which makes them resistant to feedback about their bias.

## Applications

- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Both parties perceive their own proposals as fair and the other's as self-serving. This creates impasse even when both genuinely believe they are being reasonable. Babcock, Loewenstein, Issacharoff, & Camerer (1995) demonstrated this experimentally.
- **Organizational conflict:** Departments competing for resources each sincerely believe their allocation is "fair"
- **International relations:** U.S. and China each blame the other for climate change while selecting different temporal baselines for "fair" emissions targets
- **Ethical decision-making:** Self-serving bias enables unethical behavior without conscious awareness -- the person genuinely believes their actions are justified
