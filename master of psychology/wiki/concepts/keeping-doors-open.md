# Keeping Doors Open

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

Keeping doors open is the irrational compulsion to maintain options and alternatives even when doing so is costly and the options themselves hold little value. People expend significant resources — time, money, attention, emotional energy — to prevent options from disappearing, even when they would be better off letting them close and focusing on their best available choice. The effect persists even when participants know the exact payoffs, have extensive practice, and can "resurrect" closed options at no cost.

This is not strategic option-keeping (which can be rational when uncertainty is high). It is a compulsive, loss-driven behavior that persists even when the math clearly shows it is self-defeating.

## Ariely and Shin's Door Game Experiments

Jiwoong Shin and Dan Ariely designed a series of computer-based experiments at MIT to isolate and study the door-keeping compulsion. The paradigm is elegant: participants click on doors to earn money, and under certain conditions, unvisited doors shrink and disappear. The experiments systematically vary conditions to identify what drives the behavior.

### Variant 1: The Basic Door Game

- Three doors on screen, each hiding a room that pays a random amount per click (different averages per room)
- Participants have 100 clicks total to maximize earnings
- **Control condition:** Doors stay open regardless of clicking behavior. Participants quickly identify the best-paying door and exploit it. Efficient.
- **Disappearing-doors condition:** Any door not clicked within the last 12 clicks shrinks and eventually vanishes. Once gone, it cannot be reopened.
- **Result:** Participants in the disappearing-doors condition earned approximately **15% less** than control participants. They wasted clicks keeping low-value doors alive instead of exploiting their best option.

### Variant 2: Cost of Switching

To ensure that click-wasting was driven by door-preservation (not mere exploration), Ariely and Shin added an explicit cost to switching doors. Each switch cost one click. This should have *increased* focus on the best door.

**Result:** Participants still compulsively visited disappearing doors, even when switching was expensive. The door-keeping compulsion overpowered the financial incentive to stay put.

### Variant 3: Known Payoffs

Perhaps participants were exploring because they were uncertain about which door was best. To test this, Ariely and Shin revealed the exact payoff ranges for each door. Participants knew precisely which door paid the most.

**Result:** Even with perfect information, participants still wasted clicks preserving low-value doors. The compulsion persisted even when uncertainty — the rational justification for exploration — was eliminated.

### Variant 4: Hundreds of Practice Trials

Perhaps participants needed more experience to learn that door-keeping was costly. Ariely and Shin gave participants hundreds of practice rounds before the real game.

**Result:** Practice did not eliminate the effect. Participants continued to waste clicks keeping worthless doors alive, even after extensive experience with the consequences of doing so.

### Variant 5: Small Doors (Low-Value Options)

Perhaps participants were distracted by the dramatic visual of doors shrinking. Ariely and Shin made the disappearing doors very small — visually trivial, easy to ignore.

**Result:** Participants still chased them. Even a small, visually unimpressive closing option triggered the preservation response.

### Variant 6: The Reincarnation Variant

This is the most telling condition. Disappeared doors could be brought back with a single click (zero real loss). The door was not permanently gone — it was merely hidden and instantly retrievable.

**Result:** Participants **still** chased disappearing doors. The mere visual of a closing option was sufficient to trigger the compulsion, even when the "loss" was entirely reversible at trivial cost. This rules out rational loss-prevention as the explanation. The behavior is driven by something deeper — a visceral, emotional response to the appearance of a closing option.

### Summary of Door Game Findings

| Condition | Eliminated rational explanation? | Effect persisted? |
|-----------|--------------------------------|-------------------|
| Cost of switching | Yes (switching penalized) | Yes |
| Known payoffs | Yes (no uncertainty) | Yes |
| Practice trials | Yes (experience) | Yes |
| Small doors | Yes (visual salience) | Yes |
| Reincarnation | Yes (no real loss) | Yes |

The compulsion to keep doors open is **robust to every rational modification**. It is not driven by uncertainty, inexperience, visual distraction, or actual loss. It is driven by a deep, loss-averse response to the mere idea of a closing option.

## The Buridan's Ass Problem

The keeping-doors-open compulsion creates a secondary pathology: **costly indecision**. When two or more options are similarly attractive, the inability to close any door leads to paralysis — and the cost of not deciding often exceeds the difference between the options.

The reference is to Buridan's Ass — the philosophical thought experiment of a donkey placed equidistant between two identical bales of hay, who starves because it cannot decide which to eat. The parable is absurd, but the human equivalent is common:

- A student accepted to two equally good graduate programs agonizes for months, missing housing deadlines and orientation events at both
- A couple deciding between two houses of similar value delays so long that both sell to other buyers
- A manager choosing between two qualified candidates leaves the position unfilled, burning the team out

In each case, **the cost of the indecision vastly exceeds the difference between the options.** Either choice would have been fine. The failure to choose is the real loss.

Ariely connects this to [[loss-aversion]]: choosing option A means "losing" option B, and loss aversion makes that loss loom larger than the gain from committing. So people delay, hedge, keep both options alive — and pay the hidden cost of paralysis.

## Opportunity Cost Blindness

The keeping-doors-open bias is amplified by **opportunity cost blindness** — the systematic failure to account for what you give up by preserving options. Every click spent keeping a low-value door alive is a click not spent on the high-value door. Every hour spent maintaining a dying relationship is an hour not invested in a thriving one. Every dollar spent on an unused subscription is a dollar not saved or spent on something valuable.

Frederick, Novemsky, Wang, Dhar, & Nowlis (2009) demonstrated that people rarely spontaneously consider opportunity costs. In one study, consumers asked "Would you buy this $15 DVD?" were significantly less likely to buy it when the alternative was phrased as "Would you buy this $15 DVD or keep the $15 for other purchases?" — simply reminding people that money spent here cannot be spent elsewhere changed behavior. Without the prompt, the opportunity cost was invisible.

This connects to the door game: participants focusing on the shrinking door were not simultaneously calculating the opportunity cost of their door-preserving clicks. The loss of the door was vivid and salient; the loss of earnings from suboptimal clicking was abstract and invisible.

## Connection to Loss Aversion

The keeping-doors-open effect is fundamentally driven by [[loss-aversion]] — Kahneman and Tversky's finding that losses are psychologically approximately twice as painful as equivalent gains are pleasant. Closing a door is experienced as a loss, even when the door has low or zero value. The pain of that loss exceeds the pleasure of the gain from focusing resources on the best option.

This explains the otherwise puzzling reincarnation result: even when the "loss" is completely reversible, the visual of a closing door triggers the loss-averse response. The emotional system responds to the appearance of loss, not to its actual magnitude or permanence.

## Connection to Sunk Cost

Keeping doors open also connects to the [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] through the related phenomenon of **escalation of commitment.** Once you have invested in keeping a door open (clicks, time, money, emotional energy), that investment becomes a sunk cost that further motivates continued investment. "I've already spent 20 clicks keeping this door alive; I can't let those clicks go to waste." This creates a vicious cycle: the more you invest in preserving an option, the harder it becomes to let it go.

## Historical and Literary Parallels

### Xiang Yu's Burned Ships (208 BC)

General Xiang Yu of Chu crossed the Zhang River to battle the Qin army, then ordered his troops to sink their boats and smash their cooking pots. With retreat impossible, his soldiers fought with desperate intensity and won a decisive victory despite being outnumbered. By deliberately closing the "retreat" door, Xiang Yu eliminated the keeping-doors-open problem and focused all resources on the single remaining option.

### Cortez at Veracruz (1519)

Hernan Cortez reportedly scuttled his ships after landing in Mexico, preventing his men from retreating to Cuba. Whether historically accurate or not, the strategic logic is identical to Xiang Yu's: destroy the retreat option to focus commitment.

### Erich Fromm's "Escape from Freedom"

Fromm argued that modern abundance creates an existential burden: when you can be anything and do anything, the pressure to keep all doors open becomes paralyzing. The promise of unlimited choice feels like freedom but functions as a trap. True freedom, Fromm suggested, comes from commitment — from voluntarily closing doors and investing deeply in the ones that remain.

## Applications

- **Career decisions:** When two career paths are similarly attractive, agonizing over the choice costs more than picking either one. Set a decision deadline, make the best available choice, then close the door and commit. The opportunity cost of continued deliberation almost always exceeds the difference between the options.
- **Parenting:** Spreading children across every activity "just in case" prevents depth in any one area. Deliberately closing some doors (not auditioning for the school play AND the travel soccer team AND the robotics club) creates focus. Research on expertise consistently shows that depth beats breadth.
- **Personal relationships:** Maintaining dying relationships "just in case" prevents investment in viable ones (Dana's two-boyfriend example in Ariely). The time and emotional energy spent preserving a low-value relationship is time not invested in a high-value one.
- **Consumer behavior:** Buying products with excess features "just in case" or keeping unused subscriptions active reflects the same compulsion. Audit recurring subscriptions quarterly and cancel anything unused — the "loss" of the option is almost always less costly than the ongoing payment.
- **Business strategy:** Xiang Yu's "burn the ships" approach — deliberately eliminating the retreat option — is the antithesis of this bias and can powerfully focus effort. Companies that try to compete in too many markets simultaneously often underperform those that commit to a focused strategy.
- **Digital life:** Keeping browser tabs open "for later," maintaining accounts on platforms you never use, holding onto digital files "just in case" — these are low-stakes versions of the same compulsion. The cost is attentional rather than financial, but attention may be the scarcest resource of all.

## Open Questions

- Does the effect scale with the number of options? Is keeping 10 doors open proportionally worse than keeping 3? Iyengar and Lepper's (2000) jam study suggests that more options increases paralysis, but the precise relationship between option count and door-keeping is understudied.
- Are there personality differences in susceptibility? Schwartz's maximizers (who always seek the best option) vs. satisficers (who accept "good enough") from *The Paradox of Choice* likely differ in door-keeping behavior, but this has not been directly tested in the Ariely/Shin paradigm.
- Can pre-commitment mechanisms (like Xiang Yu's ship-burning) be designed into decision environments to counteract this bias? What does a "ship-burning" interface look like for career choices or consumer decisions?
- How does this interact with [[regret-avoidance]]? Is door-keeping driven by anticipated regret about closed options? If so, can interventions that reduce anticipated regret (such as emphasizing the reversibility of decisions) reduce door-keeping?
- In the digital age, where the cost of keeping options open has decreased dramatically (free accounts, cloud storage, bookmarks), has the bias become more or less problematic? The cost per option has dropped, but the sheer number of options has exploded.
