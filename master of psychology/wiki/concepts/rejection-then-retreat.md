# Rejection-Then-Retreat (Door-in-the-Face)

**Phase(s):** 1, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The rejection-then-retreat technique (also called door-in-the-face) is a compliance strategy where the requester first makes a large request that is sure to be rejected, then retreats to a smaller request — which is the one they actually wanted. The retreat is perceived as a concession, triggering an obligation to reciprocate with a concession of one's own (i.e., compliance). The technique simultaneously engages the [[contrast-principle]], making the second request appear smaller than it would in isolation.

## Key Findings

- **Zoo Trip Experiment** (Cialdini, Vincent, Lewis, Catalan, Wheeler, & Darby, 1975): Direct request to chaperon delinquents to the zoo → 17% compliance. First ask for 2 hours/week for 2 years, *then* retreat to zoo trip → **50% compliance** (3x increase). The second request was objectively large — it just needed to be *smaller* than the first.
- **Blood donation study** (Cialdini & Ascani, 1976): Rejection-then-retreat targets were more likely to agree to donate *and* more likely to agree to future donations (84% vs. 43% gave phone numbers for future contact).
- **Canadian volunteer study** (Miller, Seligman, Clark, & Bush, 1976): Not only did rejection-then-retreat produce more verbal agreement (76% vs. 29%), but those who agreed were more likely to **actually show up** (85% vs. 50%).
- **UCLA bargaining study** (Benton, Kelley, & Liebling, 1972): Three key findings about the concession strategy — it produced (1) the most money, (2) the most perceived responsibility in targets, and (3) the most satisfaction in targets.

## Mechanisms

Three reinforcing forces make this technique exceptionally powerful:

1. **Reciprocal concessions** (from [[reciprocation]]): The retreat *is* a concession, and the rule of reciprocity obligates a return concession — compliance.
2. **Perceptual contrast** (from [[contrast-principle]]): The second request seems smaller against the backdrop of the first, just as a sweater seems cheaper after buying a suit.
3. **Sweet side effects**:
   - **Responsibility**: Targets feel they *dictated* the final agreement (because they "forced" the requester to retreat), making them more committed to following through
   - **Satisfaction**: Agreements forged through concessions feel more satisfying than those reached directly. Satisfied targets are more likely to agree to future requests.

### Boundary Condition
If the initial request is so extreme as to be seen as unreasonable or not in good faith, the tactic **backfires** (Schwarzwald, Raz, & Zvibel, 1979, Bar-Ilan University). The retreat from an absurd position is not perceived as a genuine concession and is not reciprocated. The skilled practitioner's initial position is "exaggerated just enough to allow for a series of small reciprocal concessions" (Thompson, 1990).

## Applications

- **Cialdini's Boy Scout encounter**: $5 circus tickets (rejected) → $1 chocolate bars (accepted). Cialdini ended up with unwanted chocolate bars and $2 less.
- **The Watergate break-in**: G. Gordon Liddy's $1M → $500K → $250K proposal sequence. Only Frederick LaRue (who missed the first two meetings and thus lacked the contrast) objected.
- **Labor negotiations**: Unions routinely open with extreme demands they don't expect to win, creating room for "concessions" that draw real concessions from the other side.
- **Door-to-door sales**: If the sale is refused, retreat to a request for referral names. The referral request is seen as a concession from the purchase request.
- **Warranty/service contract sales**: Offer 3-year coverage first (rejected), then retreat to 1-year coverage (accepted at 70% rate vs. industry 40%).
