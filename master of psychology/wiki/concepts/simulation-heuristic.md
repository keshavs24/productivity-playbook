# Simulation Heuristic

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The simulation heuristic is a mental operation in which people judge the probability, propensity, or closeness-to-occurrence of an event by mentally "running" a model of the situation -- constructing scenarios or counterfactual alternatives -- and using the **ease** of reaching a particular outcome as a proxy for its likelihood. It functions like an internal Monte Carlo simulation: the more easily the mental model produces an outcome, the more probable or propensious it seems.

## Key Findings

- Introduced by Kahneman & Tversky (1982) as an extension of [[availability]] -- specifically, "availability for construction" as opposed to availability by retrieval ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch14-simulation-heuristic]])
- The heuristic serves at least five judgmental functions: prediction, probability assessment, conditional probability, counterfactual assessment, and causality testing
- Mental simulation follows robust, systematic rules -- most importantly the [[downhill-change-rule]]: people undo events by restoring normal/expected values, not by introducing exceptions
- The "Mr. Tees and Mr. Crane" problem demonstrates that identical objective outcomes produce different emotional reactions because of differential ease of counterfactual simulation
- Scenario construction (a deliberate application of the heuristic) produces systematic biases: favoring dramatic transitions over gradual change, and generating planning optimism

## Mechanisms

The simulation heuristic works because mental model construction is a fast, natural cognitive operation. People instinctively "run forward" from initial conditions or "run backward" from outcomes, constructing narratives that connect states. The ease of this construction is then substituted for probability -- an easy question replacing a hard one.

Key constraints on simulation:
- **Downhill bias:** Simulations strongly prefer restoring normalcy over introducing exceptions (see [[downhill-change-rule]])
- **Focus rule:** Modifications center on the main object of attention, not peripheral elements
- **Asymmetric distance:** The psychological distance from an exception to the norm is shorter than from the norm to the exception
- **No horizontal changes:** Arbitrary substitutions of equally likely values essentially never occur

## Applications

- **Planning and project management:** The simulation heuristic explains [[focalism|planning optimism]] -- each link in a plan seems plausible (downhill), so the overall plan seems likely to succeed, even though the cumulative probability of at least one failure is high
- **Risk assessment:** Risks produced by slow, incremental processes are underestimated because they lack the dramatic transitions that simulations favor
- **Legal and moral judgment:** Causality testing via simulation ("Would it have happened anyway?") shapes attributions of blame and responsibility
- **Emotional forecasting:** [[counterfactual-thinking|Counterfactual emotions]] (regret, frustration, grief) are calibrated by the ease of simulating a better alternative
- **Negotiation:** Post-negotiation satisfaction depends on how easily one can simulate having obtained a better deal

## Open Questions
- Is the simulation heuristic a genuinely independent heuristic or a subtype of [[availability]]? Kahneman & Tversky argue it deserves independent status, but the boundary is debatable.
- How does the simulation heuristic interact with expertise? Do experts run "better" simulations with more realistic constraints, or are they equally subject to the downhill bias?
- What is the relationship between the simulation heuristic and [[system-1-system-2]]? Is scenario construction a System 2 operation that inherits System 1 biases?
