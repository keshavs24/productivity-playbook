# Winner's Curse

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The winner's curse is the phenomenon in which the winner of an auction or competitive bidding situation systematically overpays, because winning itself is evidence that the winner's valuation was the highest -- and therefore most likely to be above the true value. More broadly, it refers to the tendency in negotiation to fail to consider the other party's decision-making process and the information conveyed by their willingness to accept a deal.

## Key Findings

- **Bazerman & Samuelson "Acquiring a Company" problem:** A company worth between $0-$100 to the current owner (uniformly distributed) is worth 50% more under the acquirer's management. The rational bid is $0, because winning the auction reveals the company is worth less than your bid. Most MBA students bid $50-$75, failing to consider the adverse selection problem. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch4]])
- **Ball, Bazerman, and Carroll (1991):** Tested whether people could learn to avoid the winner's curse through experience. Participants played 20 rounds of the Acquiring a Company problem with real money and full feedback. Only 5 of 72 MBA participants learned the correct response. Experience alone does not cure the bias. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch12]])
- **Bereby-Meyer and Grosskopf (2008):** Even hundreds of trials do not lead most participants to solve the problem -- the bias is deeply resistant to learning
- **Kagel and Levin (1986):** In auction settings, the market as a whole "learns" over time, but mainly because the most aggressive bidders go broke and drop out -- not because individual bidders improve

## Mechanisms

The winner's curse results from:
1. **Failure to consider the other party's cognitions:** The bidder does not think about what the other party's acceptance of the bid reveals about the item's true value
2. **[[bounded-awareness]]:** People fail to consider information that is available but not immediately salient -- specifically, the selection bias inherent in winning
3. **Competitive arousal:** The desire to "win" the auction overrides careful analysis of what winning actually means
4. **[[overconfidence-in-calibration]]:** Bidders are overconfident in their ability to accurately value the item

## Applications

- **M&A decisions:** Before making an acquisition bid, ask: "If they accept this offer, what does that tell me about the true value?" The seller has better information about the company than the buyer. Acceptance of a high bid is itself informative.
- **Auction bidding:** Set maximum bids in advance and account for the winner's curse by bidding below your estimated value
- **Hiring:** If a candidate accepts an offer quickly and enthusiastically, consider whether you overpaid relative to their alternatives
- **General negotiation (Phase 5):** Always consider: "Why is the other party willing to agree to this?" Their willingness to accept may reveal information that changes the value of the deal

## Open Questions
- Can structured decision aids (e.g., requiring bidders to write down "what does it mean if they accept?") reduce the winner's curse in practice?
- How does the number of bidders affect the severity of the winner's curse?
- Is the winner's curse more severe in domains where the true value is harder to determine (e.g., art, technology companies)?
