# Functional Fixedness

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Functional fixedness is a cognitive bias in which people are unable to see objects as serving functions beyond their conventional or typical use. People become "fixed" on an object's standard function and fail to represent it as having novel potential, which blocks the application of problem-solving operators that require unconventional use of available objects.

## Key Findings

- **Two-string problem** (Maier, 1931): Two strings hang from the ceiling too far apart to grasp both at once. The solution is to tie pliers to one string as a pendulum weight. Only 39% of participants found this solution within 10 minutes because they did not perceive pliers as a weight. ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch8]])
- **Candle problem** (Duncker, 1945): Mount a candle on a wall using a box of tacks and matches. The solution is to empty the box, tack it to the wall as a platform. When the box was filled with tacks (reinforcing its container function), difficulty increased significantly because participants saw the box as a container rather than a platform.
- **Training overcomes fixedness:** McCaffrey (2012) trained participants to decompose objects into their primitive parts and features (material, shape, size). For the tack box, they would describe "a flat piece of cardboard" and "its shape." This training improved solution rates on functional-fixedness problems from 49% to 83%.
- Functional fixedness reflects the broader principle that problem representation determines which operators are available -- when an object is represented only by its conventional function, operators requiring unconventional use cannot apply.

## Mechanisms

Functional fixedness operates through problem representation. Objects are encoded in memory with their typical function as a central feature. During problem solving, this representation makes conventional-function operators highly available while blocking novel-function operators. The bias is strengthened by contextual cues that reinforce conventional use (e.g., a box full of tacks reinforces its container function).

## Applications

- When stuck on a problem, systematically list the **properties** of available objects rather than their **functions** -- this breaks functional fixedness
- In creative work, practice "generic-parts technique" -- describe objects by their material, shape, and size rather than their name or category
- Recognize that functional fixedness is a specific case of inappropriate problem representation -- the broader principle applies wherever conventional framing blocks creative solutions
