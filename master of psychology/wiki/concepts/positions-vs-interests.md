# Positions vs. Interests

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

The positions-versus-interests distinction is the central insight of [[principled-negotiation]] and the most important idea in [[getting-to-yes|Getting to Yes]]. A **position** is a concrete demand or stated stance — what a negotiator says they want. An **interest** is the underlying need, desire, concern, or fear that motivated that position — *why* they want it.

Positions are what you have decided upon. Interests are what caused you to so decide. Interests are the silent movers behind the hubbub of positions.

The insight is deceptively simple but transformative: once negotiators shift from arguing about positions to exploring interests, the space of possible agreements expands dramatically. Positions appear to be in direct conflict; interests often overlap, complement, or can be traded.

## The Orange Negotiation

The orange negotiation is the classic teaching parable for the positions-versus-interests distinction, used in virtually every negotiation course worldwide:

**The setup:** Two siblings are fighting over a single orange. Each wants the orange. Their positions are incompatible — there is only one orange.

**The positional approach:** A compromise would be to cut the orange in half. Each sibling gets half an orange. Both are partially satisfied, neither is fully satisfied. This is the standard positional "split the difference" outcome.

**The interest-based approach:** A parent asks each child *why* they want the orange.
- Child A wants the orange **to eat the fruit.** Their interest is the pulp.
- Child B wants the orange **to use the zest for baking.** Their interest is the rind.

**The solution:** Child A gets all the fruit. Child B gets all the rind. Both are 100% satisfied — a result impossible under positional bargaining, which would have given each child half the fruit and half the rind (50% satisfaction for both).

**Why this matters:** The parable illustrates several key principles:
1. **Positions mask compatible interests.** The positions ("I want the orange") appeared directly opposed. The interests (fruit vs. rind) were perfectly compatible.
2. **Compromise is often suboptimal.** "Split the difference" is the default positional solution, but it leaves value on the table when interests are compatible.
3. **Asking "why?" reveals the solution.** The entire shift from impossible conflict to easy resolution came from a single question: "Why do you want the orange?"
4. **The pie is not fixed.** Positional thinking assumes a fixed pie (one orange, to be divided). Interest-based thinking reveals that the pie has different components that different parties value differently, allowing integrative agreements that expand the total value.

## Camp David in Full: Egypt, Israel, and the Sinai (1978)

The Camp David Accords are the paradigm case of positions-versus-interests at the scale of international diplomacy. Fisher, Ury, and Patton use Camp David as the central example in *Getting to Yes* because it demonstrates how apparently irreconcilable positions can yield to creative solutions when interests are identified.

### The Positions (Irreconcilable)

- **Egypt's position:** Return every inch of the Sinai Peninsula, which Israel had occupied since the 1967 Six-Day War. Complete, unconditional withdrawal. Not one square meter of Egyptian territory under Israeli control.
- **Israel's position:** Keep at least some of the Sinai. Israel had invested heavily in military infrastructure, settlements, and airfields in the Sinai. Withdrawal meant losing strategic depth against a potential Egyptian attack.

These positions were **directly contradictory.** If Egypt got every inch, Israel got nothing. If Israel kept some, Egypt's sovereignty was violated. Positional bargaining would focus on where to draw the line — how many inches of the Sinai to return — producing a zero-sum competition over territory.

### The Interests (Compatible)

President Jimmy Carter and his team, using what would later be codified as the principled negotiation approach, pushed both sides to articulate their underlying interests:

- **Egypt's interest: SOVEREIGNTY.** The Sinai had been Egyptian territory for millennia. Its occupation was a matter of national humiliation. Egypt needed to restore its sovereign authority over its own land. The flag had to fly over the entire Sinai. This was a matter of national identity, not just geography.
- **Israel's interest: SECURITY.** Israel had no historical attachment to the Sinai itself. What Israel feared was Egyptian tanks massing on its border — a repeat of the 1967 and 1973 wars. Israel needed assurance that returning the Sinai would not create a military threat. The interest was protection from attack, not control of sand.

### The Solution (Integrative)

Once the interests were clear, the solution was almost obvious:

**Full Egyptian sovereignty over a demilitarized Sinai.**

- Egypt got every inch of the Sinai back. The Egyptian flag flew over the entire peninsula. Sovereignty was fully restored.
- Israel got a demilitarized buffer zone — the Sinai was returned without military forces. Egyptian tanks could not mass on Israel's border because military presence in the Sinai was prohibited by treaty.

Both interests were fully satisfied. Neither position was compromised. The solution was invisible as long as the negotiation focused on positions (how many inches to return) but became obvious once it focused on interests (sovereignty vs. security).

### What Camp David Teaches

1. **Incompatible positions do not mean incompatible interests.** The positions were binary (return or keep territory). The interests were orthogonal (sovereignty vs. security) and could be satisfied simultaneously.
2. **Creative solutions require interest-level thinking.** No amount of positional compromise (returning 80% of the Sinai, or 90%) would have satisfied both sides. Only the interest-level solution — full sovereignty plus demilitarization — achieved full mutual satisfaction.
3. **The negotiator's job is to reframe.** Carter's contribution was not a clever proposal but a reframing of the conversation: from "how much territory?" to "what does each side actually need?" The reframing was the breakthrough.

## The Library Window Story

Fisher, Ury, and Patton's simplest teaching example:

**The situation:** Two people in a library. One wants the window open; the other wants it closed. They argue about how far to leave it open — half open, one-third open, two-thirds open. No compromise satisfies either party.

**The librarian asks "why?"**
- Person A wants fresh air (interest: ventilation)
- Person B wants to avoid a draft (interest: comfort/avoiding cold air)

**The solution:** The librarian opens a window in the next room — providing fresh air circulation without a direct draft on Person B.

**The principle:** The positions (open vs. closed) appeared binary and incompatible. The interests (fresh air vs. no draft) were compatible. The solution required shifting from the presented dimension (this window) to a broader solution space (any source of ventilation) — which only becomes visible when you understand interests.

## The Five Basic Human Needs as Interests

Fisher, Ury, and Patton identify five basic human needs that commonly underlie negotiating positions. These are the most powerful interests because they are universal, deeply felt, and often unstated:

### 1. Security
The need to feel safe from physical, financial, or psychological harm. This is the interest that drove Israel at Camp David. Security interests underlie positions about contracts, guarantees, warranties, non-compete clauses, and insurance.

**Example:** An employee demanding a long-term contract (position) may be driven by fear of layoff (security interest). Alternatives: severance guarantee, performance-based retention bonus, or a verbal commitment with a strong track record.

### 2. Economic Well-Being
The need for material adequacy — enough money, resources, or financial stability to live comfortably. The most commonly stated interest but rarely the only one.

**Example:** A supplier demanding a 15% price increase (position) may be facing rising material costs (economic interest), but may also be feeling undervalued relative to competitors (recognition interest). Addressing the recognition interest through a preferred vendor designation or public endorsement may reduce the required price increase.

### 3. Belonging (A Sense of Being Part of Something)
The need to feel included, respected as a member, and part of a community or team. This is often the hidden interest in organizational conflicts.

**Example:** A department head blocking a reorganization (position) may fear that their team will be disbanded and they will lose their community (belonging interest). Ensuring the team stays together — even under a different structure — may resolve the resistance.

### 4. Recognition
The need to be acknowledged, valued, and treated with dignity. This is often the most underestimated interest in negotiation. Fisher and Ury cite the Mexico natural gas negotiation: the U.S. attempted to negotiate a price reduction with Mexico using purely economic arguments, but Mexico's interest was recognition as an equal partner. The U.S. team's failure to treat Mexico with dignity derailed a straightforward deal.

**Example:** A colleague who refuses to support your proposal (position) may feel that their expertise was not consulted (recognition interest). Asking for their input — genuinely seeking their expertise — may convert them from opponent to ally.

### 5. Autonomy (Control Over One's Life)
The need to make one's own decisions and not be dictated to. This connects to [[self-determination-theory]] and the basic psychological need for autonomy.

**Example:** A teenager's refusal to follow household rules (position) may be driven by the need for self-determination (autonomy interest). Involving the teenager in *setting* the rules (giving them voice in the process) satisfies the autonomy interest while achieving the parents' goals.

### The Power of Unspoken Needs

These five needs are often the most powerful interests in a negotiation precisely because they are **unspoken.** A buyer fixated on price (stated interest: economic well-being) may be equally or more motivated by fear of being taken advantage of (security), desire to be seen as a skilled negotiator (recognition), or need to make an independent decision without being pressured (autonomy). Addressing only the stated economic interest while ignoring these deeper needs produces technically acceptable deals that feel unsatisfying — and unsatisfying deals tend to unravel.

## Techniques for Uncovering Interests

Fisher, Ury, and Patton recommend several methods:

### Ask "Why?"
The most direct technique: "Why do you want that?" or "What would that give you?" or "What concerns are driving your proposal?" Each "why?" peels back a layer from position to interest.

### Ask "Why Not?"
When you cannot understand why someone will not accept a reasonable proposal, construct their **presently perceived choice** — what they believe they are choosing between. This technique requires putting yourself in their shoes and mapping their decision from their perspective.

Fisher and Ury illustrate this with the **Iranian hostage crisis analysis.** Rather than asking "Why won't Iran release the hostages?" they asked "What is the decision facing an Iranian leader right now?" and mapped the perceived consequences of releasing vs. holding. This revealed that the interests (avoid appearing weak, maintain revolutionary credibility, punish American interference) were political and reputational — not the economic or diplomatic interests the U.S. had been addressing.

### Realize Each Side Has Multiple Interests

A common error is treating each side as having a single interest. In reality, every negotiator represents multiple interests — their own, their organization's, their constituency's, their reputation's. The Israeli negotiators at Camp David were simultaneously serving:
- National security (military interest)
- Settler communities (political interest)
- The Israeli public's desire for peace (social interest)
- Their own political careers (personal interest)

### Make a List and Prioritize

Write down your interests in order of importance. Then write down your best guess of the other side's interests in order of importance. The most creative agreements come from trading items that are high-priority for one side and low-priority for the other — trades that cost little but give a lot.

### Separate Inventing from Deciding

Brainstorm options that address interests without evaluating them. The pressure to commit (which activates positional thinking) must be temporarily removed to allow creative exploration of the interest space. This connects to [[invent-options-for-mutual-gain]].

## Mechanisms

The positions-vs-interests distinction works because:

1. **Positions mask compatible interests.** When people take positions, they present a single solution to a complex problem. Multiple solutions to the same underlying need become invisible. This connects to [[fixed-pie-bias]] — the assumption that the negotiation is zero-sum when it usually is not.

2. **Ego attachment to positions.** As negotiators defend their positions, their egos become identified with them (see [[commitment-and-consistency]]). The more they clarify and defend a position, the more committed they become. Shifting to interests breaks the ego-position bond because interests can be satisfied in multiple ways.

3. **Positions invite counterpositions.** A stated position tells the other side what you want and implies you do not care about what they want. This triggers [[reactive-devaluation]] and defensive responses. Sharing interests, by contrast, invites collaboration.

4. **Interests are more fundamental and stable than positions.** While positions are arbitrary (there are always multiple positions that could satisfy the same interest), interests reflect genuine human needs. Negotiating at the interest level gets closer to the real problem — and closer to real solutions.

5. **Interests expand the negotiation space.** Positions create a one-dimensional negotiation (more for me = less for you). Interests create a multi-dimensional space where trades, creative options, and integrative agreements become possible. The pie is not fixed — it can be expanded when interests are traded efficiently.

## Applications

- **Salary negotiation:** The position is "$120,000." The interests might include financial security, recognition of value, parity with peers, professional growth, work-life balance, and intellectual challenge. Understanding these interests opens options beyond base salary — equity, title, flexibility, development budget, sabbatical, remote work, team composition.

- **Business partnerships:** Position: "We want 60% of the revenue." Interests: covering fixed costs, ensuring commitment, maintaining cash flow, protecting reputation. Revenue-sharing structures, milestone payments, equity arrangements, or guaranteed minimums may satisfy interests better than any fixed percentage split.

- **Family disputes:** "I want the house" (position) may mask interests in stability for children, financial security, neighborhood community, control over living space, or simply not wanting to lose something familiar ([[loss-aversion]]). Understanding these interests may reveal that a different living arrangement satisfies the real needs better than fighting over the specific asset.

- **International relations:** Beyond Camp David, the positions-vs-interests framework has been applied to virtually every major diplomatic negotiation since 1981. The Oslo Accords, the Good Friday Agreement, and numerous trade negotiations have drawn explicitly on principled negotiation concepts.

- **Workplace conflicts:** Two departments fighting over budget allocation (positions) may actually share interests in organizational success, team morale, and individual recognition. Reframing the conversation from "how much do we each get?" to "what do we each need to succeed, and how can we help each other?" often reveals integrative solutions.

- **Customer complaints:** A customer demanding a refund (position) may be motivated by feeling disrespected (recognition), fear of being stuck with a defective product (security), or frustration at wasted time (autonomy). Addressing these interests — through a sincere apology, an upgraded replacement, and expedited service — may cost less than the refund and produce a more satisfied customer.

## Open Questions
- Can interests themselves be misleading? People sometimes confuse what they think they want (stated interests) with what would actually make them better off (true interests). The research on [[affective-forecasting]] suggests people are poor predictors of what will satisfy them. Should negotiators be skeptical of self-reported interests?
- How deep should the interest analysis go? At some point, "interests behind interests" becomes philosophical rather than practical. "Why do you want security?" leads to "because I want to protect my family" leads to "because I love them" — which is true but not actionable.
- Does the positions-to-interests framework work equally well in all cultures? In some high-context cultures, interests are communicated indirectly and asking "why do you want that?" may be seen as confrontational.
- How do power asymmetries affect the framework? When one party has dramatically more power, they may have no incentive to explore interests — they can simply impose their position. Does principled negotiation require a minimum level of power balance to function?
