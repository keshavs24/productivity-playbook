# Werther Effect

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The Werther effect is the tendency for publicized suicides to trigger additional, imitative suicides. Named by sociologist David Phillips (University of California, San Diego) after Goethe's 1774 novel *Die Leiden des jungen Werthers* (The Sorrows of Young Werther), whose fictional hero's suicide sparked a wave of emulative suicides across Europe so severe that several countries banned the book. The Werther effect is a lethal demonstration of [[social-proof]]: troubled individuals learn from a publicized suicide that self-destruction is an "appropriate" action for someone in their situation.

## Key Findings

- **58 excess suicides per story** (Phillips, 1974): Examining US suicide statistics from 1947-1968, Phillips found an average of 58 more suicides than usual within two months of every front-page suicide story
- **Geographic specificity:** Suicides increase principally in regions where the story was highly publicized; areas without coverage show no comparable increase
- **Dose-response relationship:** The wider the publicity given to the first suicide, the greater the number of subsequent suicides
- **Excess deaths, not timing shifts:** Post-story suicide rates do not subsequently drop below baseline — these are people who would otherwise have gone on living
- **1,000% increase in commercial airline crash deaths** after certain highly publicized suicide stories (Phillips, 1979)
- **Automobile fatalities** also spike after front-page suicides (Phillips, 1980)
- **Post-suicide-story crashes are deadlier:** Average fatalities per commercial airline crash are 3x greater if the crash occurs one week after a front-page suicide story; car wreck victims die 4x more quickly than normal
- **Demographic matching of imitators to models:** Young suicide victims produce young lone drivers dying in single-car crashes; older suicide victims produce older drivers dying in crashes
- **Pattern matching extends to type:** Single-victim suicides produce single-fatality wrecks; suicide-murder combinations produce multi-fatality wrecks
- **Temporal pattern:** Peak danger 3-4 days after the story, a secondary peak about a week later, no effect by day 11
- **Copycat homicides** (Phillips, 1983): Heavyweight championship fights on network TV (1973-1978) produced measurable increases in the US homicide rate, with demographic specificity — when a black fighter lost, homicides rose among young black males; when a white fighter lost, among young white males
- **Teenagers most vulnerable:** Impressionable, imitation-prone teenagers are the most frequent victims of copycat suicide (Bollen & Phillips, 1982; Gould & Shaffer, 1986; Phillips & Cartensen, 1986, 1988; Schmidtke & Hafner, 1988)
- **Contagion extends to mass violence:** Airplane hijackings (1970s), product tamperings (1980s, ~30 copycat incidents per nationally publicized case; Toufexis, 1993), school shootings (Littleton 1999 followed by multiple incidents within days)

## Mechanisms

Phillips' insight unifies all post-suicide increases (direct suicides, car crashes, plane crashes) as manifestations of the same phenomenon: **copycat suicide via [[social-proof]].**

1. Troubled individuals learn of another's suicide and, applying the principle of social proof, decide it is an appropriate action for themselves
2. Some commit suicide directly (causing the overt suicide rate to rise)
3. Others wish to disguise their deaths (to protect reputations, spare families, enable insurance payouts) and stage fatal "accidents" — car crashes, plane crashes
4. The [[similarity-and-social-proof]] principle explains the demographic matching: people imitate the suicides of those most *like* them

The disguised-suicide hypothesis is supported by three converging lines of evidence:
- Post-suicide-story crashes are more deadly than typical crashes (consistent with intentional lethality)
- The demographic profile of crash victims matches the suicide-story victim
- The pattern of single vs. multiple victims matches the original story

## Applications

- **Media responsibility:** These data argue strongly against sensationalizing suicide stories. Newspaper editors and broadcasters should consider that their coverage will predictably cause additional deaths
- **Public health:** Understanding the Werther effect has led to media guidelines for responsible reporting of suicide in many countries
- **Travel safety:** Travelers are most at risk 3-4 days after a major suicide story and again about a week later
- **Social proof awareness:** The Werther effect is perhaps the most powerful demonstration that social proof can influence even the most extreme human decisions
