# Escalation of Commitment

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Escalation of commitment is the tendency for decision-makers to continue -- and increase -- investment in a previously selected course of action beyond what rational analysis would prescribe, particularly after receiving negative feedback about the initial decision. It is the behavioral manifestation of the [[sunk-cost-fallacy]], driven by self-justification, [[loss-aversion]], impression management, and competitive dynamics.

## Key Findings

- **Staw (1976) R&D allocation experiment:** Participants who made the initial investment (high-responsibility) allocated significantly more funds to a failing division than those told someone else made the initial decision (low-responsibility). When the initial investment succeeded, allocations were equal. **The mechanism is self-justification** -- negative feedback is dissonant with the initial decision, and further investment resolves the dissonance. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch8]])
- **Staw and Ross (1978):** Escalation is more pronounced when failure can be attributed to external causes (economic downturn) rather than the decision itself
- **Bazerman, Giuliano, & Appelman (1984):** Groups are less likely to escalate than individuals, but groups that escalate do so to a greater degree
- **Schoorman (1988):** Supervisors who participated in hiring decisions positively biased subsequent performance appraisals. Those who disagreed with the hire biased appraisals negatively.
- **Staw & Hoang (1995):** NBA teams gave more playing time and were less likely to trade higher draft picks regardless of actual performance -- sunk cost of draft position influenced playing decisions
- **Friedman (1996):** Mountain climbers escalate commitment in life-or-death conditions. Jeffrey Z. Rubin, escalation scholar, died in a 1995 climbing accident.
- **Nick Leeson / Barings Bank (1995):** Classic escalation -- hid early trading losses, escalated to ever-larger gambles in the loss domain, destroyed a 233-year-old bank ($1.4B losses)

## Mechanisms

Four additive causes:

**1. Perceptual biases:** Confirmation bias leads decision-makers to selectively attend to information supporting the initial decision and ignore disconfirming evidence (Staw, 1980; Caldwell & O'Reilly, 1982).

**2. Judgmental biases:** [[loss-aversion]] and [[framing-effects]]. Quitting is framed as accepting a sure loss. Continuing is a gamble that might recover. Risk seeking in the loss domain (from [[prospect-theory]]) drives continued investment.

**3. Impression management:** Admitting failure is costly to reputation. Society rewards consistency and punishes "flip-flopping" (Staw & Ross, 1980). Ross and Staw (1986): people are more likely to be rewarded for escalating than for changing course.

**4. Competitive irrationality** (specific to competitive escalation): The desire to "win" replaces the goal of profit. See [[competitive-escalation]].

## Applications

- **Management:** Separate initial decision-makers from subsequent evaluators. Reward decision quality at each stage, not consistency (Staw & Ross, 1987). Hedge funds rotate portfolios so the trader who bought does not also decide when to sell.
- **Personal decisions:** Recognize when "I've invested too much to quit" is sunk-cost reasoning. Evaluate only future costs and benefits.
- **Investing:** Set stop-loss orders in advance, before emotional attachment develops
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Be aware that your counterpart may be escalating due to prior investment, making them less flexible. Also watch for your own escalation.
- **Pre-mortem exercise:** Ku (2008) -- imagining regret from escalation BEFORE entering a situation reduces subsequent escalation

## Open Questions
- Can organizations design decision architectures that systematically prevent escalation without also preventing valuable persistence?
- How does escalation interact with culture? Do collectivistic cultures (where face is more important) show more impression-management-driven escalation?
- Is there an optimal point at which persistence becomes escalation, or is it always context-dependent?
