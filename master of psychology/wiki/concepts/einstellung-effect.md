# Einstellung Effect

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Einstellung effect (also called **mechanization of thought**) is a specific and powerful form of [[set-effect]] in which repeated use of a particular solution method creates such a strong bias toward that method that the solver (1) uses it even when simpler solutions exist, and (2) fails to solve problems that require a different method. The term comes from the German word for "setting" or "attitude."

## Key Findings

- Luchins (1942) demonstrated the effect with water-jug problems. After solving 5 problems using the B - 2C - A method, 83% of participants continued using this complex method on problems easily solvable by A + C or A - C, versus less than 1% of control participants ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch8]])
- Most dramatically, 64% of set participants failed problem 8 (which could NOT be solved by B - 2C - A), versus only 5% of controls
- The simple instruction "Don't be blind" after problem 5 allowed more than 50% of participants to overcome the effect -- showing that awareness can partially counter mechanized thinking
- The Einstellung effect is also a form of **negative transfer** -- skills learned for one class of problems actively harm performance on a different class ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch9]])

## Mechanisms

Repeated successful use of a method strengthens that procedure in memory, making it increasingly automatic and available. When a new problem is encountered, the strengthened procedure is retrieved and applied before alternative approaches are considered. The effect is strongest when the practice method is complex enough that finding it feels like an achievement, reducing motivation to search for simpler alternatives.

## Applications

- In any domain where you have extensive practice, periodically ask: "Is there a simpler way to do this?" Your expertise may be blinding you to better approaches
- When teaching, vary the solution methods required across practice problems to prevent Einstellung
- The Einstellung effect suggests that "best practices" can become traps when conditions change
