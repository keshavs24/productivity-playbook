# Mastery Experience

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Enactive mastery experiences are the most influential source of [[self-efficacy]] information because they provide the most authentic evidence of whether one can muster whatever it takes to succeed (Bandura, 1997, pp.80-86). Successes build a robust belief in personal efficacy; repeated failures undermine it, particularly if failures occur before a sense of efficacy is firmly established. Unlike the other three [[sources-of-self-efficacy]], mastery experience provides direct, first-hand evidence of capability.

## Key Findings

- Enactive mastery produces stronger and more generalized efficacy beliefs than vicarious experiences, cognitive simulations, or verbal instruction alone (Bandura et al., 1977; Biran & Wilson, 1981; Feltz, Landers, & Raeder, 1979; Gist, 1989)
- A resilient sense of efficacy requires experience in overcoming obstacles through perseverant effort -- easy successes breed fragile efficacy that is easily disrupted by failure
- Performance alone does not provide sufficient information to judge capability -- appraisal is an inferential process moderated by multiple cognitive processing factors
- Skill transmission with social validation of personal efficacy produces large benefits; skill transmission alone achieves little with individuals beset by strong self-doubts (Schunk & Rice, 1987)
- Elder and Liker (1982): Women with adaptive resources during the Great Depression emerged more self-assured; those without coping resources were left with a sense of ineffectualness

**Cognitive processing factors that moderate the mastery-efficacy link:**

1. **Preexisting self-knowledge structures:** Self-schemata create interpretive biases -- experiences consistent with self-beliefs are noticed, given significance, and remembered; inconsistent experiences are minimized. Efficacy beliefs are both products and constructors of experiences. Fictitiously created efficacy beliefs resist discreditation and survive contradictory behavioral experiences (Cervone & Palmer, 1990; Lawrence, 1988). Connects to [[anchoring-and-adjustment]].

2. **Task difficulty and contextual factors:** Mastery of difficult tasks conveys new efficacy information; succeeding at easy tasks is redundant. Successes achieved with external assistance carry little efficacy value. Faulty performances under adverse conditions have weaker negative implications than those under optimal circumstances.

3. **Effort expenditure:** For young children, high effort signals gaining ability; for adults, high effort implies low ability (Nicholls & Miller, 1984). Low effort renders failures nondiagnostic. High effort that begets rising accomplishments enhances efficacy for those who view ability as acquirable but not for those who view it as inherent (Schunk & Cox, 1986). Connects to [[attribution-theory]].

4. **Selective self-monitoring:** People who selectively attend to and recall poorer performances underestimate their efficacy. Self-modeling (observing edited videotapes of oneself performing successfully) enhances both efficacy and performance (Dowrick, 1983; Schunk & Hanson, 1989a). Connects to [[availability]].

5. **Attainment trajectories:** Temporal patterns matter -- those who experience periodic failures but continue improving raise their efficacy more than those who succeed but plateau. Interpreting a plateau as approaching capacity limits reduces future expectations.

## Mechanisms

Mastery experience builds efficacy through an inferential process in which the relative contribution of ability and nonability factors to performance is weighted:

1. **Success** --> Attribution to personal capability --> Raised efficacy beliefs --> Greater effort and persistence --> More success (virtuous cycle)
2. **Failure** --> Attribution to personal incapability --> Lowered efficacy beliefs --> Reduced effort and persistence --> More failure (vicious cycle)
3. **Success despite obstacles** --> Attribution to both capability and perseverance --> Robust, resilient efficacy that withstands future setbacks (the most adaptive pattern)

The connection to [[deliberate-practice]] is direct: structured mastery experiences with graduated difficulty and feedback are simultaneously the core of deliberate practice AND the strongest builder of self-efficacy.

## Applications

- **Graduated mastery:** Structure learning environments with graduated difficulty levels that ensure initial success while progressively increasing challenge (Bandura, 1986a)
- **Break complex skills into subskills:** Development of cognitive basis of competencies is facilitated by breaking complex skills into easily mastered subskills organized hierarchically
- **Reframe obstacles as useful:** Teach that some difficulties serve a beneficial purpose in teaching that success usually requires sustained effort
- **Manage early failure:** Protect efficacy during early skill acquisition by ensuring successes predominate before a sense of efficacy is firmly established
- **Self-modeling interventions:** Have people review recordings of their successful performances (with errors edited out) to enhance efficacy (Dowrick, 1991)
