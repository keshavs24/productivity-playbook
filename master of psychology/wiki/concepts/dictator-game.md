# Dictator Game

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition
A variant of the [[ultimatum-game]] in which the proposer (the "dictator") unilaterally decides how to split a sum of money with a recipient, and the recipient has no power to reject the allocation. Because the recipient cannot punish unfairness, the dictator game isolates pure fairness preferences from strategic considerations.

## Key Findings
- **Ochs and Roth (1989):** Compared dictator and ultimatum games directly. In the ultimatum game, no proposer chose a 100:0 split. In the dictator game, 36% of proposers took everything. Yet 64% still gave the other party something -- even when they didn't have to.
- **Pay-what-you-want pricing:** Gneezy, Gneezy, Nelson, and Brown (2010) found pay-what-you-want schemes (a real-life dictator game) can be more profitable than fixed pricing when linked to charitable causes, because social obligation encourages fair payment even when selfishness is costless.
- **Third-party punishment:** Fehr and Fischbacher (2004) added an observer who could pay to punish stingy dictators. Despite it being costly ($1 cost to observer = $3 penalty to dictator), 55% of observers chose to punish dictators who gave less than half. This demonstrates [[altruistic-punishment]].
- **Silent exit option:** Dana, Cain, and Dawes (2006) gave dictators a third choice: exit silently with $9, where the recipient would never know the game existed and get nothing. One-third chose the silent exit rather than play fairly -- suggesting people want to appear fair more than they want to be fair.
- **Hidden payoff condition:** Dana, Weber, and Kuang (2007) found that when dictators had to click a box to see the consequences of their choice for the other player, 50% chose not to click, then chose the selfish option. Willful ignorance enables selfish behavior.

## Mechanisms
The dictator game reveals the tension between self-interest and internalized fairness norms:
- When accountability is present (others can see), fairness norms exert strong pressure
- When accountability is removed (silent exit, hidden consequences), many people prefer selfishness -- but they want plausible deniability about it
- This suggests fairness behavior is partly driven by self-image management, not purely by intrinsic altruism
- The willingness of third-party observers to incur costs to punish unfairness suggests fairness enforcement is a deeply embedded social norm, not just a strategic calculation

## Applications
- **Organizational transparency:** Making decision-makers' choices visible to others increases fairness behavior
- **Accountability structures:** Systems that make it easy to remain ignorant of consequences enable [[indirectly-unethical-behavior]]
- **Charitable design:** Linking payments to charitable causes leverages social obligation to increase voluntary fairness
