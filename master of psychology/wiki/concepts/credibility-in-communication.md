# Credibility in Communication

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Credibility in communication refers to your audience's perception of you -- their belief, confidence, and faith in you as a communicator. [[munter|Munter]] identifies five factors that determine credibility (based on social power theorists French, Raven, and Kotter): **rank**, **goodwill**, **expertise**, **image**, and **common ground**.

Credibility exists in two forms:
- **Initial credibility** -- the audience's perception of you *before* you communicate, based on who you are, what you represent, or how you have related to them previously
- **Acquired credibility** -- the audience's perception of you *as a result of* what you write or say

This concept is the modern managerial application of [[ethos]] from Aristotle's [[three-modes-of-persuasion]].

## Key Findings

### The Five Credibility Factors

| Factor | Based On | How to Stress Initial | How to Build Acquired |
|--------|----------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Rank** | Hierarchical power, position | Emphasize your title or rank | Associate with or cite high-ranking people |
| **Goodwill** | Personal relationship, track record, trustworthiness | Refer to relationship history or track record | Emphasize audience benefits ("what's in it for them"), offer balanced evaluation, acknowledge conflicts of interest |
| **Expertise** | Knowledge, competence | Share your expert understanding, explain how you gained expertise | Associate with or cite authoritative sources |
| **Image** | Attractiveness, audience's desire to be like you, authenticity, sincerity | Emphasize attributes the audience finds attractive | Communicate openly, connect sincerely, show appropriate emotion |
| **Common ground** | Shared values, ideas, problems, needs | Establish shared values or ideas | Acknowledge similarities, tie message to common ground |

### The Bank Account Metaphor

Initial credibility functions as a "bank account." When your initial credibility is high:
- The audience may trust you even with unpopular or extreme decisions
- Each use of this trust "draws down" the balance
- You must "deposit" more through goodwill gestures and further proof of expertise

When your initial credibility is low:
- You must rely entirely on acquired credibility
- The quality of your communication itself must earn trust
- Associate yourself with high-credibility people or cite authoritative sources

### Credibility as a Persuasion Tool

The less involved your audience is with the topic or issue, the more important your credibility becomes as a factor for persuasion. Specific credibility-based persuasion techniques:

- **Shared values/common ground** -- highly persuasive when established at the beginning, before controversial recommendations
- **Goodwill/reciprocity** -- people feel obligated to reciprocate gifts, favors, and concessions (connects to [[reciprocation]])
- **Goodwill/liking** -- people are more persuaded by people they like; meet one-to-one, find similarities, offer genuine praise (connects to [[liking-principle]])
- **Image/emotionality** -- connecting emotionally with your audience and showing genuine commitment
- **Rank/expertise by association** -- citing credible experts or getting high-status introductions
- **Rank/punishment** -- using threats and punishments. Munter explicitly warns this is inappropriate for most situations: threats produce tension, provoke counter-aggression, increase fear and dislike, work only when present, and may eliminate undesired behavior without producing desired behavior

### Relationship to Aristotle's Ethos

Munter's five factors map onto and extend Aristotle's concept of [[ethos]]. Aristotle identified three components of ethos: *practical wisdom* (phronesis), *virtue* (arete), and *goodwill* (eunoia). Munter's model:
- Retains **goodwill** as a factor
- Expands *practical wisdom* into **expertise** (knowledge/competence)
- Reframes *virtue* into **image** (authenticity/sincerity) and **common ground** (shared values)
- Adds **rank** (positional/hierarchical power) -- a factor Aristotle, writing about democratic assemblies, did not foreground in the same way

## Mechanisms

Credibility works through the heuristic processing pathway described in the [[elaboration-likelihood-model]]. When audiences are not motivated or able to process a message carefully, they rely on source credibility as a mental shortcut: "If this person is credible, their conclusion is probably correct." Even when audiences *do* process carefully, high credibility enhances the persuasiveness of strong arguments.

The bank-account mechanism reflects the psychological principle of trust: trust is built slowly through consistent positive interactions and depleted quickly through failures. This connects to the concept of [[reciprocation]] -- goodwill deposits are reciprocated with trust.

## Applications

- **Before any communication:** Assess your initial credibility with this specific audience on this specific topic. Are you high or low on each of the five factors?
- **Compensating for low credibility:** If low on rank, emphasize expertise or common ground. If low on expertise, cite authoritative sources or associate with credible people.
- **Choosing direct vs. indirect:** High credibility supports [[direct-vs-indirect-organization|direct approach]]; low credibility with sensitive messages may require indirect approach
- **Building credibility over time:** Treat every communication as a deposit or withdrawal from the credibility bank account

## Open Questions

- How do the five credibility factors weight differently across cultures? Munter notes that relationship-oriented cultures weight goodwill more heavily while task-oriented cultures weight expertise, but specific empirical data is sparse.
- How does digital communication (where visual image and rank signals are diminished) change the relative importance of the five factors?
- Is there a credibility "floor" below which no communication technique can overcome audience resistance?
