# Endowment Effect

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.85

## Definition

The endowment effect is the pattern whereby people demand significantly more to sell an item they own than they would pay to acquire the same item. Ownership itself creates value -- not because the item becomes objectively more valuable, but because giving it up is coded as a loss, and [[loss-aversion]] makes losses hurt roughly 2-2.5x as much as equivalent gains.

## Key Findings

- **Kahneman, Knetsch, & Thaler (1990) mug experiment:** Sellers (who owned the mug) required a median of **$7.12** to part with it. Buyers offered a median of **$2.87**. Choosers (given a choice between mug and money) valued it at $3.12. Buyers and choosers were similar; ownership alone inflated value by ~2.5x. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch6]])
- **Ownership-to-valuation ratios of approximately 2:1** are common across endowment experiments, consistent with the ~2:1 loss-aversion ratio from [[prospect-theory]]
- **Thaler's Michael Jordan ticket problem:** Students willing to pay $330 (Problem 11) but demanded $1,920 to sell (Problem 12) -- a 5.8:1 ratio for a unique, emotionally significant good
- **eBay data:** Fully one-third of items listed on eBay fail to sell because no buyer bids more than the seller's reserve price (Null, 2007). The endowment effect systematically inflates seller expectations.
- **Thaler (1980)** coined the term, linking it to [[prospect-theory]]: once you own something, your reference point shifts so that the current state includes the item. Selling it becomes a loss from the new reference point.
- **Lerner et al. (2004)** (from [[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch7]]): Emotions modulate the endowment effect. Disgust reduces both buying and selling prices (desire to expel). Sadness increases buying prices and decreases selling prices (desire to change circumstances). The endowment effect is not fixed -- it is sensitive to emotional state.

## Mechanisms

The endowment effect is a direct consequence of [[loss-aversion]] operating through [[reference-dependence]]:

1. **Before ownership:** The item is evaluated as a potential gain from the reference point (not having it). Value = acquisition utility.
2. **After ownership:** The reference point shifts to include the item. Selling it is now evaluated as a loss. Loss aversion amplifies the subjective cost of giving it up.
3. **Result:** The minimum selling price (WTA) exceeds the maximum buying price (WTP) for the same item by the same person, solely because of the ownership frame.

The effect extends to anything "owned" -- not just physical objects but positions, opinions, divisions of a company, and courses of action (connecting to [[escalation-of-commitment]]).

## Applications

- **Real estate:** Home sellers consistently overprice, leading to extended time on market. Understanding the endowment effect helps sellers adopt a more realistic reference point.
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Each side overvalues what they currently have and undervalues what the other side offers. Both parties perceive concessions (losses) as more costly than the other side's concessions (gains).
- **M&A:** Companies hold onto underperforming divisions because selling feels like a loss
- **Consumer behavior:** Free trial periods exploit the endowment effect -- once you "own" the subscription, canceling feels like a loss
- **Career decisions:** Overvaluing current job benefits relative to opportunities elsewhere

## CVF Collection Extensions (Ch. 8)

The landmark Ch. 8 paper ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch8-endowment-loss-aversion-status-quo]]) by Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler provides the definitive experimental treatment:

- **The endowment effect, [[loss-aversion]], and [[status-quo-bias]] are shown to be three manifestations of the same underlying principle:** people evaluate outcomes as changes from a reference point, with losses weighted more heavily than gains.
- **Trading experiments:** When mugs were allocated randomly and markets conducted, actual trade volume was consistently about half the predicted amount. Control experiments with tokens (redeemable for cash) produced expected volume -- ruling out transaction costs and confusion.
- **Knetsch (1989) mug/chocolate experiment:** ~90% of participants retained their initial endowment (mug or chocolate), far more than the 50% predicted if preferences were independent of endowment.
- **The WTA-WTP gap is a formal measure of loss aversion in riskless choice:** The disparity between willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay has implications for cost-benefit analysis, environmental valuation, and legal damages.
- **Coase theorem challenged:** Initial allocations DO affect final outcomes, even with low transaction costs, because loss aversion creates a wedge between buying and selling prices.

## Ariely Extensions (Predictably Irrational, Ch. 8)

Ariely's Duke basketball ticket study ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch8-ownership]]) provides one of the most dramatic demonstrations of the endowment effect:

- **Duke basketball ticket experiment (Ariely & Carmon):** N=100+ students. All camped out for weeks and entered a lottery. Ticket holders demanded average **~$2,400** to sell; non-holders offered average **~$170** to buy. **Ratio: ~14:1** -- far exceeding the typical ~2:1 ratio from mug experiments. Not a single transaction was possible.
- **Three quirks of ownership identified by Ariely:**
  1. **We fall in love with what we already have** -- even randomly assigned possessions (e.g., adopted children from China matched randomly to couples; all parents believed the match was perfect)
  2. **We focus on what we might lose, not what we might gain** -- sellers think about losing the experience/memories, not gaining money
  3. **We assume others share our perspective** -- sellers expect buyers to feel the same emotional attachment
- **Virtual ownership:** The endowment effect can be triggered before actual ownership:
  - Online auction bidders who are highest bidders longest develop stronger ownership feelings and bid higher (Heyman, Orhun, & Ariely experiment)
  - Trial promotions exploit virtual ownership -- once you have the "gold package," downgrading feels like a loss
  - 30-day money-back guarantees work because ownership feelings develop faster than people expect
  - Advertising creates virtual ownership through imagery ("see yourself driving that BMW")
- **The IKEA effect (Norton):** Pride of ownership is inversely proportional to ease of assembly -- more effort invested = stronger ownership feelings = higher perceived value
- **Ownership of ideas:** The endowment effect extends to opinions, ideologies, and political positions -- making us intellectually rigid
- **Ariely's personal Cambridge house story:** Extensive customizations (removed walls, added sauna) that he and his wife loved made the house harder to sell because buyers didn't share their valuation of the modifications. Their real estate agent told them to rebuild walls to sell the house.
- **Zillow housing crash data (2008):** 62% of homeowners believed their home had not decreased in value during the housing crash; 56% planned more improvements. The endowment effect literally distorts perception of market reality.

## Open Questions
- Does the endowment effect diminish with trading experience? (Some evidence says yes for professional traders)
- Does it apply equally to gains and losses of non-material things (status, relationships, beliefs)? (Ariely suggests yes -- ownership of ideas creates ideological rigidity)
- How quickly does ownership establish the new reference point? (Ariely's evidence: very quickly -- virtual ownership through auctions and trial periods is sufficient)
