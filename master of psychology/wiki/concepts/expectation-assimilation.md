# Expectation Assimilation

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Expectation assimilation is the process by which prior expectations actively reshape subjective experience to conform to those expectations. Unlike simple [[confirmation-heuristic|confirmation bias]] (which biases interpretation of ambiguous evidence), expectation assimilation changes the *actual sensory experience* itself. When we expect something to taste good, it genuinely tastes better; when we expect something to be painful, we feel more pain. Crucially, this is not mere reporting bias — expectations must be present *during* the experience to exert their effect.

## Key Findings

- **Beer experiments (Ariely, Lee, & Frederick):** When MIT pub patrons were told about vinegar in beer *before* tasting, they disliked it. When told *after* tasting, they liked it just as much as those never told. Timing of expectation-setting information is critical — expectations must be present during the experience to alter it. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]])
- **Coffee condiment experiment (Ariely, Ofek, & Bertini):** Identical coffee with odd condiments displayed in fancy glass containers vs. Styrofoam cups. Fancy containers produced higher ratings, greater willingness to pay, and stronger recommendations — the coffee ambience literally changed the taste. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]])
- **Coke vs. Pepsi fMRI study (McClure et al.):** Blind tasting activated similar hedonic brain regions for both drinks. Branded tasting of Coke activated additional DLPFC regions (associations, higher-order cognition) that enhanced the experience beyond the chemical properties of the drink. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]])
- **Joshua Bell Metro experiment (Washington Post):** World-class violinist playing as a street performer was ignored by 98% of 1,097 commuters. Context set expectations so low that genius-level performance was not perceived. Bell himself noted "the expectations would triumph over the experience." ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]])
- **Stereotypes as expectations (Shih, Pittinsky, & Ambady):** Asian-American women's math performance shifted based on which stereotype was primed (gender vs. race), demonstrating that activated expectations alter actual cognitive performance, not just self-reports. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]])
- **Priming studies (Bargh, Chen, & Burrows):** Elderly-word priming caused young participants to walk more slowly — behavioral expectations shaped actual behavior. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch10-expectations]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Top-down neural modulation:** Expectations activate higher-order brain regions (DLPFC) that project to and modulate sensory processing areas, literally changing the neural response to stimuli (McClure et al. Coke/Pepsi fMRI data)
2. **Pre-experiential framing:** Expectations set the reference point against which experience is evaluated. When the reference point is high (expensive restaurant, famous musician), ambiguous sensory signals are resolved in the direction of the expectation.
3. **Selective attention:** Expectations guide attention toward confirming features and away from disconfirming ones (related to [[confirmation-heuristic]])
4. **Temporal constraint:** The mechanism operates *during* perceptual processing, not retroactively. Post-experience knowledge cannot reshape an experience that has already been encoded — this distinguishes expectation assimilation from cognitive reappraisal.

## Applications

- **Marketing and branding:** Product descriptions, packaging, and brand associations create expectations that produce genuine subjective quality differences, even with identical products
- **Food and hospitality:** Elaborate menu descriptions, attractive plating, appropriate glassware, and upscale ambience increase actual enjoyment — not just willingness to pay
- **Medicine:** Extends directly to [[placebo-effect]] and [[placebo-pricing]] — expectations set by price, branding, and doctor confidence produce real physiological outcomes
- **Education:** Teacher expectations for students (Pygmalion effect/Rosenthal effect) can shape actual student performance
- **Conflict resolution:** Ariely suggests "blind" presentation (presenting arguments without party attribution) to allow direct experience before expectations bias evaluation

## Open Questions
- Can expectation assimilation be "unlearned"? Ariely notes that even after knowing about the bias, he still experienced enhanced pleasure when expecting a positive experience.
- What is the boundary between expectation assimilation (genuine perceptual change) and reporting bias (saying what you expect to feel)? The timing experiments suggest the boundary is real, but more neuroscience evidence is needed.
- How do conflicting expectations resolve? (e.g., expensive wine in a plastic cup — price says "good" but container says "bad")
- Alexander Pope wrote "Blessed is he who expects nothing, for he shall never be disappointed." Ariely counters that positive expectations genuinely enhance experience. Is there an optimal expectation level?
