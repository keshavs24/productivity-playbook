# Mental Model Theory

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Mental model theory (Johnson-Laird, 1983) proposes that people reason about logical arguments by constructing a **mental model** -- a concrete mental representation of a situation that satisfies the premises -- and then inspecting that model to determine whether the conclusion holds. Reasoning errors arise because people typically construct only **one** model and fail to consider alternative models that would falsify the conclusion. The theory explains a wide range of errors in syllogistic reasoning.

## Key Findings

- Johnson-Laird (1983) developed a computer simulation of the theory that reproduces many of the errors people make in categorical syllogism tasks ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch10]])
- The theory predicts that syllogisms requiring consideration of more alternative models should be harder -- this has been confirmed experimentally
- Neurological evidence: right-hemisphere damage impairs syllogistic reasoning more than left-hemisphere damage, consistent with the spatial/visual nature of mental model construction (Johnson-Laird, 1995)
- Brain imaging (Kroger, Nystrom, Cohen, & Johnson-Laird, 2008): the right frontal cortex is more active during syllogistic reasoning than the left; deductive reasoning is right-localized while probabilistic reasoning is left-localized
- The Chernobyl disaster: engineers failed to consider the possibility that the reactor was no longer intact -- a real-world failure to construct alternative mental models (Johnson-Laird, personal communication, 2003)

## Mechanisms

When presented with premises like "All squares are striped" and "Some striped objects have bold borders," a person imagines a concrete scenario (e.g., striped squares with and without bold borders, striped circles with bold borders). If the conclusion "Some squares have bold borders" is true in this imagined scenario, the person accepts it. The error occurs because an alternative scenario exists (all bold borders are on non-square objects) that satisfies the premises but falsifies the conclusion. Constructing multiple models imposes heavy demands on working memory, so people typically stop after the first plausible model.

## Applications

- **Decision making:** Failure to consider alternative scenarios is a pervasive real-world problem -- from engineering disasters (Chernobyl) to medical diagnosis. Deliberately constructing counter-examples is a debiasing strategy.
- **Training reasoning:** Teaching people to systematically generate alternative models (counter-examples) may be more effective than teaching formal logic rules
- **Collaborative reasoning:** Okada and Simon (1997) found pairs outperform individuals because partners prompt consideration of alternative models
