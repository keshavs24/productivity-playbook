# Testable Credentials

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Testable credentials are claims that allow the audience to verify the truth for themselves, rather than relying on the authority of the speaker or the weight of statistics. The communicator outsources credibility to the audience by challenging them to "see for yourself" or "try before you buy." This is the most audience-empowering form of credibility because it transforms the listener from a passive recipient of claims into an active evaluator of evidence. The concept was introduced by [[heath-brothers]] in [[made-to-stick]].

## Key Findings

- Wendy's "Where's the Beef?" campaign (1984) did not use external authority (no celebrity endorser) or internal statistics ("11% more beef"). Instead, it implicitly challenged customers to compare burger sizes themselves. Wendy's made a falsifiable claim -- any customer with a ruler could verify it. The number of customers who believed Wendy's burgers were larger increased by 47% within two months
- Ronald Reagan's 1980 debate question -- "Are you better off now than you were four years ago?" -- deferred credibility to the audience's own lived experience rather than citing inflation rates or unemployment statistics
- The Positive Coaching Alliance's "Emotional Tank" exercise asked coaches to try draining and filling someone's emotional tank. The asymmetry (easy to criticize, hard to support) was experienced firsthand, making the lesson stick
- Barry Marshall drinking H. pylori bacteria to prove ulcers are bacterial was an extreme form of testable credential -- "See for yourself; if you drink this gunk, you'll get an ulcer"
- The NBA rookie orientation used women who revealed themselves as HIV-positive the morning after flirting with rookies, creating an experiential testable credential about AIDS risk
- **Danger:** Testable credentials can backfire when the "see for yourself" step is valid but the conclusion is false. The Snapple/KKK rumor exploited this: "Look at the circled K on the bottle" was verifiable, but the conclusion (KKK ownership) was entirely invalid

## Mechanisms

1. **Audience empowerment:** When people verify something themselves, they own the conclusion. Self-generated conclusions are more resistant to counter-persuasion than conclusions received from others
2. **Experiential learning:** Direct experience is more vivid, memorable, and persuasive than secondhand information. The "Emotional Tank" exercise converts an abstract principle into a felt experience
3. **Commitment and consistency:** Once someone has verified a claim and formed their own conclusion, [[commitment-and-consistency]] makes them more likely to act on it
4. **Bypassing skepticism:** Modern audiences are trained to be skeptical of sources and their motives. Testable credentials sidestep source skepticism entirely by saying "don't trust me -- trust your own experience"

## Applications

- **Marketing and advertising:** Frame claims as verifiable challenges rather than assertions. "Compare our product with theirs" is more credible than "our product is 23% better"
- **Teaching:** Ask students to experience the phenomenon before explaining it. The [[availability]] bias is better taught by asking "which kills more: homicide or suicide?" and letting students discover their wrong intuitions than by defining the bias abstractly
- **Persuasion and debate:** Reagan's technique of asking a question the audience can answer from their own experience ("Are you better off?") is more powerful than presenting external evidence
- **Sales:** Offer free trials, demos, and taste tests -- these are all forms of testable credentials
- **Training:** The Emotional Tank exercise and the NBA rookie orientation both show that experiential testable credentials are more powerful than lectures or statistics

## Open Questions

- How do testable credentials interact with [[confirmation-bias]] -- do people tend to verify claims in ways that confirm what they already believe?
- What is the ethical boundary between a legitimate testable credential and a misleading one (as in the Snapple/K case)?
- In digital environments, how can testable credentials be adapted when audiences cannot physically "try before they buy"?
