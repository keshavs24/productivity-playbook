# Commander's Intent

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Commander's Intent (CI) is a concept developed by the U.S. Army in the 1980s to solve a fundamental communication problem: detailed battle plans become useless on contact with the enemy. CI is a crisp, plain-talk statement that appears at the top of every order, specifying the plan's goal and the desired end-state of an operation. It communicates the *why* and the *what* without specifying the *how*, enabling soldiers at all levels to improvise toward the goal when plans inevitably break down.

The [[heath-brothers]] use Commander's Intent as the central metaphor for the "Simple" principle in the [[success-framework]]: finding and communicating the essential core of any message.

## Key Findings

- **Plans are useful because planning is useful.** Colonel Tom Kolditz (West Point behavioral sciences): "The planning process forces people to think through the right issues. But as for the plans themselves, they just don't work on the battlefield."
- **CI operates at multiple levels of abstraction.** At high levels: "Break the will of the enemy in the Southeast region." At tactical levels: "My intent is to have Third Battalion on Hill 4305, cleared of enemy, with only ineffective remnants remaining, so we can protect the flank of Third Brigade as they pass through the lines."
- **CI never overspecifies.** It avoids enough detail that it risks being rendered obsolete by unpredictable events. "You can lose the ability to execute the original plan, but you never lose the responsibility of executing the intent."
- **CI enables improvisation.** When people know the desired destination, they can invent their own route. The artillery knows to fire smoke in the breech area; the mechanics know to prioritize bridge repair — all from a single intent statement.
- **The Combat Maneuver Training Center** recommends officers arrive at CI by answering two questions: (1) "If we do nothing else during tomorrow's mission, we must ___." (2) "The single, most important thing that we must do tomorrow is ___."

## Mechanisms

CI works through several cognitive mechanisms:

1. **Forced prioritization:** There can be only one core, just as there can be only one lead in a news story. "If you say three things, you don't say anything." This fights decision paralysis by providing a clear priority.
2. **Decision filter:** The core message serves as a decision filter for novel situations. Herb Kelleher's "THE low-fare airline" instantly resolves the chicken-salad dilemma without Kelleher being present.
3. **Scalable autonomy:** CI aligns behavior without requiring play-by-play instructions, allowing organizations to function at scale. Hoover Adams's "Names, names, and names" guided hundreds of people through thousands of decisions over fifty-five years.

## Applications

- **Organizational strategy:** Southwest Airlines' "We are THE low-fare airline" functioned as a Commander's Intent for thirty years, guiding thousands of employees' decisions
- **Political campaigns:** James Carville's "It's the economy, stupid" on the Clinton campaign whiteboard — a CI that kept the campaign focused amid complexity and media pressure
- **Local journalism:** Hoover Adams's "Names, names, and names" drove the *Daily Record* to 112% community penetration — the highest in the country
- **Product design:** Jeff Hawkins's wooden Palm Pilot block fought feature creep by making the CI tangible — "four things, done well"
- **Teaching:** Any lesson plan benefits from having a CI — the single most important thing students must understand

## Open Questions

- How does CI interact with organizational culture? Southwest Airlines had both a CI and a strong culture ("Have fun at work"). Does the CI need a supporting culture to function, or can it create one?
- At what level of abstraction should a CI operate? Too concrete and it becomes a plan (brittle); too abstract and it becomes a mission statement (useless). What determines the right level?
