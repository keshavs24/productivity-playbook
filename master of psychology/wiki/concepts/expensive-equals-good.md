# Expensive = Good Heuristic

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The expensive = good heuristic is a mental shortcut in which people use price as an automatic indicator of quality. When uncertain about the true quality of an item, consumers default to the assumption that higher price signals higher value. The flip side also holds: "inexpensive = bad" — the word "cheap" means both low-priced and inferior.

## Key Findings

- **Turquoise jewelry** (Cialdini): Jewelry that wouldn't sell at fair prices was accidentally marked at 2x the original — and sold out immediately. Quality-hungry tourists used price as their sole quality cue.
- **Chivas Regal** (Aaker, 1991): A struggling whiskey brand raised its price far above competitors with no product change. Sales skyrocketed.
- **Wine brain-scan study** (Plassmann et al., 2008): Subjects tasting the *same wine* rated it as more pleasurable when told it cost $45 vs. $5. Critically, their brain pleasure centers were *more activated* — the perception wasn't just reported, it was neurologically real.
- **Uncertainty as moderator** (Cronley et al., 2005): People who are unsure of an item's quality rely heavily on this stereotype. When quality is assessable through other means, the heuristic weakens.

## Mechanisms

The heuristic is a form of [[representativeness]] — price "represents" quality because in most markets, price and quality *do* correlate (Rao & Monroe, 1989). The shortcut works well enough most of the time. It becomes a vulnerability when:
1. The correlation doesn't hold (the jewelry was already fairly priced)
2. The price is deliberately manipulated (Chivas Regal, setup pricing)
3. The buyer lacks domain knowledge to assess quality independently

It operates as a [[click-whirr]] response: the trigger feature (high price) activates an automatic behavioral sequence (assume quality, purchase).

## Applications

- **Premium pricing strategy**: Price high to signal quality, especially when quality is hard to evaluate
- **Decoy pricing**: Use a high-priced option to make a mid-priced option seem reasonable
- **The jeweler's double play**: Mark up slow-moving items. If they sell at the higher price, profit. If they don't, mark them "Reduced" to the original price — and bargain-hunters snap them up, believing they're getting a deal on premium goods.
