# Ultimatum Game

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition
An experimental economics game in which one player (the proposer) divides a sum of money between themselves and a second player (the responder), who can either accept or reject the split. If the responder rejects, both players receive nothing. First studied systematically by Guth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze (1982).

The ultimatum game is a cornerstone of behavioral economics because it demonstrates that human decision-making systematically departs from pure economic rationality -- people sacrifice personal gain to punish perceived unfairness.

## Key Findings
- **Proposer behavior:** Average demand is less than 70% of the pie; the most frequent offer is a 50/50 split. Proposers anticipate fairness expectations.
- **Responder behavior:** Offers below 20% are routinely rejected, even when the total pie equals several months' wages (Cameron, 1999; Hoffman, McCabe, & Smith, 1996; Straub & Murnighan, 1995)
- **Disadvantageous counteroffers:** Ochs and Roth (1989) found that 81% of rejected offers in multi-round ultimatum games were followed by counteroffers demanding LESS than the rejected amount -- suggesting punishment motivation, not strategic bargaining
- **Cross-cultural universality:** Henrich et al. (2001) tested 15 societies worldwide and found fairness mattered in all of them, though specific norms varied by cultural context
- **Cross-species parallels:** Brosnan and de Waal (2003) found capuchin monkeys refused to work or eat when given cucumbers while their neighbors received tastier grapes for the same task
- **Neuroimaging (Sanfey et al., 2003):** Unfair offers activated the anterior insula (negative emotions) and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (rational calculation). Emotional responses were stronger for offers from humans than from computers. When the insula "won," participants rejected; when the prefrontal cortex "won," they accepted.
- **Stake size robustness:** Multiple studies show the pattern holds even when rejection means losing substantial real money

## Mechanisms
Two competing forces drive responder behavior:
1. **Emotional rejection of unfairness** -- The anterior insula generates a negative emotional response to perceived unfairness
2. **Rational self-interest** -- The prefrontal cortex calculates that any positive amount is better than nothing

When the emotional response dominates (which it does for offers perceived as exploitative), people reject economically profitable deals. This is not irrational noise -- it is a systematic, predictable pattern tied to fairness norms.

Proposer behavior reflects anticipation of this pattern: proposers offer fair splits not out of pure altruism but because they correctly predict that unfair offers will be rejected.

## Applications
- **Negotiation:** Understanding that counterparts will reject deals they perceive as unfair, even at personal cost, should inform offer strategy
- **Pricing and management:** Businesses that act in economically rational but "unfair" ways (price gouging, pay inequity) face systematic punishment from customers, employees, and partners
- **Everyday life:** Every posted price is an ultimatum ("take it or leave it") -- fairness norms pervade commercial transactions
- **Pay-what-you-want pricing:** Gneezy, Gneezy, Nelson, and Brown (2010) found this can be more profitable than fixed pricing when linked to charitable giving, because social obligation encourages fair payment
