# 7-38-55 Rule

**Phase(s):** 5 — Negotiation
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The 7-38-55 Rule (also known as Mehrabian's Rule) states that only **7% of a message** is based on the words used, while **38% comes from tone of voice** and **55% from body language and facial expressions**. Originally developed by UCLA psychology professor Albert Mehrabian in two famous studies on liking and attitude communication, the rule is applied by [[chris-voss]] as a critical negotiation tool for detecting incongruence — mismatches between what people say and what they actually mean.

## Key Findings

- **Original context**: Mehrabian's studies focused on situations where someone is forming an attitude about another person — specifically when verbal and nonverbal messages conflict. The rule is most applicable in these ambiguous, attitude-laden situations ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])
- **Negotiation application**: Body language and tone of voice — not words — are a negotiator's most powerful assessment tools. Voss often flies great distances to meet face-to-face, even when most of the content could be communicated by phone ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])
- **Incongruence detection**: When tone of voice or body language does not align with the literal meaning of words, the speaker is quite possibly lying or at least unconvinced. Use labels to discover the source of incongruence ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])
- **Black Swan implications**: Email doesn't allow for tone-of-voice effects or reading nonverbal cues — this is why Black Swans are incredibly hard to uncover remotely. You miss 93% of the message without face-to-face interaction ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch10-black-swan]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Dual-channel processing**: Humans process verbal content consciously but read tone and body language more automatically and instinctively. When channels conflict, the nonverbal signal typically carries the true message.
2. **Leakage**: Even skilled deceivers "leak" their true feelings through micro-expressions, voice pitch changes, and body posture shifts. These leaks are detectable with practice.
3. **Incongruence as diagnostic**: Mismatches between word content and paraverbal/nonverbal signals indicate areas where the counterpart is uncertain, lying, or under pressure — exactly where Black Swans may be hiding.

## Practical Application in Negotiation

When you detect incongruence:

1. **Label it**: "I heard you say 'Yes,' but it seemed like there was hesitation in your voice."
2. **Invite correction**: "No, this is important, let's make sure we get this right."
3. **Result**: The counterpart will either clarify their genuine concern or strengthen their commitment — either way, you've improved the deal's chances of successful implementation.

This technique pairs directly with the [[rule-of-three]] — using the 7-38-55 awareness across three confirmations makes it nearly impossible for a counterpart to sustain a counterfeit "Yes."

## Important Caveats

- The original research specifically focused on situations involving feelings and attitudes, not all communication. Mehrabian himself has noted that the ratios should not be generalized to all communication contexts.
- The rule nonetheless offers negotiators a useful ratio for prioritizing what to pay attention to — tone and body language over mere words.
- Cultural variation in nonverbal expression may shift the specific percentages, but the principle that nonverbal channels dominate remains broadly supported.
