# Career Self-Efficacy

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Career self-efficacy refers to beliefs about one's capabilities for career-related activities including career choice and development, occupational decision making, job search behaviors, mastery of occupational roles, and adaptation to occupational transitions (Bandura, 1997, Ch.10). It encompasses multiple facets: efficacy for fulfilling educational requirements and job functions, efficacy for various decisional subskills (gathering occupational information, self-appraisal, planning), and efficacy for managing the interpersonal and self-regulatory demands of occupational life.

## Key Findings

- The higher the perceived efficacy to fulfill educational requirements and job functions, the wider the range of career options people seriously consider and the greater their interest (Betz & Hackett, 1981; Lent et al., 1986; Matsui, Ikeda, & Ohnishi, 1989) ([[summary-3-11-se-ch10-organizational-functioning]])
- People rapidly eliminate entire classes of vocations from consideration on the basis of perceived efficacy, whatever benefits they might hold
- Perceived mathematical efficacy contributes more significantly to educational and career choices making use of quantitative skills than does amount of math preparation, level of ability, past achievement, or anxiety over mathematical activities (Hackett & Betz, 1989)
- Self-efficacy predicts occupational preferences better than expectancy-value theory (Wheeler, 1983): when analyzed together, only efficacy beliefs emerged as predictor of where people chose to pursue occupational life
- Gender differences: male college students judge themselves equally efficacious for both male- and female-dominated occupations; female students judge themselves more efficacious for female occupations but weaker for male-dominated ones, despite no differences in actual ability (Betz & Hackett, 1981)
- Gender effects on vocational interests are entirely mediated through math achievement and efficacy beliefs -- when both controlled, gender makes no independent contribution (Lapan, Boggs, & Morrill, 1989)
- Only perceived decision-making efficacy was a significant predictor of occupational indecision among multiple predictors including locus of control (Taylor & Popma, 1990)
- Perceived efficacy predicted successful reemployment following job loss beyond search behavior (Kanfer & Hulin, 1985; Clifford, 1988)
- Computer-assisted career guidance markedly increases perceived efficacy and reduces indecisiveness (Fukuyama et al., 1988)

## Mechanisms

Career self-efficacy operates through the four [[self-efficacy-mediating-processes]]:

1. **Cognitive:** Efficacy beliefs determine which occupations people regard as viable and what decisional strategies they employ
2. **Motivational:** Efficacy beliefs influence career goal level, perseverance through setbacks, and effort invested in preparatory education
3. **Affective:** Low career efficacy generates anxiety about occupational decisions and performance; high efficacy reduces occupational stress
4. **Selection:** By influencing which career paths people pursue, efficacy beliefs shape developmental trajectories that are difficult to reverse

Social cognitive theory posits a reciprocal but asymmetric relationship between perceived efficacy and occupational interests, with efficacy beliefs playing the stronger determinant role. Perceived efficacy creates interests through engrossment in activities and the self-satisfactions derived from fulfilling personal challenges.

## Applications

- **Career counseling:** Assess and build self-efficacy for a wider range of career options. Computer-assisted guidance programs can increase perceived efficacy and reduce indecisiveness
- **Combating gender stereotyping:** Provide female role models in nontraditional fields; structure mastery experiences that demonstrate capability regardless of gender (Schunk & Lilly, 1984)
- **Occupational transitions:** Use guided mastery programs to build efficacy for new skill requirements during career changes or technological transitions
- **Job search:** Train job-search skills through modeling strategies for networking, interviewing, and self-presentation (Tracy & Adams, 1984)
- **Entrepreneurship:** Build entrepreneurial efficacy through vision-setting, challenging growth goals, and mastery of technical and innovative skills
