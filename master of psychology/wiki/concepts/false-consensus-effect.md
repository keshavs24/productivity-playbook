# False Consensus Effect

**Phase(s):** 1, 2, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The false consensus effect (also called the egocentric attribution bias) is the tendency to **see one's own behavioral choices and judgments as relatively common and appropriate** while viewing alternative responses as uncommon, deviant, and more revealing of the respondent's stable dispositions. People systematically overestimate the proportion of others who share their views and behaviors.

The term *relative* is critical: a person who walks tightropes between skyscrapers recognizes few share this choice, but the false consensus bias leads them to see their choice as less deviant than non-tightrope-walkers would estimate.

## Key Findings

- **Ross, Greene, & House (1977) -- hypothetical questionnaire:** Subjects who chose a particular alternative in conflict situations estimated it was more common than did subjects who chose the opposite. They also made more confident and extreme trait inferences about the person who chose differently from themselves. ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch9-attribution-shortcomings]])
- **Ross, Greene, & House (1977) -- "Eat at Joe's" sandwich-board study:** Subjects asked to walk around campus wearing a sign. Compliant subjects estimated 62% of peers would agree; refusers estimated only 33% would agree. Both groups made stronger dispositional inferences about the opposite-choice peer.
- The effect appears in both hypothetical questionnaire scenarios and real behavioral situations with consequential choices.

## Mechanisms

1. **Selective exposure:** People associate with those who share their background, experiences, interests, values, and outlook. This biased sample genuinely does respond as we would, inflating our estimates of consensus.
2. **[[availability]]:** The specific behaviors we have chosen or would choose are more readily retrievable from memory, more easily visualized, and more easily imagined (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973). Our own behavioral options are more cognitively "available."
3. **Ambiguity resolution:** Situational features are often ambiguous. People resolve ambiguity in accord with their own tendencies, then assume others resolve it the same way. Subjects who feared ridicule from wearing the sandwich-board sign assumed similar peers would also focus on ridicule; subjects who focused on helping the experimenter assumed peers would share that focus.

## Applications

- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** Negotiators overestimate the extent to which the other side shares their framing of the issues. This creates false confidence and impedes perspective-taking.
- **Persuasion (Phase 6):** Communicators assume their audience shares their priors and values, leading to poorly calibrated messages. Understanding false consensus enables better audience analysis.
- **Leadership:** Leaders may assume their team shares their priorities and concerns, failing to recognize genuine disagreement or alternative perspectives.
- **Marketing:** Product developers assume broader market appeal than warranted because they over-project their own preferences.
- **Political polarization:** Each faction overestimates its position's popularity, contributing to mutual surprise at election outcomes.
