# Belief Perseverance

**Phase(s):** 1, 6
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

Belief perseverance is the tendency to **stick with an initial judgment even when subsequently learned information contradicts it** or the evidence for it has been retracted. Once we make up our minds, we're inclined to keep them made up.

## Key Findings

- Across decades of studies, participants maintain original impressions even after the basis for those impressions is revealed as erroneous (Anderson, 1995; Ross, Lepper, & Hubbard, 1975) ([[summary-1-03-social-psychology-ch4]])
- Jurors struggle to disregard evidence ruled inadmissible
- Scientists are slow to discount conclusions from fabricated data
- Voters remain influenced by debunked political misinformation
- Connected to cognitive dissonance: inconsistent thoughts are uncomfortable, so we resist information that challenges existing beliefs

### Ross & Anderson (Ch. 9) — Experimental Detail ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch9-attribution-shortcomings]])

**Paradigm 1: Biased assimilation and polarization (Lord, Lepper, & Ross, 1979):**
- Subjects who strongly supported or opposed capital punishment were given the *same* two empirical studies -- one pro-deterrence, one anti-deterrence.
- Both groups rated the study supporting their position as "more convincing" and "better conducted."
- Net effect: the two groups became *more* polarized after reading the same evidence -- contrary to any normative expectation.
- Upon reading a supporting result, beliefs became considerably more extreme; these changes were maintained through procedural details. Upon reading an opposing result, beliefs weakened only slightly, and subjects reverted upon reading details.

**Paradigm 2: Postdebriefing perseverance of personal impressions (Ross, Lepper, & Hubbard, 1975):**
- Subjects received false feedback on a suicide-note discrimination task, then were totally debriefed (told feedback was predetermined, unrelated to performance).
- Despite total discrediting, subjects in the "success" condition still estimated higher past performance (18.33 vs. 12.83), predicted better future performance (18.33 vs. 14.25), and rated their ability higher (5.00 vs. 3.83) than "failure" subjects. All differences significant (p < .05 to p < .001).
- Observers who witnessed the false feedback also showed perseverance in their impressions after debriefing.

**Paradigm 3: Postdebriefing perseverance of broader theories (Anderson, Lepper, & Ross, 1980):**
- Subjects formed theories about the relationship between firefighters' professional performance and their risk-taking preference scores, based on *only one pair* of specific cases.
- These theories survived revelation that the cases were fictitious and that different subjects had received opposite pairings.
- **Over 50% of the initial effect of the "case history" information remained after debriefing.**
- When subjects were explicitly required to formulate causal explanations before debriefing, the perseverance effect was *greatly enhanced* -- subjects who explained were only trivially less certain than subjects who received no debriefing.

**Additional replications:**
- Jennings, Lepper, & Ross (1980): Impressions of interpersonal persuasion ability persisted after learning outcomes were inauthentic.
- Lepper, Ross, & Lau (1979): Erroneous impressions of "logical problem solving abilities" persevered *two months later*, affecting academic course choices.

## Mechanisms

Once a belief forms, the mind generates supporting causal explanations and connections. Even if the original evidence is retracted, these generated explanations persist — the belief has become "self-supporting" through elaboration. This connects to [[representativeness]] (the belief "fits" the existing schema) and [[availability]] (supporting evidence is easier to recall than contradicting evidence).

Ross and Anderson identify three specific mechanisms ([[summary-2-06-hb-ch9-attribution-shortcomings]]):

1. **Biased search, recollection, and assimilation:** Confirmatory evidence is taken at face value; disconfirmatory evidence receives critical scrutiny (Lord et al., 1979). People do not "go back and reassess all of the evidence" when a belief's basis is undermined.
2. **Formation of causal explanations:** People account *for* their beliefs by generating causal stories. A subject told firefighting correlates with risk-taking will easily construct a logical rationale -- and this explanation persists after the data are discredited. Anderson et al. (1980) showed that requiring causal explanation *before* debriefing dramatically increased perseverance.
3. **Behavioral confirmation / self-fulfilling prophecies:** Actions based on beliefs create new data confirming them (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968 "Pygmalion" studies; Snyder & Swann, 1978).

## Applications

- **Persuasion (Phase 6):** First impressions and initial claims have outsized persistence. Getting your frame established first creates a belief that resists later counter-evidence.
- **Negotiation:** Initial perceptions of the counterpart (trustworthy/untrustworthy, tough/flexible) persist even when contradicted by later behavior.
- **Marketing:** First brand impressions are extremely durable. A strong positive launch impression persists through later missteps; a poor first impression is very hard to overcome.
