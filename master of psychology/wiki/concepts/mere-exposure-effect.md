# Mere Exposure Effect

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

The mere exposure effect is the tendency for people to develop a preference for things simply because they are familiar with them. Repeated contact with a person, object, or stimulus — even when the contact occurs below conscious awareness — breeds liking. The effect is a key factor underlying the [[liking-principle]].

However, the effect has an important boundary condition: contact must occur under positive or neutral circumstances. Contact under conditions of frustration, conflict, or competition can produce the opposite — *decreased* liking.

## Key Findings

- **General finding:** We like things that are familiar to us (Monahan, Murphy, & Zajonc, 2000)
- **Mirror image preference — Mita, Dermer, & Knight (1977):** Women preferred their mirror image (the face they see daily); their friends preferred the true photograph (the face they see). Both were responding to the more familiar version
- **Elections:** Voters often choose candidates merely because the name seems familiar (Grush, 1980; Grush, McKeough, & Ahlering, 1978). An Ohio candidate won the attorney-general race after changing his name to Brown — a traditional Ohio political name
- **Subliminal exposure — Bornstein, Leone, & Galley (1987):** Faces flashed too quickly for conscious recall still produced liking — the more frequent the exposure, the more the subjects liked that person and were persuaded by their opinions
- **Online advertising — Fang (2007):** Banner ads shown more frequently increased liking for the product even when participants were unaware of seeing the ads
- **School desegregation — Stephan (1978):** Desegregation is more likely to *increase* prejudice than decrease it, because contact occurs under competitive, frustrating conditions. The "contact approach" to racial harmony fails when conditions are hostile (Richeson & Shelton, 2007; Swap, 1977; Zajonc, Markus, & Wilson, 1974)
- **Sherif's Robbers Cave (Sherif et al., 1961):** Pleasant joint activities (picnics, movies) between hostile groups produced food fights and shoving matches — mere contact failed entirely. Only structured cooperation toward shared goals reduced hostility
- **Aronson's jigsaw classroom (Aronson et al., 1978):** Cooperative learning in desegregated schools produced more friendship and less prejudice; traditional competitive contact did not

## Mechanisms

1. **Processing fluency:** Familiar stimuli are processed more easily; this fluency is experienced as a positive feeling, which is then attributed to the stimulus itself
2. **Safety signal:** Familiarity implies that a stimulus has been encountered before without harm, creating a feeling of safety
3. **Unconscious operation:** The effect works even for stimuli presented below the threshold of conscious awareness (Bornstein et al., 1987), suggesting it does not require deliberate processing

**Critical boundary condition:** The contact must not carry distasteful experiences. Continued exposure under conditions of frustration, conflict, or competition leads to *less* liking. This explains why school desegregation in competitive classrooms increases rather than decreases prejudice.

## Applications

- **Advertising:** Repeated ad exposure increases product liking, even at subliminal levels
- **Political campaigns:** Name recognition through repeated exposure is a genuine electoral advantage
- **Cooperative learning:** The [[cooperation-and-liking]] approach (Aronson's jigsaw classroom) leverages familiarity under positive conditions to produce cross-group liking
- **Sales:** Repeated contacts with a salesperson, provided they are pleasant, increase liking and compliance
- **Online presence:** Frequency of appearance in digital feeds may leverage the mere exposure effect

## Open Questions

- At what point does repeated exposure become overexposure, producing boredom or irritation rather than liking?
- How does the mere exposure effect interact with the novelty-seeking drive? Does personality moderate the strength of the effect?
- In the age of algorithmic content curation, does the mere exposure effect contribute to filter bubbles — we like what we see often, and algorithms show us more of what we already like?
