# Contingent Contracts

**Phase(s):** 2, 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Contingent contracts are negotiated agreements in which the terms depend on future events or outcomes that are currently uncertain. Rather than arguing about whose prediction is correct, parties "bet" on the outcome, with each side accepting terms favorable to their prediction. Contingent contracts exploit differences in beliefs about the future to create value, bypass impasse, diagnose bluffs, and establish performance incentives.

## Key Findings

- **Bazerman and Gillespie (1999):** A TV production company (predicting a 9-share rating) and a TV station (predicting a 7-share) reached impasse over price for syndication rights, each rating point worth $1M. A contingent contract tying the price to actual ratings would have resolved the impasse and created value. Instead, the show was not aired in that market. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch11]])
- **Malhotra and Bazerman (2007):** A client skeptical of her lawyer's ability signs a contingent fee arrangement -- large payment if the client wins, nothing if she loses. The contingent contract aligns incentives and allows the deal despite differing confidence levels.
- **Common real-world examples:** Royalties (publisher bets on book's success), sales commissions (employer bets on salesperson's performance), performance bonuses, earn-outs in M&A.

## Mechanisms

Contingent contracts create value in four ways:

1. **Building on differences in predictions:** Each party structures the deal based on their own (possibly overconfident) beliefs about the future. Both parties are satisfied ex ante because each expects the contingency to resolve in their favor.

2. **Short-circuiting debates:** Rather than arguing about uncertain forecasts, parties agree to let reality decide. This is especially useful when [[overconfidence-in-calibration]] makes each side certain they are right.

3. **Diagnosing bluffs:** When someone makes a claim ("This product will sell 100,000 units"), proposing a bet based on that claim tests sincerity. If sincere, they accept the bet. If bluffing, they typically back down -- the rejection reveals the bluff.

4. **Establishing incentives:** Tying payment to performance aligns the parties' interests going forward and motivates effort.

## Applications

- **M&A negotiations:** Earn-out provisions tie part of the acquisition price to the acquired company's future performance
- **Employment negotiations:** Contingent compensation (bonuses, equity vesting based on milestones) when parties disagree about the employee's future contributions
- **Vendor agreements:** Performance-based pricing when vendor and client disagree about service quality or delivery timelines
- **Bluff detection:** In any negotiation where the other party makes strong claims about the future, propose betting on those claims

## Negotiation Genius Extensions

[[malhotra|Malhotra]] and [[bazerman|Bazerman]] in [[negotiation-genius|Negotiation Genius]] provide extensive practical guidance on contingent contracts:

- **The Moms.com contingency clause:** When Hollyville predicted ratings of 5-6 and WCHI predicted 3-4, a contingency clause (if ratings > 5, WCHI pays $1M surcharge; if ratings < 4, WCHI refunds $1M) created $500K in *expected* value for both parties — each believed they had a 70% chance of winning the bet. This took total value from $5.1M to $6.1M. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch2-creating-value]])
- **Dennis Rodman contract (1997):** The Chicago Bulls negotiated an unprecedented contingency-laden contract: $4.5M guaranteed, up to $10.5M based on games played, playoff participation, and rebounding title. Rodman earned nearly the maximum. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch2-creating-value]])
- **Three caveats for contingency contracts:** (1) Dangerous if the other party has better information than you, (2) useful only when uncertainty can be resolved objectively, (3) must be *incentive compatible* — the clause should not create perverse incentives for the other party to sabotage outcomes (e.g., a TV station could air the show at bad times to tank ratings and win the bet). ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch2-creating-value]])
- **Contingent contracts as lie detectors (Ch. 1):** In the Hamilton Real Estate case, proposing a contingency clause tied to commercial development would either protect the seller from deception or expose the buyer's bluff through their refusal to accept the clause. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch1-claiming-value]])

## Open Questions

- What happens when both parties are overconfident in the same direction about a future event?
- How should contingent contracts be structured to minimize disputes about measurement and verification of outcomes?
