# Autonomy-Supportive Management

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Autonomy-supportive management is the application of [[autonomy-support]] principles to the workplace. It is a specific managerial orientation and **trainable skill set** in which managers relate to employees by taking their perspectives, providing informational feedback, supporting initiative and choice, offering meaningful rationale, minimizing controlling language, and acknowledging employees' feelings and opinions.

It contrasts with **controlling management**, which relies on contingent rewards and punishments, surveillance, evaluation pressure, imposed goals, and controlling language ("you should," "you must") to motivate performance.

Critically, autonomy-supportive management is **not laissez-faire or permissive**. It involves providing clear structure, expectations, and feedback -- but delivering them in ways that support rather than undermine employees' sense of volition and self-direction.

## Key Findings

### Intervention Evidence: Managers Can Be Trained

- **Deci, Connell, & Ryan (1989) -- The landmark Fortune 500 study:** Organization-development intervention training managers in three core concepts: (1) understanding others' perspectives, (2) providing informational feedback, (3) supporting subordinates' initiative, choice, and participation. Over 13 months: trained managers became more autonomy-supportive; subordinates showed positive changes in perceptions, satisfaction, and trust -- including trust in top management many levels removed. Company attitude surveys confirmed positive changes in intervention branches vs. control branches
- **Forest, Gilbert, Beaulieu, Le Brock, & Gagne (2014):** Economic utility analysis of the Deci et al. (1989) intervention calculated mental health savings. **ROI exceeded 3:1** -- the intervention improved well-being and saved the organization money
- **Hardre & Reeve (2009):** Randomly assigned Fortune 500 managers to training vs. control. Five weeks later: trained managers significantly more autonomy-supportive; five weeks after that, their employees were significantly more autonomous and engaged than employees of untrained managers

### Effects on Employee Outcomes

- **Otis & Pelletier (2005):** Police officers with autonomy-supportive supervisors had more autonomous motivation, fewer daily hassles, lower physical symptoms, stronger retention intentions
- **Levesque, Blais, & Hess (2004):** Gambian teachers with need-supportive supervisors were more autonomously motivated with greater job and life satisfaction
- **Baard, Deci, & Ryan (2004):** In banking, perceived autonomy support from managers predicted basic need satisfaction, which predicted work performance and well-being
- **Olafsen, Halvari, Forest, & Deci (2015):** Autonomy support was strongly related to need satisfaction, intrinsic motivation, and procedural justice; pay level was not
- **Trepanier, Fernet, & Austin (2013b):** The opposite of autonomy support -- psychological harassment by superiors -- predicted less autonomous motivation, more controlled motivation, lower wellness, and higher turnover intentions

### Effects on Organizational Change

- **Gagne, Koestner, & Zuckerman (2000):** Autonomy-supportive introduction of change (rationale + choice + acknowledgment) reduces resistance
- **Gagne et al. (2000):** During tumultuous organizational change (including downsizing), autonomy-supportive managers' subordinates were more accepting of change
- **Lynch, Plant, & Ryan (2005):** Psychiatric hospital staff who received autonomy-supportive change management internalized the value of the new program and became more autonomy-supportive toward patients, who in turn became more autonomously motivated

## Mechanisms

### The Seven Training Aims (Hardre & Reeve, 2009)

1. **Perspective-taking:** Take employees' perspectives; understand their feelings and beliefs without becoming controlling
2. **Choice provision:** Provide greater choice and inputs to decision making
3. **De-emphasis of rewards/sanctions:** Understand that turning to rewards and sanctions to motivate employees is often maladaptive
4. **Informational positive feedback:** Learn to give more informational positive feedback and support competence experiences
5. **Constructive negative feedback:** Conceptualize "negative feedback" as a process of supporting employees to find meaningful solutions
6. **Exploration and self-initiation:** Encourage exploration and self-initiation
7. **Communication awareness:** Understand how communications and language affect motivation (providing rationale; minimizing controlling language)

### Managerial Functions Done Autonomy-Supportively

- **Decision making:** Informational feedback, rationale, optimal challenge, acknowledging feelings, encouraging participative decision making
- **Goal setting:** Participative goal setting facilitates internalization and commitment. Employees who helped set goals are more likely to accept them as criteria for evaluation
- **Performance evaluations:** Supervisors who are autonomy-supportive and responsive in evaluation conversations produce more effective evaluations. A problem-solving approach to overcoming barriers is both effectance-relevant and positively motivating

## Applications

- **Management training programs:** Evidence shows that even a brief training intervention (Hardre & Reeve, 2009) produces measurable improvements in manager behavior and employee outcomes within weeks
- **Organizational design:** Build autonomy support into management development curricula, not as a one-time event but as an ongoing practice
- **Change management:** Apply the three conditions for autonomy-supportive change: clear rationale, some choice in implementation, acknowledgment of employees' feelings about the change (Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, & Leone, 1994)
- **Technology adoption:** When managers are supportive and the organization's rewards are perceived as just, employees are more autonomously motivated to adopt new technology (Mitchel et al., 2012)
