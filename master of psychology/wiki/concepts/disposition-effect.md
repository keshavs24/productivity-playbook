# Disposition Effect

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The disposition effect is the well-documented tendency for investors to sell winning investments too early (to lock in gains) and hold losing investments too long (to avoid realizing losses). The term reflects investors' "disposition" to hold losers and sell winners. It is a direct application of [[prospect-theory]] to financial decision-making: in the gains domain, investors are risk-averse (sell to secure the sure gain); in the loss domain, they are risk-seeking (hold, hoping for recovery).

## Key Findings

- **Odean (1998):** Demonstrated a strong preference among investors to hold stocks below purchase price (avoiding "becoming losers") and sell stocks above purchase price ("becoming winners"). The purchase price serves as the reference point. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch10]])
- **Odean (1999):** The winners that investors sell end up outperforming the losers they keep. Stock selection and taxes combine to make winner-selling and loser-holding a wealth-destroying strategy.
- **Barber, Odean, and Zheng (2005):** The same pattern holds for mutual funds -- investors hold onto losing funds and oversell winning ones
- **Calvet, Campbell, and Sodini (2009):** Investors become MORE eager to sell winners as performance improves -- the bias intensifies with larger gains
- **Tax irrationality:** Selling winners creates taxable capital gains, while selling losers creates tax-deductible losses. The disposition effect is therefore doubly irrational -- it leads to both worse investment returns AND higher tax bills.

## Mechanisms

1. **[[prospect-theory]] reference dependence:** The purchase price anchors evaluation. Gains (stock price above purchase price) trigger risk aversion; losses (below purchase price) trigger risk-seeking.
2. **[[loss-aversion]]:** Booking a loss is psychologically painful. As long as you hold a declining stock, you can pretend the loss doesn't exist. Selling forces you to enter it in "mental accounts" on the loss side.
3. **Regret avoidance:** Selling a winner and watching it go higher causes regret. Selling a loser and watching it recover causes even more regret. The asymmetry in anticipated regret favors inaction on losers.
4. **[[mental-accounting]]:** Each stock position is evaluated in its own mental account rather than as part of the overall portfolio.

## Applications

- **Personal investing:** Set sell discipline rules in advance (e.g., rebalancing at fixed intervals) to override the disposition effect
- **Tax-loss harvesting:** Deliberately sell losers to realize tax deductions -- the opposite of what the disposition effect encourages
- **Portfolio management:** Evaluate positions based on future expected returns, not on whether they are currently above or below your purchase price
- **Organizational decisions:** The disposition effect extends beyond stocks -- any situation where a decision-maker evaluates a project relative to its sunk costs rather than its future prospects

## Camerer's Extended Field Evidence (Ch. 16, CVF)

Camerer ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch16-pt-in-the-wild]]) adds critical field details:

- **Odean's brokerage data:** Investors held losing stocks a median of **124 days** vs. only **104 days** for winners. The unsold losers returned only **5%** the next year while sold winners returned **11.6%** -- investors systematically sold the wrong stocks.
- **December exception:** The winner-loser holding difference disappears in December when tax-loss selling motivation temporarily overwhelms the disposition effect, providing a within-subject control.
- **Weber and Camerer (1998) experimental control:** Subjects whose shares were automatically sold every period did not buy back losers more than winners. This proves the effect is about reluctance to **realize** losses (an act of commission), not optimism about loser recovery.
- **Housing market (Genovese & Meyer):** Strong disposition effect in housing sales -- owners facing nominal losses set prices too high and keep houses on the market too long.
- **Key PT ingredients:** The disposition effect is driven by the [[reflection-effect]] (risk-seeking in losses -- hold the loser and gamble on recovery) combined with [[loss-aversion]] (the pain of booking a realized loss).
