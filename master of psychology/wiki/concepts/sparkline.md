# Sparkline (Presentation Form)

**Phase(s):** 7
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The sparkline is a visual analysis tool developed by [[duarte|Nancy Duarte]] in *[[resonate]]* to graphically represent the contour of a presentation. The term is borrowed from [[edward-tufte|Edward Tufte]], who coined it for small, inline data graphics. In Duarte's usage, a sparkline maps how a presentation's content moves between *what is* (the audience's current reality) and *what could be* (the presenter's proposed future), while also tracking shifts in emotion and delivery method over time.

The sparkline is the visual expression of the **presentation form** — Duarte's adaptation of dramatic story structure (drawing from Gustav Freytag's Pyramid, Syd Field's Paradigm, and Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey) specifically tailored for presentations rather than cinema or literature.

## Key Findings

- Every great presentation analyzed in *Resonate* follows the presentation form when mapped as a sparkline, despite the presenters being unaware of the form. Steve Jobs's 2007 iPhone launch, Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech, Benjamin Zander's TED talk, Richard Feynman's gravity lecture, and Pastor John Ortberg's sermons all follow the same underlying contour.
- The sparkline has multiple layers: a gray line for spoken words (moving between what is and what could be), colored lines for contrasting emotion and delivery methods, time codes, engagement indicators (laughter, clapping), and verbal cues.
- No two sparklines are alike because no two presentations are alike — the form has enormous flexibility and rigid adherence would make presentations too predictable.

## Structure of the Presentation Form

The sparkline visualizes five structural elements:

1. **Beginning** — Paint a picture of the audience's current world (*what is*). Keep it concise (no more than 10% of total time). This establishes common ground.
2. **Turning Point 1: Call to Adventure** — Create a dramatic gap between what is and [[what-is-what-could-be]], jolting the audience from complacency. Must be explicit, not vague.
3. **Middle** — Alternate between what is and what could be, creating contrast through content, emotion, and delivery. This back-and-forth creates the characteristic up-and-down shape.
4. **Turning Point 2: Call to Action** — Clearly define what you are asking the audience to do. Four audience types: doers, suppliers, influencers, innovators.
5. **End** — Describe the *new bliss* — the inspirational world that has adopted the idea. Ends on a higher plane than the beginning. The dashed line after the end represents the audience's journey after leaving the presentation.

## Mechanisms

The sparkline works because it exploits the psychological power of **contrast**. The alternation between what is and what could be creates dramatic tension — an imbalance the audience wants to resolve. This connects to [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]]: by opening a gap between current reality and a desired future, the presenter creates a sense of incompleteness that drives attention and motivation.

The three types of contrast that generate the sparkline's contour:
- **Content contrast:** What is vs. what could be
- **Emotional contrast:** Analytical content vs. emotional content (the "beats" of cinema)
- **Delivery contrast:** Traditional vs. nontraditional delivery methods

## Applications

- Use the sparkline as a diagnostic tool: after drafting a presentation, map its contour. If the line stays flat (all what is, no what could be), the presentation lacks vision. If it stays elevated (all what could be, no what is), it lacks grounding.
- Validate that turning points are strong and explicit
- Verify that contrast exists in all three dimensions (content, emotion, delivery)
- Review case studies of great communicators to learn from their sparkline patterns
