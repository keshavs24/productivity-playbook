# Causality Orientations Theory (COT)

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Causality Orientations Theory (COT) is the third of SDT's six mini-theories. While [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]] and [[organismic-integration-theory]] focus on how social contexts affect motivation, COT addresses the complementary question: how do relatively stable **individual differences** in motivational orientations influence how people interpret and respond to those contexts? COT proposes three general causality orientations -- [[autonomy-orientation]], [[control-orientation]], and [[impersonal-orientation]] -- that represent personality-level propensities to orient toward certain features of environments and regulate behavior accordingly.

## Key Findings

- Everyone has **all three orientations** to some degree -- COT does not categorize people into types but sees each person as having a profile of relative strengths across the three orientations
- Orientations are **both outcomes of and inputs to development** -- a bidirectional, self-reinforcing cycle where early autonomy support builds autonomy orientation, which leads to interpreting future contexts as more autonomy-supportive
- Causality orientations are **empirically distinct from the Big Five** personality traits (Olesen et al., 2010, 2011) and explain additional variance over FFM
- Orientations can be **primed** -- experimental activation of autonomous or controlled orientations produces effects parallel to chronic trait measures (Levesque & Pelletier, 2003; Hodgins et al., 2006)
- The three orientations align in parallel with **regulatory styles** (autonomy with identified/integrated/intrinsic; controlled with introjected/external; impersonal with amotivation) and with **social contexts** (autonomy-supportive, controlling, amotivating)
- Assessed with the **General Causality Orientations Scale** (GCOS; Deci & Ryan, 1985a; Vallerand et al., 1987)

## Four Formal Propositions

1. **Proposition I:** People have three motivational orientations (autonomy, controlled, impersonal) representing global individual differences that affect situation-specific motivation, need satisfaction, behavior, and experience
2. **Proposition II:** Orientations are developmental outcomes of biological and social-contextual factors impacting need satisfaction
3. **Proposition III:** Orientations affect effectiveness and well-being, mediated by domain-specific motivations and need satisfactions
4. **Proposition IV:** Subtle environmental cues can prime any of the three orientations, significantly affecting behavior even when the chronic orientation is relatively weak

## Mechanisms

COT explains individual differences in motivation through:
1. **Selective attention** -- people orient toward different features of environments based on their dominant orientation
2. **Context interpretation** -- the same environment is construed as informational, controlling, or amotivating depending on the person's orientation
3. **Context seeking/creating** -- people actively seek and shape environments consistent with their orientations
4. **Self-reinforcing cycles** -- orientations shape context interpretations, which shape experiences, which further strengthen the orientation

## Applications

- **Understanding resilience:** High autonomy orientation buffers against the undermining effects of external rewards (Hagger & Chatzisarantis, 2011)
- **Predicting health outcomes:** Autonomy orientation independently predicts need satisfaction and well-being in work organizations, even controlling for the quality of the social context (Baard, Deci, & Ryan, 2004)
- **Relationship quality:** Autonomy orientation predicts openness, honesty, less defensiveness, and higher quality relationships (Hodgins et al., 1996, 2003)
- **Clinical implications:** Impersonal orientation is associated with psychopathology, including schizophrenia (Cooper et al., 2015) and anorexia nervosa (Strauss & Ryan, 1987)
