# Chunking

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Chunking is the process by which experts perceive and remember information in larger meaningful units (chunks) rather than individual elements. Through extensive experience, experts develop the ability to recognize familiar patterns and encode them as single units in memory, effectively multiplying their memory capacity within their domain of expertise.

## Key Findings

- **De Groot (1965, 1966):** Chess masters recalled positions of 20+ pieces after just 5 seconds of exposure, while duffers recalled only 4-5 pieces. With **random** board positions, masters showed NO advantage -- proving the effect depends on meaningful patterns, not superior general memory ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch9]])
- **Chase and Simon (1973):** Systematically compared beginners, Class A (advanced) players, and masters. Masters recalled both more chunks per board (7.7 vs. 5.3) and larger chunks (3.8 vs. 2.4 pieces per chunk). More than half of masters' chunks were pawn chains and other common game configurations.
- **Simon and Gilmartin (1973):** Estimated chess masters have acquired approximately **50,000 different chunks** -- roughly the same as a vocabulary in one's native language
- **Chase and Ericsson (1982):** Participant SF increased his digit span from 7 to 81 by chunking digits into running times (e.g., 3492 = "3 minutes 49.2 seconds -- near world-record mile time"). He used this to convert a span for 7 digits into a span for 7 patterns, then developed hierarchical "retrieval structures" to organize 22 such patterns. The skill was completely domain-specific -- his letter span remained normal.
- **Bilalic, Langner, Ulrich, and Grodd (2011):** The right fusiform visual area (normally engaged in face recognition) showed enhanced activation in chess experts viewing board positions, and responded more strongly to normal than random positions. Novices showed no such differentiation.
- Expert memory extends to long-term memory: Class A chess players showed no recall loss after a 30-second interfering delay, unlike weaker players (Charness, 1976). But expert chess players showed the same poor memory for three-letter trigrams as ordinary participants.

## Mechanisms

Chunking works by transforming the memory task. Instead of remembering 20 individual chess pieces, a master remembers 5-6 familiar patterns. Each pattern is a single unit in working memory. This has two components:
1. **Pattern recognition:** Through thousands of hours of experience, experts build a vast library of recognizable patterns (estimated ~50,000 for chess masters)
2. **Retrieval structures:** Experts develop organized ways to store and access these patterns in long-term memory, enabling recall of more patterns than working memory alone would allow (Chase & Ericsson, 1982)

Newell and Simon (1972) linked chunking to expertise in problem solving: masters have not only learned patterns but also learned what to do in the presence of each pattern. The chunk triggers the appropriate action without deliberate search.

## Applications

- Expertise in any domain involves building a library of recognizable patterns -- invest in exposure to many examples
- When learning a new domain, actively look for recurring patterns and name them -- this accelerates chunk formation
- Understand that expert "intuition" is largely pattern recognition based on a vast chunk library, not mystical insight (see [[expert-intuition]])
- Domain expertise does NOT transfer to general memory improvement -- chunking is domain-specific
