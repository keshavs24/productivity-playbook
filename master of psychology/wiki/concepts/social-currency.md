# Social Currency

**Phase(s):** 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Social currency is the idea that people share things that make them look good to others. Just as people use money to buy products and services, they use social currency to achieve desired positive impressions among their families, friends, and colleagues. We share remarkable facts, insider knowledge, and impressive experiences because doing so makes us seem smart, cool, entertaining, or in-the-know. Social currency is the first principle of [[berger]]'s [[stepps-framework]].

## Key Findings

- **Self-sharing activates reward circuits.** Harvard neuroscientists Jason Mitchell and Diana Tamir found that disclosing information about the self activates the same brain circuits that respond to food and money. People were willing to take a 25% pay cut for the chance to share their opinions.
- **40% of what people talk about is their own experiences.** Around half of tweets are "me" focused. People share to construct a positive self-image.
- **More remarkable products get talked about twice as often.** [[berger]] and Raghu Iyengar's analysis of 6,500 products and brands found that remarkability strongly predicted word of mouth frequency.
- **Paying people to share can backfire.** Monetary incentives crowd out intrinsic motivation. Once paid, the quality and quantity of word of mouth becomes proportional to payment rather than genuine enthusiasm.

## Mechanisms

Three mechanisms mint social currency:

### 1. Inner Remarkability
Find what makes something interesting, surprising, or novel. Remarkable things are worthy of remark — they break patterns of expectation. Examples: Snapple's surprising cap facts, Barclay Prime's $100 cheesesteak, JetBlue's unexpected comfort, *The Blair Witch Project*'s "found footage" format, black toilet paper at a dinner party.

Remarkability also evolves through retelling — people exaggerate details to make stories more interesting, which amplifies social currency through social transmission.

### 2. Game Mechanics
Rules, feedback loops, levels, and leaderboards make activities fun and create visible achievements that people want to share. Airlines gamified flying with frequent flier programs and status tiers. Foursquare gamified location visits with badges and "mayor" status. People share their achievements, and along the way they spread the word about the brand.

Key requirements for effective game mechanics:
- **Quantify performance** with clear metrics
- **Make relative standing intuitive** (red/yellow/green circles > confusing platinum/sapphire/diamond tiers)
- **Enable social comparison** (people prefer to do better than others, even at absolute cost — the Harvard $50K vs. $100K study)

### 3. Making People Feel Like Insiders
Scarcity and exclusivity make people feel special and increase perceived value. If something is difficult to obtain, people assume it must be worth the effort ([[social-proof]]). If something is invitation-only or limited-time, knowing about it or having access feels like insider knowledge.

Examples: Please Don't Tell (hidden bar, phone-booth entrance), Rue La La (invitation-only flash sales, $350M exit), McDonald's McRib (periodic scarcity created fervent demand), Disney Vault (pulling titles off market creates urgency).

Note: Making access difficult is different from making it impossible. The goal is to create allure, not frustration.

## Applications

- **Product design:** Find the inner remarkability in any product — what makes it surprising, novel, or pattern-breaking?
- **Loyalty programs:** Gamify engagement with clear levels, visible achievements, and social comparison opportunities
- **Exclusivity marketing:** Use invitation-only access, limited-time offers, and insider knowledge to make customers feel special
- **Content creation:** Share surprising facts, insider tips, or remarkable stories that make the audience look good for passing them on
- **Avoid monetary incentives for referrals** — they can crowd out the intrinsic desire to share
