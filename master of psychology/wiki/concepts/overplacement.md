# Overplacement

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Overplacement is the tendency to **falsely think we rank higher than others** on various dimensions, particularly in competitive contexts. It is the third form of overconfidence (Larrick, Burson, & Soll, 2007), alongside [[overprecision]] and [[overestimation]]. The classic manifestation is the "better-than-average" effect.

## Key Findings

- 93% of American drivers reported themselves more skillful than the median (Svenson, 1981) ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch2]])
- 60% of students rated their ability to get along with others in the top 10%; 25% placed themselves in the top 1% (College Board, 1976-1977)
- John Cannell (1989) called this the "Lake Wobegon effect" -- where "all the children are above average"
- **Critical nuance:** Overplacement occurs on easy tasks; **underplacement** occurs on difficult tasks (Kruger, 1999; Chambers et al., 2003). People believe they are more likely than others to live past 70 (common event) but less likely to live past 100 (rare event); more likely to own homes but less likely to own islands (Kruger & Burrus, 2004)
- The fundamental driver: people focus on their own abilities and **neglect the reference group** (Klar, Karelitz, Roziner, & Levi, 2012; Zell & Alicke, 2009; Moore & Kim, 2003). We exaggerate our own abilities while failing to consider that others face similar opportunities and challenges
- Consequences: inflated litigation expectations (Neale & Bazerman, 1985), excessive entrepreneurial entry into markets (Camerer & Lovallo, 1999; Moore, Oesch, & Zietsma, 2007), costly failed mergers (Malmendier & Tate, 2008). The majority of mergers fail (Ravenscraft & Scherer, 1989)
- Career selection effect: people choose vocations where they think they are distinctively good, creating a built-in overplacement bias within any field (Moore & Cain, 2007; Tesser, 1988)
- People rate themselves more highly on abstract/subjective dimensions (e.g., "environmentally friendly") than on specific behaviors (e.g., "recycling, reusing paper") (Wade-Benzoni, Li, Thompson, & Bazerman, 2007)

## Mechanisms

Overplacement stems from an egocentric focus: when evaluating ourselves relative to others, we have far more information about our own qualities than about others'. We focus on ourselves, exaggerate our abilities, and fail to consider that others face similar advantages and challenges. The comparison is further distorted by the selection effect -- people self-select into domains where they feel competent, ensuring that within any domain, most participants feel above average. Additionally, others' true levels of happiness, ability, or performance are often invisible to us (Klar & Giladi, 1999).

## Applications

- **Negotiation:** Overplacement leads to inflated expectations of winning in court, causing excessive litigation and impasses (Neale & Bazerman, 1985)
- **Entrepreneurship:** Reference group neglect leads to excessive entry into easy industries (where competition is fiercest) and insufficient entry into difficult ones (where competition is lighter) (Moore, Oesch, & Zietsma, 2007)
- **Investment:** Overplacement in stock-picking ability drives excessive trading and merger activity
- **Self-assessment:** The prescription is not to suppress confidence but to systematically consider the competition -- invest in understanding your reference group
