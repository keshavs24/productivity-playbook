# In-Group Favoritism

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition
The tendency to preferentially help, support, and allocate resources to people who share one's own group identity -- nationality, religion, race, gender, alma mater, profession, or other demographic characteristics. In-group favoritism is a manifestation of [[bounded-ethicality]] because it operates as a seemingly innocent impulse to help "people like us" while functionally discriminating against out-group members.

## Key Findings
- Dasgupta (2004): Reviewed nearly 100 studies demonstrating that people have a greater tendency to associate positive characteristics with in-groups and negative characteristics with out-groups
- **Favoritism as discrimination:** Favoring in-group members when allocating scarce resources (jobs, loans, college admissions) is mathematically equivalent to penalizing people who are different from you. In-group favoritism is "giving extra credit for shared demographic traits" (Bazerman & Moore)
- **Bernhard, Fischbacher, and Fehr (2006):** Willingness to enforce social norms by punishing violators is much greater when the victim of unfairness is similar to the punisher (same ethnic, racial, or language group)
- **Mortgage lending (Messick & Bazerman, 1996):** Discriminatory mortgage practices are more likely driven by in-group favoritism (making too many loans to in-group whites) than by overt hostility toward minorities. Given limited resources, favorable treatment of in-group members depletes funds available for out-group applicants.
- **Legacy admissions:** At top U.S. colleges, "legacies" (children of alumni) make up 10-15% of Ivy League classes. A 1990 Department of Education report found the typical Harvard legacy student was "significantly less qualified" than the average non-legacy in every relevant area but sports. University officials are often blind to the discriminatory effect of their own policies.
- People regard favors for in-group members as virtuous ("networking," "helping a friend") without recognizing the cost to out-group members

## Mechanisms
- **Identity-based categorization:** Humans naturally categorize others into in-groups and out-groups based on shared characteristics
- **Comfort asymmetry:** People are more comfortable doing favors for similar others than for different others
- **Moral reframing:** Helping in-group members is perceived as "doing something nice," while the equivalent harm to out-groups remains invisible
- **Systemic amplification:** When in-group members hold majority positions of power, individual acts of favoritism aggregate into systematic exclusion of minorities

## Applications
- **Hiring practices:** Recognize that "culture fit" hiring and referral networks often function as in-group favoritism mechanisms
- **Resource allocation:** Audit who benefits from discretionary resources (mentorship, project assignments, networking introductions) and check for in-group patterns
- **Self-reflection:** Before doing a favor for someone like you, ask whether you would do the same for someone different -- and whether your favoritism reduces others' opportunities
- **Institutional design:** Blind review processes, structured interviews, and explicit diversity criteria can counteract in-group favoritism at the system level
