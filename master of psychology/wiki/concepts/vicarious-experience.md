# Vicarious Experience

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Vicarious experience is the second most powerful [[sources-of-self-efficacy|source of self-efficacy]], operating through modeling and social comparison (Bandura, 1997, pp.86-101). People partly judge their capabilities by comparing their performances with those of others. Seeing or visualizing people similar to oneself perform successfully typically raises efficacy beliefs in observers that they themselves possess the capabilities to master comparable activities. Conversely, observing similar others fail can lower efficacy expectations.

## Key Findings

- Vicarious experiences are generally weaker than direct mastery experiences, but under some conditions they can override the impact of direct experience
- Modeling is especially influential when people have had little prior experience on which to base evaluations of their capabilities -- they rely more heavily on modeled indicators (Takata & Takata, 1976)
- Modeled successes by similar others raise observers' efficacy; modeled failures lower it (Brown & Inouye, 1978, Figure 3.2)
- Perceived dissimilarity to the model greatly reduces the impact of modeling -- exposure to a model depicted as well-versed in mathematics had no effect on students' math efficacy when they assumed the model was different from them (Prince, 1984)
- Diversified modeling (different people mastering different difficult tasks) is superior to a single model performing the same tasks (Bandura & Menlove, 1968; Kazdin, 1974a, 1975, 1976)
- Exposure to multiple skilled models produces stronger efficacy beliefs than observing a single model (Schunk, Hanson, & Cox, 1987)
- Social comparison operates as an ongoing process -- people see accomplishments of associates and compare their rate of progress against others of similar status

**Modes of modeling:**
- **Behavioral modeling:** Functional patterns adopted directly
- **Symbolic modeling:** Television, media, video technologies expand model availability
- **Cognitive modeling:** Models verbalize thought processes aloud; more effective than direct instruction alone (Gorrell & Capron, 1990); cognitive modeling with skill enactment is most effective (Fecteau & Stoppard, 1983)
- **Self-modeling:** Edited videotape replays of successful performances; more effective than didactic instruction (Scraba, 1990; Dowrick, 1991)
- **Cognitive self-modeling:** Visualizing oneself mastering progressively more challenging situations; benefits partly mediated by efficacy boosts

**Coping vs. mastery models:**
- **Coping models** begin timorously but gradually overcome difficulties through determined effort -- especially valuable for observers who are unsure of themselves
- **Mastery models** perform calmly and faultlessly -- convey functional information about how to exercise control
- Mixed evidence on which is more effective; when attention is explicitly focused on cognitive skills being modeled, both are equally effective
- Coping modeling is more likely to contribute to resilience under difficult, long-duration circumstances (relevant to innovators and social reformers)
- Coping modeling is more influential if embellished with modeled expressions of self-efficaciousness (Schunk & Hanson, 1985; Schunk et al., 1987)

**Key moderators:**
- **Performance similarity:** Similar or slightly higher ability provides the most informative comparison
- **Attribute similarity:** Age, sex, race, education similarity increases model impact, even when these attributes are spurious indicators of capability (Rosenthal & Bandura, 1978)
- **Multiplicity and diversity:** Multiple diverse models > single model
- **Model competence:** Highly competent models command more attention and instructional influence; competence overrides attribute dissimilarity (Lirgg & Feltz, 1991)

## Mechanisms

Vicarious experience affects efficacy through four subprocesses of observational learning (Figure 3.1):
1. **Attentional processes:** What is selectively observed from modeled events
2. **Retention processes:** Cognitive construction, symbolic coding, and rehearsal of observed information
3. **Production processes:** Translating conceptions into appropriate courses of action through conception matching
4. **Motivational processes:** External, vicarious, and self-incentives that regulate performance of observationally learned behavior

Vicarious experience also operates through **comparative self-evaluation** -- seeing the accomplishments of similar others can gladden or depress observers depending on how they fare in the social comparison (Bandura & Jourden, 1991).

## Applications

- **Mentorship design:** Match mentees with models who are similar enough to be informative but competent enough to be instructive
- **Multiple role models:** Expose learners to diverse models mastering the target activities
- **Coping model narratives:** Share stories of people who struggled but persevered -- more effective for self-doubting individuals than stories of effortless mastery
- **Cognitive modeling:** Have experts think aloud while solving problems to make covert strategies observable
- **Self-modeling video review:** Record successful performances and have people review edited highlights
