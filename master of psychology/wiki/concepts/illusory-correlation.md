# Illusory Correlation

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Illusory correlation is the **overestimation of the frequency of co-occurrence of two events that are associatively connected** — events that "go together" in the mind are perceived to co-occur more often than they actually do, even when the actual correlation is zero or negative.

## Key Findings

- Chapman and Chapman showed that both naive subjects and experienced clinicians "rediscovered" correlations between projective test responses (e.g., suspicious personality and "peculiar eyes" in drawings) that had no basis in data ([[summary-1-01-judgment-under-uncertainty]])
- Even when the data showed a *negative* correlation, the illusory correlation persisted — the associative link overwhelmed the evidence
- The effect was labeled "illusory correlation" — the pairing seems natural and the mind treats naturalness as evidence of frequency

## Mechanisms

The [[availability]] heuristic drives illusory correlation. When two events are associatively linked (suspiciousness + peculiar eyes), instances of their co-occurrence are more memorable and retrievable. This inflated retrievability is then interpreted as evidence of higher frequency. The mind confuses "these two things are conceptually related" with "these two things tend to happen together."

## Applications

- **Stereotyping:** Minority groups and distinctive behaviors co-occur infrequently in experience, but their distinctiveness makes the co-occurrence memorable — leading to inflated estimates of the association
- **Clinical diagnosis:** Clinicians may "see" symptom-diagnosis patterns that don't exist in the data because the pairing feels natural
- **Marketing:** Brand associations ("luxury" + "quality") can be created by pairing, even if the actual quality difference is negligible
- **Superstition:** Rituals paired with positive outcomes become "lucky" through illusory correlation
