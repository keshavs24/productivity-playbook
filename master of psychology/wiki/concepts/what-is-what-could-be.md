# What Is vs. What Could Be

**Phase(s):** 7
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

What Is vs. What Could Be is the fundamental contrast structure that drives the presentation form in [[duarte|Nancy Duarte]]'s *[[resonate]]*. It is the engine of persuasive presentations: the presenter alternates between describing the audience's current reality (*what is*) and painting a picture of a better future (*what could be*). This alternation creates dramatic tension — an imbalance the audience wants to resolve — producing the characteristic up-and-down contour visible in the [[sparkline]].

This is not merely a presentation technique but a universal narrative structure. Duarte's insight is that the greatest presentations in history — from Martin Luther King Jr. to Steve Jobs to Richard Feynman — all follow this alternating pattern, and that the pattern can be deliberately designed rather than left to chance.

## The Structural Pattern

### The Beginning: Establishing What Is

The presentation opens by establishing *what is* — a concise, honest formulation of the audience's current reality that everyone agrees is true. This should not take more than 10% of the total presentation time. Its purpose is threefold:

1. **Build common ground.** By accurately describing the audience's reality, the presenter demonstrates understanding and earns the right to propose change.
2. **Establish the baseline.** The power of "what could be" depends entirely on the contrast with "what is." A vivid current reality makes the future vision more dramatic.
3. **Create the gap.** The audience must feel the dissatisfaction of the present before they can desire the future. "What is" functions as the problem statement.

### The First Turning Point: The Call to Adventure

The first turning point reveals *what could be* for the first time, creating a stark contrast and making the gap clear. This moment sets the presentation in motion. Duarte draws the parallel to Joseph Campbell's "call to adventure" in the hero's journey — the moment when the hero's ordinary world is disrupted by a new possibility.

This is the inciting incident. As Robert McKee writes: "The inciting incident first throws the protagonist's life out of balance, then arouses in him the desire to restore that balance." In a presentation, the audience is the protagonist, their current reality is the ordinary world, and the presenter's vision is the call to adventure.

### The Middle: Alternation and Contrast

The middle of the presentation moves back and forth between what is and what could be, creating contrast that keeps the audience engaged. This structural movement creates three types of contrast:

1. **Content contrast** — alternating between current problems and future solutions, between data about the present and visions of the future
2. **Emotional contrast** — alternating between the discomfort of the status quo and the excitement of possibility, between fear of consequences and hope for improvement
3. **Delivery contrast** — alternating between analytical exposition (presenting what is) and passionate advocacy (presenting what could be), between slower/heavier pacing (problems) and faster/lighter pacing (solutions)

Each alternation raises the stakes. The what-is segments should progressively reveal deeper problems; the what-could-be segments should progressively flesh out a more detailed and compelling vision. The effect is an escalating series of contrasts that builds momentum toward the climax.

### The Climax: The S.T.A.R. Moment

Near the end of the middle section, the presentation reaches its climax — often a [[star-moment]] that dramatizes the contrast between what is and what could be at maximum intensity. This is the peak of the [[sparkline]].

### The End: The New Bliss

The end of the presentation describes the *new bliss* — a vivid picture of the world after the audience has adopted the idea — landing on a higher plane than where it began. The new bliss should:

- Be concrete and specific (not "a better future" but "a school where every child reads at grade level by third grade")
- Include the audience as agents of the change (they are the heroes, not the presenter)
- End on an emotionally elevated note that motivates action
- The call to action should be specific enough to execute immediately

## Duarte's Sparkline Analysis of Great Speeches

Duarte developed the [[sparkline]] — a visual mapping tool that tracks the what-is/what-could-be movement of a speech over time — and used it to analyze history's most celebrated presentations.

### Martin Luther King Jr. — "I Have a Dream" (1963)

Duarte's sparkline analysis of King's speech reveals a masterclass in what-is/what-could-be alternation:

- **What is (opening):** "The Negro is still sadly crippled by the manacles of segregation and the chains of discrimination... the Negro lives on a lonely island of poverty in the midst of a vast ocean of material prosperity." King establishes the current reality in devastating detail — the audience *feels* the injustice.
- **What could be:** "I have a dream that one day this nation will rise up and live out the true meaning of its creed: We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal." The vision is not abstract — it is rendered in specific, concrete images.
- **Alternation:** King moves back and forth throughout the speech — from the "sweltering summer of the Negro's legitimate discontent" (what is) to "the day when all of God's children will be able to sing" (what could be). Each cycle raises the emotional intensity.
- **New bliss:** "Free at last! Free at last! Thank God Almighty, we are free at last!" The ending lands on the highest emotional plane of the entire speech.

The sparkline shows that the speech's power comes not from any single section but from the **cumulative effect of repeated contrast** — each alternation amplifies the gap between reality and vision.

### Steve Jobs — iPhone Launch (2007)

Jobs' iPhone keynote follows the what-is/what-could-be pattern with surgical precision:

- **What is:** "The most advanced phones are called smartphones... but the problem is they're not so smart and they're not so easy to use." Jobs uses actual competitor products as what-is examples, showing their clunky interfaces, tiny keyboards, and confusing menus.
- **What could be:** "What we want to do is make a leapfrog product... a widescreen iPod with touch controls, a revolutionary mobile phone, and a breakthrough internet communications device." Three products. One device. The audience builds anticipation with each repetition before Jobs reveals they are all the same device.
- **S.T.A.R. moment:** "Are you getting it? These are not three separate devices. This is one device." The realization — the what-could-be collapsing into a single product — is the climactic contrast.
- **Alternation through the demo:** Jobs repeatedly shows how current phones handle tasks (painful, slow, multi-step) and then demonstrates how the iPhone handles the same task (elegant, fast, intuitive). Each demo is a micro-cycle of what-is to what-could-be.

### Richard Feynman — Challenger Investigation (1986)

Feynman's famous O-ring demonstration is often remembered as a single moment, but Duarte's analysis reveals the what-is/what-could-be pattern:

- **What is:** The Challenger exploded. Seven astronauts died. The official investigation is producing mountains of impenetrable engineering data that the public cannot understand.
- **What could be:** The cause is actually simple — understandable by anyone — and preventable.
- **S.T.A.R. moment:** The O-ring in ice water. In 30 seconds, Feynman made the incomprehensible comprehensible. The contrast between the complexity of the official investigation (what is) and the simplicity of the demonstration (what could be) was the source of its power.

## Empirical Evidence: Heritage and Greatbatch (1986)

Heritage and Greatbatch analyzed **476 political speeches** and coded every burst of audience applause. Their finding: half of all applause bursts could be attributed to moments where a form of **contrast** was communicated. Contrast was the single most powerful predictor of audience engagement, ahead of three-part lists, puzzle-solution pairs, and headline-punchline structures.

This is empirical confirmation that the what-is/what-could-be alternation is not merely an aesthetic preference but a measurably effective technique for engaging audiences. The contrast principle works because it creates cognitive and emotional movement — the audience oscillates between dissatisfaction and hope, discomfort and desire, and this oscillation keeps them actively engaged rather than passively listening.

## Psychological Mechanisms

### 1. Gap Theory of Curiosity

By revealing a gap between current reality and a possible better future, the presenter creates informational incompleteness that the audience is motivated to resolve. This is the same mechanism identified by George Loewenstein in his [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]]: curiosity arises when we perceive a gap between what we know and what we want to know. The what-is/what-could-be structure is a deliberately engineered curiosity gap.

Critically, the gap must be maintained throughout the presentation. If the presenter resolves the gap too early (presenting the complete solution immediately), the audience's curiosity is satisfied and engagement drops. The alternating structure sustains the gap by repeatedly opening it wider (what is gets worse) and partially closing it (what could be gets more detailed), maintaining tension throughout.

### 2. Cognitive Contrast Effects

The juxtaposition of current problems with future possibilities makes both more vivid. This is the [[contrast-principle]] at work — the same principle that makes a lukewarm room feel cold after you come in from the sun, or makes a $50 accessory feel cheap after you have just spent $5,000 on a laptop.

In presentations:
- The what-is baseline makes what-could-be feel more dramatic and desirable
- The what-could-be vision makes what-is feel more painful and urgent
- Each side amplifies the other — the contrast is synergistic, not merely additive

This connects to the contrast research from [[influence-science-and-practice]]: sequential presentation of contrasting stimuli distorts perception of the second stimulus relative to what it would have been perceived as in isolation.

### 3. Loss Aversion and Dual Framing

The what-is/what-could-be structure simultaneously leverages both sides of [[loss-aversion]]:

- **What is** frames the status quo as a loss — "Look at what you are losing by staying where you are." This triggers loss aversion, which is psychologically more motivating than equivalent gains.
- **What could be** frames the future as a gain — "Look at what you will gain by moving forward." This activates the approach motivation system.

By alternating between loss framing (what is) and gain framing (what could be), the presenter activates both motivational systems, creating a push-pull dynamic that is more powerful than either frame alone.

### 4. Narrative Transportation

The alternating structure creates a narrative arc — a story with a beginning (current reality), a middle (the struggle between what is and what could be), and an end (the new bliss). This arc triggers narrative transportation — the state of cognitive and emotional immersion that makes stories persuasive. Audiences transported into a narrative are less likely to counterargue and more likely to adopt the attitudes embedded in the story (Green & Brock, 2000).

## The Contrasting Elements

Contrasting elements that map to what is / what could be:

|---|---|
| Alternate point of view | Your point of view |
| Past/Present | Future |
| Pain | Gain |
| Problem | Solution |
| Roadblocks | Clear Passage |
| Resistance | Action |
| Impossible | Possible |
| Need | Fulfillment |
| Disadvantage | Advantage (Opportunity) |
| Information | Insight |
| Ordinary | Special |
| Question | Answer |
| Complication | Resolution |
| Fear | Courage |
| Scarcity | Abundance |
| Confusion | Clarity |

## Building the Sparkline: A Practical Process

Duarte recommends using the [[sparkline]] as both a planning and diagnostic tool:

1. **Draft your presentation normally** — get all your content in order
2. **Map each section to "what is" or "what could be"** — color-code or label each segment
3. **Draw the sparkline** — plot the trajectory of your presentation on a what-is/what-could-be axis over time
4. **Diagnose problems:**
   - If the sparkline is flat (staying in what-is or what-could-be for too long), add contrast
   - If there is no clear upward trajectory, the presentation lacks momentum
   - If the ending is not at the highest point, rework the closing
   - If there is no S.T.A.R. moment near the peak, design one
5. **Revise to create movement** — ensure the presentation oscillates between the two states with increasing amplitude

## Applications

- **Begin every presentation by establishing what is** — acknowledge the audience's current reality honestly and concisely. Do not start with your solution; start with their problem.
- **Make the first turning point explicit and dramatic** — the gap between what is and what could be should be crystal clear. This is your "call to adventure" moment.
- **Throughout the middle, consciously alternate** between what is and what could be rather than staying on one side. Each alternation should raise the stakes.
- **End with a vivid description of the new bliss** — the world after adoption — that leaves the audience on a higher emotional plane. Include them as the agents of change.
- **Use the [[sparkline]] to verify** that your presentation actually moves between the two states rather than staying flat. This is the diagnostic test for structural quality.
- **Apply the contrast principle at the micro level too** — within individual slides, within individual arguments, within individual stories. "Before and after" is a miniature what-is/what-could-be structure.
- **Combine with [[star-moment]]** — place your most dramatic S.T.A.R. moment at the point of maximum contrast for maximum impact.
- **Adjust the ratio for context:** Technical presentations to skeptical audiences may need more what-is (building the case for a problem before presenting the solution). Inspirational presentations may weight toward what-could-be after establishing the baseline.

## Open Questions
- How does the optimal frequency of alternation vary with presentation length? A 5-minute pitch may support 2-3 alternations; a 60-minute keynote may support 8-10. What are the boundaries?
- Does the what-is/what-could-be structure work equally well in written communication (memos, proposals, essays), or is it specifically tuned to the temporal, sequential nature of spoken presentation?
- How do cultural differences in directness and conflict comfort affect the reception of the contrast structure? In cultures where direct criticism of the status quo is uncomfortable, does a softer what-is framing work, or is the structure itself culturally bound?
- Can the structure be inverted for defensive communication — where the goal is to defend the status quo against proposed change? (What is = good; what could be = risky)
