# If/When-Then Plans (Implementation Intentions)

**Phase(s):** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Confidence:** 0.75

## Definition

If/when-then plans (also called **implementation intentions** in scholarly usage) are strategic self-statements designed to achieve a goal by: (1) readying us to *notice* specific cues in settings where we can further our goal, and (2) pre-constructing a strong *association* between those cues and an appropriate goal-consistent action. Their format: "If/when [situation X occurs], then I will [perform behavior Y]."

Cialdini identifies these plans as the most powerful form of self-directed [[pre-suasion]] because they allow individuals to install their own pre-suasive cue-action associations — essentially programming new [[click-whirr]] responses for personal benefit.

The concept was originated by psychologist **Peter Gollwitzer** at New York University, whose research program spanning over three decades has established implementation intentions as one of the most reliably effective behavior change tools in all of psychology.

## Gollwitzer's Research Program

Peter Gollwitzer introduced the concept of implementation intentions in his seminal 1993 paper "Goal Achievement: The Role of Intentions" and has built one of the most impressive and consistent research programs in social psychology around this single idea.

### The Core Insight

Gollwitzer's starting observation was that goal intentions ("I intend to achieve X") are poor predictors of actual behavior. Meta-analyses show that goal intentions account for only about 20-30% of variance in behavior — meaning that 70-80% of the time, people who sincerely intend to do something fail to follow through. The gap between intention and action is one of the largest and most consistent findings in psychology.

Gollwitzer proposed that the problem is not motivation but **strategic ignorance** — people know *what* they want to do but have not specified *when, where, and how* they will do it. Implementation intentions bridge this gap by creating an explicit plan that links a specific situational cue to a specific behavioral response.

### The Distinction: Goal Intentions vs. Implementation Intentions

| Feature | Goal Intention | Implementation Intention |
|---------|---------------|-------------------------|
| Format | "I intend to achieve X" | "If/when situation Y, then I will do Z" |
| Specificity | General, abstract | Specific situation + specific behavior |
| Mechanism | Motivation (wanting) | Automaticity (triggering) |
| Processing | Effortful, deliberate | Automatic, cue-driven |
| Vulnerability | Distraction, forgetting, competing goals | Relatively resistant to interference |
| Success rate | ~20-30% follow-through | ~60-80% follow-through |

### Meta-Analytic Evidence

Gollwitzer and Sheeran's (2006) meta-analysis of 94 independent studies found that implementation intentions had a **medium-to-large effect** (d = 0.65) on goal attainment across domains including health, academic performance, environmental behavior, and interpersonal relationships. This is a remarkably large and consistent effect for a psychological intervention, especially one that is completely free, requires no external resources, and takes only seconds to form.

## Key Studies

### The Opiate Addict Study (0% vs. 80%)

This is perhaps the most dramatic demonstration of implementation intentions in the literature. Participants were heroin and opiate addicts in a withdrawal treatment program — individuals under extreme physical and cognitive duress.

- **Task:** Complete a short employment history (resume) by the end of the day, as part of their recovery program
- **Control group:** Given the standard goal intention: "Please complete this employment history by 5 PM today." **Result: 0% compliance.** Not a single addict in the control group completed the form.
- **Implementation intention group:** Instructed to form a specific plan: "If/when it is [specific time], and I am in [specific location], then I will write my employment history." **Result: 80% compliance.** Four out of five addicts completed the form.

The contrast is staggering: from 0% to 80%, produced by nothing more than specifying when and where the action would occur. These were people in active opiate withdrawal — nauseous, anxious, in pain, with severely compromised executive function. If implementation intentions work under these conditions, they work under virtually any conditions.

### The Colonoscopy Screening Study

Sheeran and Orbell (2000) studied attendance at cervical cancer screening appointments:

- **Control group:** Women received standard information about the importance of screening. Attendance rate: approximately 69%.
- **Implementation intention group:** Women formed a plan specifying when, where, and how they would attend their screening. Attendance rate: approximately 92%.
- The 23-percentage-point improvement is clinically significant — it translates to thousands of additional cancer screenings and potentially many lives saved, from an intervention that costs nothing and takes 30 seconds to deliver.

### The Exercise Study

Milne, Orbell, and Sheeran (2002) tested implementation intentions for exercise:

- **Motivation-only group:** Received a motivational presentation about the health benefits of exercise. Exercise rates the following week: 35%.
- **Implementation intention group:** Same motivational presentation PLUS formed a plan: "During the next week, I will partake in at least 20 minutes of vigorous exercise on [day] at [time] in [place]." Exercise rates: 91%.
- The motivational presentation alone barely outperformed the control group (38% vs. 35%). Adding the implementation intention nearly tripled the rate. This demonstrates that the bottleneck is not motivation but strategic planning.

### The Epilepsy Medication Study

Medication adherence is a pervasive healthcare challenge — roughly 50% of patients with chronic conditions do not take their medications as prescribed. Brown, Sheeran, and Reuber tested implementation intentions with epilepsy patients:

- **Standard care:** 55% medication adherence
- **Implementation intention:** Patients formed plans like "When it is eight in the morning, and I finish brushing my teeth, then I will take my prescribed pill dose." Adherence rose to **79%**.
- The 24-percentage-point improvement, sustained over months, represents a major clinical benefit with zero cost.

### The Chronic Dieter Study

Adriaanse et al. studied habitually unsuccessful dieters — people who repeatedly try and fail to control their eating:

- Dieters who formed implementation intentions like "If/when I see chocolate displayed in the supermarket, then I will think of my diet" consumed fewer high-calorie snacks and lost more weight than those with equivalent goal intentions
- Critically, the implementation intentions worked for **chronic dieters** — people for whom simple motivation had repeatedly failed. The plans provided an alternative pathway to behavior change that bypassed the motivation deficit.

### The Voting Study

Nickerson and Rogers (2010) tested implementation intentions in a field experiment during the 2008 presidential primary:

- Voters contacted by phone and asked to form a plan (when will you vote? where is your polling place? what will you be doing beforehand?) voted at a rate 4.1 percentage points higher than those who received a standard get-out-the-vote call
- In single-voter households, the effect was 9.1 percentage points — a substantial increase from a brief phone conversation

## Mechanism: Automaticity via Cue-Response Linking

Standard good intentions fail ~50% of the time for two reasons:
1. **Failure to recognize opportune moments** — we don't notice situations where goal-relevant action is possible (e.g., taking the stairs instead of the elevator, bringing up a difficult topic during a natural conversational pause)
2. **Derailment by distractions** — especially on busy days, competing demands crowd out goal-directed behavior

If/when-then plans defeat both enemies through a single mechanism: **strategic automaticity.**

### How It Works

1. **The "if/when" component installs heightened vigilance** for a specific situational cue. By mentally rehearsing the trigger situation, the person becomes primed to *notice* it when it occurs. This is attentional tuning — the same mechanism that makes you notice your name in a crowded room (the cocktail party effect). The plan makes the cue perceptually salient.

2. **The "then" component pre-constructs a strong cue-action association.** When the trigger is detected, the desired behavior fires automatically, like a [[click-whirr]] response. The person does not need to deliberate, weigh options, or muster willpower. The plan has already made the decision; the situation merely executes it.

3. **The plan is self-tailored.** Unlike external nudges or environmental designs, the individual chooses cues they are likely to encounter and actions that work for their specific circumstances. This customization increases the likelihood of both noticing the cue and being able to perform the response.

### The Pavlovian Analogy

The process is fundamentally pre-suasive: like Pavlovian conditioning, it creates a manufactured association between a cue and a response. But unlike external conditioning, the individual is both the programmer and the target. This makes implementation intentions a form of **self-directed conditioning** — using the same associative learning mechanisms that underlie habits and reflexes, but deployed deliberately and strategically.

### Why Automaticity Matters

The critical advantage of automaticity is that it **bypasses the bottleneck of conscious deliberation.** Deliberation is effortful, slow, and vulnerable to depletion, distraction, and competing goals. Automatic responses are fast, efficient, and resistant to interference. By converting a deliberative intention ("I should exercise") into an automatic cue-response pair ("If it's 7 AM and I've finished coffee, then I put on running shoes"), the plan moves the behavior from System 2 to something closer to System 1 processing.

This is why implementation intentions work even under conditions of severe cognitive impairment (opiate withdrawal), emotional distress (chronic dieting frustration), and extreme busyness (voter studies). The plan does not require cognitive resources to execute — it requires only the detection of the trigger.

## Applications

- **Health:** Medication adherence, diet management, exercise initiation, smoking cessation, cancer screening attendance, dental hygiene
- **Addiction recovery:** The opiate addict study shows if/when-then plans work even under extreme cognitive and physiological impairment. Recovery programs should systematically teach implementation intentions.
- **Productivity:** "If/when I finish my morning coffee, then I will write for 90 minutes before checking email." "If/when I feel the urge to check social media, then I will take three deep breaths and return to my current task."
- **Habit formation:** Any desired habit can be anchored to an existing routine via the if/when trigger. This is the core mechanism behind habit stacking (James Clear's term): attach the new behavior to an established cue.
- **Academic performance:** "If/when the exam room doors open, then I will spend 5 minutes reviewing past academic successes" (Alan Chaikin's strategy for combating test anxiety via self-affirmation)
- **Negotiation (Phase 5):** "If/when the other party makes a take-it-or-leave-it demand, then I will pause, take a breath, and ask 'Help me understand what's driving that requirement.'" Pre-programming constructive responses to predictable negotiation provocations.
- **Difficult conversations (Phase 4):** "If/when I feel my anger rising during the conversation, then I will say 'I need a moment to think about that' and take a breath." Managing emotional reactivity through pre-planned responses.

## Design Principles for Effective If/When-Then Plans

Research suggests several principles for maximizing effectiveness:

1. **Be specific about the cue.** "When I feel stressed" is too vague. "When I sit down at my desk Monday morning and open my laptop" is specific enough to trigger detection.
2. **Be specific about the response.** "Then I will be healthier" is not a behavior. "Then I will eat an apple instead of chips" is a concrete action.
3. **Choose cues you will reliably encounter.** A plan linked to a rare situation will rarely fire. Link to daily routines for daily goals.
4. **Choose a single cue-response pair per plan.** Complex multi-step plans lose the automaticity advantage.
5. **Mentally rehearse the plan.** Simply writing it down is less effective than vividly imagining the situation and your response. Visualization strengthens the cue-response association.
6. **Review and update plans periodically.** As circumstances change, old cues may become irrelevant. Update plans to match your current environment.

## Open Questions

- What is the optimal number of if/when-then plans a person can maintain simultaneously? Too many may dilute attention or create interference between cue-response pairs.
- Do plans lose effectiveness over time if the cue-action association is never triggered (extinction)? Or does the vigilance for the cue persist indefinitely once installed?
- How do if/when-then plans interact with intrinsic motivation — do they enhance or undermine autonomous self-regulation? If plans make behavior automatic, does this reduce the sense of autonomy that [[self-determination-theory]] identifies as essential for sustained motivation?
- Can implementation intentions be combined effectively with other behavior change tools (habit tracking, social accountability, environmental design) for compounding effects?
- Do implementation intentions work equally well for approach goals ("start exercising") and avoidance goals ("stop smoking")? Some evidence suggests the mechanisms differ.
