# Efficacy Expectations vs. Outcome Expectations

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Bandura (1997) draws a fundamental distinction between two types of expectation beliefs that jointly regulate human behavior:

- **Efficacy expectations (perceived self-efficacy):** Judgments of one's ability to organize and execute given types of performances. "Can I do this?"
- **Outcome expectations:** Judgments of the likely consequences that such performances will produce. "If I do this, what will happen?"

The conditional relationship is depicted in Figure 1.3: PERSON → [Efficacy Beliefs] → BEHAVIOR → [Outcome Expectancies] → OUTCOME. Efficacy beliefs determine whether a person will attempt a behavior; outcome expectancies determine the perceived value of doing so.

This distinction is critical because much of the psychology of motivation and expectancy had, prior to Bandura, focused almost exclusively on outcome expectations (e.g., Rotter's locus of control, Tolman's expectancy theory, Atkinson's expectancy-valence theory).

## Key Findings

- Where performance determines outcome, efficacy beliefs account for most of the variance in expected outcomes -- when differences in efficacy beliefs are controlled, outcome expectations make little or no independent contribution to behavioral prediction ([[summary-3-11-se-ch1-theoretical-perspectives]])
- This holds across diverse domains: academic attainments, social behavior, weight management, health habits, pain management, phobic behavior, occupational performance, and choice of cultural milieu
- A performance is an accomplishment; an outcome is the consequence of a performance -- serious conceptual problems arise when these are conflated (e.g., treating a high-jump of seven feet as an "outcome" rather than as a performance attainment)
- Outcome expectations take three major forms: (1) **physical** -- pleasant/aversive sensory experiences; (2) **social** -- approval, recognition, rejection, censure; (3) **self-evaluative** -- self-satisfaction, pride, self-censure
- Expected outcomes are independent of efficacy beliefs only when contingencies are restrictively structured so no level of competence by certain groups can produce desired outcomes (e.g., racial segregation in professional sports, gender barriers in executive positions)
- Self-efficacy theory distinguishes degrees of controllability by personal means -- the relationship between efficacy beliefs and outcome expectations depends on how tightly contingencies between actions and outcomes are structured, either inherently or socially

## Mechanisms

The two types of expectations interact in a 2x2 matrix (Figure 1.2) that predicts distinct behavioral and affective patterns:

| | Outcome Expectations (+) | Outcome Expectations (-) |
|---|---|---|
| **Efficacy Beliefs (+)** | Productive engagement, aspiration, personal satisfaction | Protest, grievance, social activism, milieu change |
| **Efficacy Beliefs (-)** | Self-devaluation, despondency | Resignation, apathy |

- High efficacy + positive outcomes = productive engagement and satisfaction
- High efficacy + negative outcomes (unresponsive environment) = protest, social activism, efforts to change the system
- Low efficacy + positive outcomes = self-devaluation and despondency (seeing others succeed where one cannot)
- Low efficacy + negative outcomes = resignation and apathy

The distinction explains why [[locus-of-control]] is a weak predictor: locus of control conflates the question of whether actions affect outcomes with the question of whether one can perform those actions.

## Applications

- **Goal setting:** Both types of expectations matter -- people must believe both that they can perform the required actions AND that those actions will produce valued outcomes
- **Motivation in restrictive environments:** High efficacy combined with negative outcome expectations (because the system is rigged) produces social activism rather than resignation -- explaining why efficacious individuals in discriminatory systems fight for change rather than giving up
- **Performance vs. outcome measurement:** Researchers and practitioners must distinguish between assessing someone's performance attainment and the outcomes that flow from it -- confusing the two creates conceptual muddles

## Open Questions
- In practice, how cleanly can efficacy expectations and outcome expectations be separated, especially in domains where the relationship between action and outcome is ambiguous?
- How do the three forms of outcome expectations (physical, social, self-evaluative) interact with efficacy beliefs differently?
- How does the 2x2 matrix operate in collectivistic contexts where group outcomes may matter more than individual outcomes?
