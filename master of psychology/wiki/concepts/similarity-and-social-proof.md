# Similarity and Social Proof

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.5

## Definition

Similarity is the second key condition that maximizes the power of [[social-proof]]. The principle of social proof operates most powerfully when we are observing the behavior of people just like us (Festinger, 1954; Platow et al., 2005). It is the conduct of similar others that gives us the greatest insight into what constitutes correct behavior for ourselves. We are therefore more inclined to follow the lead of a similar individual than a dissimilar one (Abrams, Wetherell, Cochrane, Hogg, & Turner, 1990; Burn, 1991; Schultz, 1999).

## Key Findings

- **College fundraising** (Aune & Basil, 1994): Donations more than doubled when the requester claimed to be a fellow student ("I'm a student here, too")
- **Antismoking programs** (Murray, Leupker, Johnson, & Mittlemark, 1984): School-based programs had lasting effects only when using same-age peer leaders, not adult teachers
- **Dental anxiety** (Melamed et al., 1978): Children's dental anxiety was reduced by filmed models only when the child in the film was the same age
- **Cialdini's son Chris:** A 3-year-old boy refused to swim without an inflatable ring despite months of adult coaching, but instantly overcame his fear after seeing Tommy, also age 3, swim without one. "I'm 3 years old, and Tommy is 3 years old. And Tommy can swim without a ring, so that means I can, too."
- **Bible book sales** (Reader's Report 4.2): A salesman increased weekly sales from $550.80 to $1,209.15 (119.67% improvement) by matching testimonials to prospect demographics — female names for women, male names for men, couple names for couples
- **Werther effect demographic matching** (Phillips, 1980): Young suicide victims produce young copycat crash victims; older suicide victims produce older crash victims. The imitators match the model.
- **Copycat homicides** (Phillips, 1983): When a black boxer lost a televised fight, homicides rose among young black males; when a white boxer lost, among young white males
- **Jonestown:** By relocating to Guyana, Jones ensured that the only similar others available to Temple members were other Temple members — concentrating the power of social proof exclusively within the group
- **Advertising:** The proliferation of average-person-on-the-street testimonials reflects advertisers' understanding that ordinary viewers identify with ordinary spokespersons, not celebrities

## Mechanisms

Similarity amplifies social proof because:
1. **Informational relevance** — Similar others face similar circumstances, so their choices are more diagnostic of what is correct *for us*
2. **Identification** — We mentally project ourselves into the shoes of similar others, making their behavior feel more applicable
3. **Reduced discounting** — We are less likely to dismiss the behavior of someone like us as irrelevant to our situation

The similarity dimension that matters most depends on context — it could be age, gender, social role, profession, nationality, or circumstance. The key is perceived similarity on dimensions *relevant to the behavior in question.*

## Applications

- **Marketing/Sales:** Match testimonials and case studies to the demographic profile of the target audience. Use "people like you" framing.
- **Health interventions:** Use same-age, same-background peer educators for maximum impact
- **Parenting/Education:** Leverage peer models rather than authority figures when trying to change children's behavior
- **Influence defense:** Be aware that you are most susceptible to social proof from similar others. When you find yourself being influenced by what "people like me" are doing, that is precisely when to engage critical thinking.
- **Cult awareness:** Leaders who isolate followers ensure that the only available similar others reinforce the leader's preferred behavior
