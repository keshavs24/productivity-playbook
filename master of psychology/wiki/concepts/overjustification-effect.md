# Overjustification Effect

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The overjustification effect occurs when an external incentive (such as a reward) is provided for an activity that is already intrinsically motivating, causing the person to attribute their behavior to the external incentive rather than to their intrinsic interest. The result is a decrease in intrinsic motivation after the external incentive is removed. The term derives from attribution theory's discounting principle (Kelley, 1967): when multiple sufficient causes exist for a behavior, the perceived role of any single cause is diminished.

The classic demonstration: Lepper, Greene, & Nisbitt (1973) offered preschool children a "good player award" for drawing -- an activity they already enjoyed. In a subsequent free-choice period, these children drew significantly less than children who received no reward or who received an unexpected reward.

## Key Findings

- **Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett (1973):** The original study. Expected rewards reduced free-choice drawing. Unexpected rewards did not -- the effect requires the person to anticipate the reward before engaging
- **Deci (1971):** The first published demonstration in adults. College students paid $1 per Soma puzzle showed decreased subsequent free-choice persistence
- **Ross (1975):** Rewards had to be salient to produce the effect -- marshmallows hidden from view did not undermine; visible marshmallows did
- **Warneken & Tomasello (2008):** Demonstrated the undermining effect in children as young as 20 months, before the age at which cognitive discounting could be the mediator (Morgan, 1981), suggesting the CET/SDT mechanism (PLOC shift) is more fundamental than the attribution account
- **Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis:** 128 studies confirmed that expected, tangible, contingent rewards significantly undermine intrinsic motivation (d=-0.36 for expected tangible rewards)

## Mechanisms

### Attribution Theory Account (Lepper et al., 1973)
- When people have both intrinsic interest **and** an external reward for doing an activity, they have "overjustification" -- too many reasons
- Per the discounting principle, they discount the less salient cause (intrinsic interest) in favor of the more salient one (external reward)
- After reward removal, only the diminished intrinsic interest remains

### SDT/CET Account (Deci & Ryan, 1980a, 1985b)
- Rewards shift the perceived locus of causality (PLOC) from internal to external
- The person comes to experience the activity as something done *for the reward* rather than for its inherent satisfaction
- This thwarts the [[autonomy-need]], diminishing intrinsic motivation
- The SDT account better explains why the effect occurs in very young children (who cannot engage in attributional discounting) and why the style of reward delivery matters

### Related: Insufficient Justification Effect
- The inverse: When external justification is very small (e.g., $1 in Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959), people may increase attributed intrinsic interest to resolve [[cognitive-dissonance]]
- SDT's alternative: Small rewards are less likely to be experienced as controlling and can be informational

## Applications

- **Education:** Using sticker charts, grades, and prizes for already-interesting learning activities risks undermining children's natural curiosity. The effect is stronger for children than adults
- **Parenting:** Paying children for chores they already enjoy or for activities like reading can undermine their intrinsic interest
- **Workplace:** Converting voluntary pro-social behaviors into compensated ones can destroy the social-norm motivation (parallel to [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]])
- **Volunteerism:** Titmuss (1971) found blood donations declined when payment was introduced -- paying volunteers "crowds out" intrinsic prosocial motivation (Frey, 1997)
- **Design principle:** If people are already intrinsically interested, do not add controlling external incentives. If external incentives must be used, make them unexpected, informational, or autonomy-supportive

## Open Questions
- Is the overjustification effect permanent, or can intrinsic motivation be restored after reward removal? If so, how long does restoration take?
- How does the effect interact with digital reward systems (gamification badges, streaks, likes)?
- Does the attributional account or the SDT/PLOC account better predict effects in different age groups and cultural contexts?
