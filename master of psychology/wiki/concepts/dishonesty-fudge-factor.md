# Dishonesty Fudge Factor

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The dishonesty fudge factor is Ariely's model of everyday dishonesty, proposing that most people cheat a little — up to the point where they can still maintain a positive self-image as honest individuals. Unlike the Simple Model of Rational Crime (SMORC), which predicts that cheating scales with opportunity and inversely with detection probability, the fudge factor model holds that dishonesty is governed primarily by internal moral constraints rather than external incentives. The "fudge factor" represents the gap between complete honesty and the maximum amount of cheating a person can do while still feeling honest.

## Key Findings

### Core Fudge Factor Evidence
- **Harvard/MIT matrix task (Ariely, Mazar, & Amir):** Across four conditions with increasing opportunity and decreasing detection risk, cheating remained remarkably constant at about 3-4 extra questions claimed (out of 50). Replicated at MIT, Princeton, UCLA, and Yale. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch13-character-1]])
- **Insensitivity to detection risk:** Students who could shred all evidence and take money from a jar cheated at the same level as those whose cheating was partially traceable — contradicting cost-benefit models of crime. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch13-character-1]])
- **No total cheaters (with cash):** Of 2,000 participants across cash-based honesty experiments, only 4 (0.2%) claimed to have solved all problems. Even with complete impunity and a $100 jar available, no one took more than they "deserved." ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch14-character-2]])

### Fudge Factor Expansion with Nonmonetary Media
- **Token experiment (Ariely, Mazar, & Amir):** When payment was one step removed from cash (tokens exchangeable for money within seconds), cheating more than doubled (5.9 extra questions vs. 2.7 for cash). Total cheaters jumped from 0.2% (cash) to 16% (tokens). ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch14-character-2]])
- The fudge factor is not fixed — it expands when the medium of dishonesty is nonmonetary, because distance from cash allows greater self-deception ("I'm not really stealing money")
- Real-world manifestations: insurance fraud (stretching claims by ~10%), wardrobing ($16B/year), expense report padding, stock option backdating, credit card fine print

### Fudge Factor Elimination with Moral Reminders
- **Ten Commandments experiment:** Merely recalling the Commandments before a cheating opportunity eliminated all cheating — even for students who could remember only 1-2 of the 10. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch13-character-1]])
- **Honor code experiment (MIT):** Signing a statement about academic honesty eliminated cheating entirely — even though MIT has no honor code. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch13-character-1]])
- **Toilet paper experiment (reader Rhonda):** A note reminding housemates not to hoard communal toilet paper produced immediate compliance. ([[summary-2-S1-pi-ch13-character-1]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Superego monitoring (Freudian framework):** The internal honesty monitor activates only for large transgressions. Small cheating slips below the threshold of moral self-scrutiny — "our superego stays asleep."
2. **Self-concept maintenance:** People want to see themselves as honest. Cheating that remains small enough to be rationalized ("everyone does it," "it's not really stealing") preserves self-image. The fudge factor represents the limit of rationalizable dishonesty.
3. **Cash as moral anchor:** Physical money activates learned moral associations (social contract symbolism, "IN GOD WE TRUST"), constraining the fudge factor. Nonmonetary media lack these moral anchors, expanding the zone of rationalizable dishonesty.
4. **Moral reminder as superego activation:** Contemplating moral standards — religious or secular — temporarily activates the internal honesty monitor, shrinking the fudge factor to zero.

## Applications

- **Organizational design:** Place moral reminders (codes of conduct, ethics statements) at the point of temptation, not just at hiring. Ariely suggests signing ethics statements before filling out tax forms or expense reports, not after.
- **Financial regulation:** Recognize that distance from cash (stock options, digital currencies, complex financial instruments) inherently expands dishonesty. Design transparency mechanisms that reconnect actions to their monetary consequences.
- **Personal integrity:** The model suggests that the most effective strategy for honesty is not willpower but environment design — keeping moral standards salient and minimizing rationalization opportunities.
- **Policy design:** External controls (Sarbanes-Oxley, ethics regulations) have limited effectiveness because they address the rational cost-benefit model (SMORC) rather than the fudge factor. Internal moral reminders are more effective but harder to implement systemically.

## Open Questions
- How does the fudge factor interact with cultural norms? Ariely mentions countries with low trust (Iran, China, Latin America) but doesn't provide experimental cross-cultural data.
- Does the fudge factor change with repeated cheating? Does "moral licensing" expand it over time?
- How do group dynamics affect the fudge factor? Does seeing others cheat expand one's own fudge factor?
- Can the fudge factor be permanently reduced through training, or do moral reminders need to be perpetually renewed?
- What is the neural basis? If the superego monitoring model is correct, what brain regions correspond to the activation/deactivation of moral self-scrutiny?
