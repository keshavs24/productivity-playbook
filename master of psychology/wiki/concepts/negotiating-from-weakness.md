# Negotiating from Weakness

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Negotiating from weakness occurs when the other side's [[batna]] is relatively strong and yours is relatively weak, giving them disproportionate leverage. [[malhotra|Malhotra]] and [[bazerman|Bazerman]] argue that weakness is not a death sentence — it requires careful analysis, creative thinking, and a different strategic approach than negotiating from strength. The chapter provides two sets of strategies: five for negotiating *within* a weak position, and three for *shifting* the balance of power itself.

## Key Findings

- **Weakness is about relative BATNAs, not absolute resources.** A weak BATNA is not terribly problematic if the other side does not know it is weak — Roosevelt's campaign manager's position was extremely weak, but the photographer never knew. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch11-weakness]])
- **Both parties can be weak simultaneously.** When both BATNAs are weak, the [[zopa]] is large. The party that better leverages fundamentals (Ch. 1 claiming strategies) captures more of the large value at stake. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch11-weakness]])
- **Relinquishing power can paradoxically increase outcomes.** When Deepak had no leverage in a salary negotiation, he stopped trying to exert power and instead asked for help — and received a 10% raise. Others reciprocate vulnerability more readily than they reciprocate aggression. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch11-weakness]])
- **Portfolio thinking transforms weakness.** A firm with 5% margins that doubles margins to 10% but loses 49% of business is still better off. The strategy that is optimal for a single negotiation may be suboptimal across a portfolio of negotiations. ([[summary-5-17-ng-ch11-weakness]])

## Strategies for Negotiating Without Power

1. **Don't reveal that you are weak** — avoid inadvertently signaling desperation
2. **Overcome your weakness by leveraging their weakness** — investigate the other side's BATNA; it may be weaker than you think
3. **Identify and leverage your distinct value proposition (DVP)** — what distinguishes you from competitors beyond price
4. **If very weak, consider relinquishing power** — stop flexing and ask for help; vulnerability triggers reciprocity
5. **Strategize across your entire negotiation portfolio** — being willing to lose some deals strengthens your position across all deals

## Strategies for Shifting the Balance of Power

1. **Build coalitions with other weak parties** — unions, cooperatives, the Group of 21 at WTO (Iacocca's rebate war strategy)
2. **Leverage the power of your extreme weakness** — if your failure hurts them, they need you to survive (Romania at 1919 Paris Peace Conference)
3. **Understand and attack the source of their power** — Planned Parenthood's "Pledge-a-Picket" strategy; the developer who neutralized the corrupt politician's son's timing leverage

## Mechanisms

Weakness in negotiation arises from BATNA asymmetry, but power has multiple sources:

1. **Informational power:** What the other side knows (or does not know) about your alternatives
2. **Structural power:** Coalition membership, formal authority, ability to set rules of the game
3. **Creative power:** Ability to identify value-creating opportunities that change the game
4. **Relational power:** Trust, reputation, and the desire to maintain a valuable relationship

## Applications

- **Job seekers with one offer:** Don't reveal desperation; investigate the employer's hiring constraints; identify your unique DVP; if very weak, express genuine enthusiasm and ask for help
- **Small businesses negotiating with large customers:** Strategize across portfolio; build coalitions with other suppliers; educate customers on your DVP between deals
- **Litigation disadvantage:** Leverage extreme weakness ("pushing us into bankruptcy hurts you too"); attack the source of their litigation power
