# Mental Accounting

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.8

## Definition

Mental accounting is the set of cognitive operations people use to organize, evaluate, and keep track of financial activities. People maintain separate "mental accounts" for different categories of spending (vacation, renovation, monthly budget) and apply different decision rules to each account -- violating the economic principle of fungibility (money is money regardless of its source or label).

## Key Findings

- **Thaler (1999) Swiss trip:** Travel expenses felt less bothersome when coded to the same account as a speaking fee than when the fee and trip were in separate mental accounts. The same expenditure feels different depending on which "account" it comes from. ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch6]])
- **Milkman, Beshears, Rogers, & Bazerman (2008):** A $10 grocery store coupon increased total spending by $2. Rationally, a $10 windfall should not change behavior -- but it did, because it was coded into a "found money" account with different spending rules.
- **Wine problem (Shafir & Thaler, 2006):** $20 bottle now worth $75 -- what does it cost to drink? 30% said $0 (expensed away), 18% said $20 (purchase price), 25% said -$55 (transaction gain), 20% said $75 (correct opportunity cost). Only economists consistently chose the rational answer.
- **IRS problem:** Two $100 losses from separate accounts (federal + state) feel worse than one $200 loss from a single account, due to diminishing sensitivity in the [[value-function]]
- **Transactional utility (Thaler, 1985):** People evaluate transactions through both acquisition utility (objective value) and transactional utility (deal quality vs. expectations). A beer at a resort hotel ($2.65 acceptable) vs. grocery store ($1.50 acceptable) -- same beer, same beach consumption, different price expectations create different mental accounts.

## Mechanisms

Mental accounting derives from [[prospect-theory]]'s value function:

**Hedonic editing rules** (how to maximize subjective well-being given the value function's shape):
1. **Segregate gains:** Multiple small gains > one large gain (concavity means first dollars of each gain are most valued). Give gifts over time, not all at once.
2. **Aggregate losses:** One large loss < multiple small losses (convexity means first dollars of each loss hurt most). Pay bills in a lump sum when possible.
3. **Integrate small losses with larger gains:** The loss is absorbed by the gain. Cancel a small fee against a larger benefit.
4. **Segregate small gains from larger losses:** The "silver lining" principle. A small refund during a costly move still provides joy.

The **annoying-losses fund:** Thaler's colleague who sets aside money at the start of the year for parking tickets and library fines, reducing irritation. At year's end, remaining balance goes to charity.

## Applications

- **Personal finance:** Understanding mental accounting can prevent irrational spending triggered by "found money" (tax refunds, bonuses, coupons) and improve saving discipline
- **Marketing/pricing:** Frame price components to match favorable mental accounts. "Shipping included" vs. "$5 shipping" creates different account entries
- **Compensation design:** Annual bonuses (segregated gain) feel better than equivalent salary increases (merged with existing account). Multiple small raises feel better than one large raise.
- **Government policy:** "Bonus" framing produces dramatically more spending than "rebate" framing for identical payments ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch6]])
- **Gift-giving:** Segregate gifts over time rather than giving many at once

## CVF Collection Extensions (Chs. 1, 14)

Thaler's definitive mental accounting paper ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch14-mental-accounting-matters]]) provides the comprehensive treatment, and Kahneman & Tversky's overview ([[summary-2-07-cvf-ch1-choices-values-frames]]) introduces mental accounting to riskless choice:

- **Three components of mental accounting:** (1) How outcomes are perceived and experienced (transaction utility, [[hedonic-editing]]); (2) How activities are assigned to accounts (budget categories, account boundaries); (3) How often accounts are evaluated (choice bracketing, evaluation frequency).
- **[[transactional-utility]] formalized:** Every purchase produces acquisition utility (value of good vs. price) and transaction utility (price vs. reference price). The beer-on-the-beach example: same beer, same beach consumption, but people pay $2.65 from a resort hotel vs. $1.50 from a grocery store.
- **Payment depreciation:** The pain of a purchase fades over time, making prepayment psychologically attractive and explaining why season tickets feel "free" by mid-season.
- **[[myopic-loss-aversion]]:** The combination of loss aversion and narrow bracketing (frequent evaluation) explains the equity premium puzzle. Daily portfolio evaluation makes stock volatility painfully salient; annual evaluation does not.
- **The behavioral life-cycle hypothesis (Shefrin & Thaler, 1988):** Three mental accounts with different marginal propensities to consume: current income (highest MPC), current assets (medium), future income (lowest).
- **Self-control function:** Mental accounts serve as pre-commitment devices -- budget categories prevent overspending, income labeling constrains spending, and designated savings accounts exploit non-fungibility for beneficial outcomes.
- **The calculator/jacket problem (Ch. 1):** 68% drive to save $5 on a $15 calculator but only 29% drive to save $5 on a $125 calculator -- topical accounts make the savings feel different despite being identical in absolute terms.
