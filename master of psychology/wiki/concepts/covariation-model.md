# Covariation Model

**Phase(s):** 1
**Confidence:** 0.6

## Definition

The covariation model (Kelley, 1967) is a systematic framework for how people determine whether to make an internal (dispositional) or external (situational) attribution for someone's behavior. The model proposes that people act like "naive scientists," examining how a behavior covaries across three dimensions -- consensus, distinctiveness, and consistency -- to infer its cause.

## Key Findings

### The Three Dimensions

- **Consensus:** Do other people behave the same way toward this stimulus? (High consensus = everyone reacts this way; low consensus = only this person reacts this way)
- **Distinctiveness:** Does this person behave this way toward other stimuli, or only this one? (High distinctiveness = only toward this stimulus; low distinctiveness = toward many stimuli)
- **Consistency:** Does this person behave this way toward this stimulus across different times and situations? (High consistency = every time; low consistency = rarely)

### Attribution Patterns

**Internal (dispositional) attribution** when:
- **Low consensus** -- only this person behaves this way
- **Low distinctiveness** -- this person acts this way with everyone/everything
- **High consistency** -- this person does it every time

Example: "John laughs at the comedian. Nobody else laughs (low consensus). John laughs at everything (low distinctiveness). John always laughs at this comedian (high consistency)." Conclusion: John is easily amused (internal attribution).

**External (situational) attribution** when:
- **High consensus** -- everyone reacts this way
- **High distinctiveness** -- only to this particular stimulus
- **High consistency** -- every time

Example: "John laughs at the comedian. Everyone laughs at the comedian (high consensus). John doesn't laugh at other comedians (high distinctiveness). John always laughs at this comedian (high consistency)." Conclusion: The comedian is genuinely funny (external attribution).

### Limitations

- People **underuse consensus information** -- they give it less weight than the model predicts they should, relying more heavily on distinctiveness and consistency
- People don't always have access to all three dimensions -- often we observe behavior without knowing how others react (consensus) or how the person behaves in other situations (distinctiveness)
- The model describes an ideal rational process; in practice, people often skip this analysis entirely and default to dispositional attributions via the [[fundamental-attribution-error]]

## Mechanisms

1. **Covariation principle:** People attribute an effect to a cause that is present when the effect is present and absent when the effect is absent -- a basic causal reasoning strategy
2. **Multiple observation integration:** The model requires aggregating observations across people (consensus), stimuli (distinctiveness), and time (consistency) -- a cognitively demanding process
3. **Interaction with the FAE:** Gilbert's (1989) two-step attribution model suggests that Step 1 (automatic internal attribution) occurs before any covariation analysis. The covariation model describes Step 2 -- the effortful, controlled adjustment process that people often fail to complete, especially under cognitive load

## Applications

- **Performance evaluation:** Understanding whether an employee's poor performance reflects their ability (internal) or their circumstances (external) requires assessing all three dimensions -- but managers often skip consensus and distinctiveness data
- **Relationship attributions:** Happy couples make external attributions for partner's negative behavior (high distinctiveness: "she only snaps when stressed") and internal attributions for positive behavior; distressed couples do the opposite
- **Legal reasoning:** Jurors implicitly use covariation logic when assessing whether a defendant's behavior was characteristic (low distinctiveness, high consistency) or situationally driven (high distinctiveness)

## Open Questions

- How do people handle missing information? When only one or two of the three dimensions are available, which heuristics substitute for the missing data?
- Does training in the covariation model reduce the [[fundamental-attribution-error]]? If people learned to systematically check consensus, distinctiveness, and consistency, would they make more accurate attributions?
- How does cultural context affect which dimensions receive the most weight? Collectivistic cultures (which favor situational attributions) may naturally weight consensus and distinctiveness more heavily than individualistic cultures.
