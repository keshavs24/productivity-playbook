# Ego Involvement

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Ego involvement is an intrapersonal motivational state in which a person's self-esteem or sense of worth becomes contingent on the outcome of a performance. Rather than engaging in an activity out of interest or enjoyment (task involvement), the ego-involved person engages to prove their worth to themselves or others. Within [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]], ego involvement represents an **internally controlling** state that undermines [[intrinsic-motivation]] even in the absence of external pressures.

Three definitions of ego involvement exist in the literature (Greenwald, 1982):
1. Striving based in threats to esteem **by others** -- one's ego is on the line regarding others' evaluations
2. Ego on the line regarding **self-evaluation** -- maintaining self-worth by demonstrating high competence relative to others
3. Personal importance (without evaluative pressure) -- investing in an activity because it matters personally

SDT is primarily concerned with definitions 1 and 2, where ego involvement creates internal pressure to prove worth. The third definition is closer to identified regulation and is not inherently controlling.

The contrast is **task involvement**: being interested in the activity for its own sake, focused on learning, mastery, and enjoyment rather than proving worth.

## Key Findings

### Ego Involvement Undermines Intrinsic Motivation
- **Ryan (1982):** Participants introduced to hidden-figures puzzles as a "test of creative intelligence" (ego-involving) vs. "experimental puzzle task" (task-involving). All received positive feedback. Ego-involved participants showed **significantly less** intrinsic motivation despite feeling competent. Self-worth satisfaction fulfilled the extrinsic goal, leaving no intrinsic reason to persist
- **Butler (1987); Koestner, Zuckerman, & Koestner (1987); Plant & Ryan (1985):** All replicated the negative effect of ego involvement on intrinsic motivation
- **Ryan, Connell, & Plant (1990):** Ego-involved readers showed less comprehension and recall than task-involved readers

### Distinguishing Intrinsic from Ego-Involved Persistence
- **Anderson & Rodin (1989):** Ego-involved participants given nonconfirming feedback showed **greater** persistence than those given positive feedback -- but this was pressured persistence aimed at restoring self-worth, not intrinsic interest
- **Baumeister & Tice (1985):** Low-self-esteem participants given humiliating feedback showed the highest persistence -- desperate self-worth salvaging, not intrinsic motivation
- **Ryan, Koestner, & Deci (1991):** Critical diagnostic -- under task involvement, persistence correlated positively with self-reported interest/enjoyment. Under ego involvement, persistence was **uncorrelated** with interest. Behavioral persistence alone cannot distinguish intrinsic from controlled motivation

### Ego-Involved Competition
- **Reeve & Deci (1996):** After competition, losers persevered on old problems (internally controlled -- trying to prove themselves) but did not explore new ones. Winners explored new problems (intrinsic motivation). Losing decreased intrinsic motivation and prompted internally controlled persistence

### Meta-Analytic Evidence
- **Rawsthorne & Elliot (1999):** Meta-analysis of 23 experiments confirmed ego involvement and performance goals led to significantly less intrinsic motivation than task involvement and learning goals. Under ego involvement, confirming feedback reduced persistence (goal achieved, no need to continue); nonconfirming feedback increased controlled persistence

### Ego Involvement and Objective Self-Awareness
- **Plant & Ryan (1985):** Placing participants in front of a camera or mirror (inducing public self-consciousness/objective self-awareness) decreased intrinsic motivation -- self-objectification creates an internally evaluative, controlling stance

### Ego Involvement and Performance Quality
- **Standage, Treasure, Hooper, & Kuczka (2007):** Ego-involved participants showed greater self-handicapping before performance
- **Bober & Grolnick (1995):** Ego-involved individuals showed less self-consistency in self-evaluations when given counter-schematic feedback

## Mechanisms

1. **Internal control:** Ego involvement converts the motivation from "I want to do this" to "I have to do well to feel worthy." This is experienced as an internal pressure -- the person becomes their own controller
2. **PLOC shift:** Despite being internal, ego involvement creates an external perceived locus of causality. The person acts not from genuine interest but from pressure to maintain self-esteem
3. **Conditional self-worth:** Self-esteem becomes contingent on performance outcomes, creating anxiety and pressure that conflict with the relaxed, interested engagement of intrinsic motivation
4. **Internalization of controls:** Ego involvement often derives from social environments of conditional regard -- internalized standards of worth initially imposed by others
5. **Narrowed processing:** Under ego involvement, people adopt a rigid focus on proving competence rather than the flexible, exploratory approach that characterizes intrinsic motivation

## Applications

- **Education:** Frame tasks as opportunities for learning rather than tests of ability. De-emphasize grades and rankings. Avoid language that ties performance to personal worth ("This will show how smart you are")
- **Sport:** Distinguish between ego-oriented goals (winning, beating others) and task-oriented goals (improving, mastering skills). Ego-oriented goals are associated with aggression, cheating, and poor sportsmanship (Donahue et al., 2006; Vallerand & Losier, 1994)
- **Workplace:** Avoid cultures of conditional regard where worth is tied to metrics. Promote learning goals over performance goals
- **Personal development:** Recognize self-pressuring patterns ("I have to," "I should," "I must") as internally controlling. Cultivate task involvement and intrinsic interest

## Open Questions

- How does social media (with its constant social comparison) affect baseline levels of ego involvement?
- Is some degree of ego involvement useful for motivation in competitive contexts, or is task involvement always superior?
