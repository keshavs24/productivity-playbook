# Invent Options for Mutual Gain

**Phase(s):** 5
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

"Invent options for mutual gain" is the third principle of [[principled-negotiation]]. It prescribes that before trying to reach agreement, negotiators should set aside a designated time to think up a wide range of possible solutions that advance shared interests and creatively reconcile differing interests. The key prescription is to **separate inventing from deciding** — generate first, evaluate later.

## Key Findings

- **Four obstacles inhibit creative option generation:** (1) premature judgment, (2) searching for the single answer, (3) the assumption of a [[fixed-pie-bias|fixed pie]], (4) thinking that "solving their problem is their problem." ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch4-options]])
- **The Circle Chart provides a systematic method for broadening options.** Four types of thinking: Problem (what's wrong in the real world) → Analysis (diagnose in theory) → Approaches (general prescriptions) → Action Ideas (specific feasible steps). Shuttling between specific and general multiplies options. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch4-options]])
- **Differences between parties create value.** The Jack Sprat principle: differences in interests, beliefs, time values, forecasts, and risk aversion are the raw material for dovetailing — creating agreements that give each side what it values most at low cost to the other. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch4-options]], [[value-creation-in-negotiation]])
- **Agreements can vary along multiple dimensions of strength:** substantive/procedural, permanent/provisional, comprehensive/partial, final/in-principle, binding/nonbinding, first-order/second-order. When a full agreement is impossible, a weaker version may be reachable. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch4-options]])
- **Making the other side's decision easy is the ultimate test of a good option.** Write a "yesable proposition" — a proposal to which the other side can respond with a single word: yes. ([[summary-5-16-gty-ch4-options]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Overcoming [[fixed-pie-bias]]:** The fixed-pie assumption is the single greatest barrier to creative negotiation. By explicitly looking for shared interests and ways to dovetail differing interests, negotiators discover that the pie is almost never truly fixed.
2. **Leveraging differences:** Paradoxically, differences between parties are not obstacles but opportunities. Different valuations of time, risk, or outcomes allow trades that make both sides better off — the essence of [[value-creation-in-negotiation]].
3. **Brainstorming bypasses premature judgment:** The no-criticism rule of brainstorming prevents System 2 (analytical/critical thinking) from squelching System 1 (creative/associative thinking) before options have been fully generated.
4. **The Circle Chart exploits the specific-to-general ladder:** Moving from a specific problem to a general diagnosis to a general approach to a specific action generates more and better options than staying at any single level of abstraction.

## Applications

- **India-Pakistan Indus River dispute:** Deadlocked on water-sharing positions until the scope was enlarged to include new irrigation projects, storage dams, and engineering works — creating enough value for both sides.
- **Townsend Oil and Pageville:** A tax dispute became a shared economic development project when shared interests in industrial expansion were identified.
- **Baseball salary negotiations:** Different forecasts about a player's future performance allow contingent contracts (base salary plus performance bonuses) that satisfy both risk-averse owners and confident players.
- **Brainstorming in labor-management:** The coal mine example shows facilitators using the no-criticism rule to generate creative options (joint training programs, union softball teams, picnics) that no single side would have proposed.

## Open Questions
- How effective is brainstorming with the other side when there is significant distrust? Fisher and Ury acknowledge the risks (accidental disclosure, premature commitment) but argue the benefits usually outweigh them.
- Does the "separate inventing from deciding" principle apply equally in time-pressured negotiations?
