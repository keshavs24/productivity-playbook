# Reciprocation

**Phase(s):** 1, 2, 5, 6
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The rule of reciprocation states that we should try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us. It creates a sense of future obligation that accompanies the receipt of favors, gifts, invitations, and concessions. It is one of the most potent and pervasive weapons of social influence, present in every human culture studied.

## Key Findings

- **Universal**: Gouldner (1960) found that all human societies subscribe to the reciprocity rule. Leakey and Lewin (1978) argue it is what makes us human — it enabled the first non-zero-sum exchanges.
- **Overpowers liking**: Regan (1971) — subjects who received an unsolicited Coke from "Joe" bought 2x as many raffle tickets. Critically, for those who received the Coke, *liking Joe had no effect on compliance*. Subjects who disliked Joe bought just as many tickets.
- **Enforces uninvited debts**: The American Disabled Veterans saw response rates nearly double (18% → 35%) by including unsolicited personalized address labels. You don't need to ask for a gift to feel obligated to reciprocate it (Paese & Gilin, 2000).
- **Triggers unequal exchanges**: A 10¢ Coke produced 50¢ in raffle ticket purchases (500% ROI). A jump-start led to a car loan that was totaled. The discomfort of indebtedness is so strong that people will give more than they received just to be free of it.
- **Long-lived**: Ethiopia sent $5,000 in relief aid to Mexico in 1985 — reciprocating aid Mexico sent during Italy's invasion of Ethiopia in *1935*. The Netherlands' extraordinary Katrina response was reciprocating New Orleans' 1953 flood aid.
- **Three obligations** (Mauss, 1954): To give, to receive, and to repay. The obligation to *receive* is what makes the rule exploitable — you can't easily refuse an unsolicited gift.

## Mechanisms

The rule serves a critical evolutionary function: it enabled the development of reciprocal relationships, division of labor, and exchange systems. A shared sense of future obligation means resources can be given without being lost, lowering inhibitions against initiating exchange.

Two forces drive repayment:
1. **Internal discomfort**: Indebtedness is deeply unpleasant. We are conditioned from childhood to chafe under obligation.
2. **External sanctions**: Violators are labeled moochers, ingrates, freeloaders — and scrupulously shunned (Wedekind & Milinski, 2000). Even those who give *without allowing repayment* are disliked (Gergen et al., 1975).

The rule has two main applications:
1. **Direct reciprocation**: Give a gift/favor first, then make your request (Hare Krishna flower → donation; Amway BUG; free samples; restaurant candy)
2. **Reciprocal concessions**: Make a concession (retreat from a larger request) to trigger a return concession → [[rejection-then-retreat]]

## Applications

- **Sales**: Free samples, gifts with purchase, "free" inspections/consultations, the Amway BUG
- **Fundraising**: Unsolicited gifts with donation requests (address labels, greeting cards, flowers)
- **Politics**: Campaign contributions → legislative favors (7x voting influence per AP study); LBJ's legislative success built on years of favor-stockpiling
- **Negotiation**: Opening high to create room for concessions (Phase 5)
- **Personal relationships**: Small consistent favors build obligation over time (the Oregon employee stuck at her job because her boss gave holiday gifts)

## Open Questions

- How does reciprocation interact with digital/anonymous contexts (e.g., freemium software, content creators)?
- Is there a reliable decay rate for reciprocal obligation? Small favors fade with time (Burger et al., 1997; Flynn, 2002), but notable gifts can persist for decades (Ethiopia/Mexico).
- How do communal vs. exchange relationships moderate the rule? (Clark & Mills, 1979 suggest communal relationships suppress strict accounting)
