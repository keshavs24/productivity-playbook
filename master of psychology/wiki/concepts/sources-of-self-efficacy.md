# Sources of Self-Efficacy

**Phase(s):** 3
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Self-efficacy beliefs are constructed from four principal sources of information (Bandura, 1997, Ch.3, pp.79-115). These four sources provide the raw data from which efficacy judgments are constructed, but information from any source is not inherently enlightening -- it becomes instructive only through cognitive processing and reflective thought. A distinction must be drawn between information conveyed by experienced events and information as selected, weighted, and integrated into self-efficacy judgments.

The four sources, in order of influence:

1. **[[mastery-experience]]** (enactive mastery experiences) -- the most powerful source
2. **[[vicarious-experience]]** (modeling and social comparison)
3. **[[verbal-persuasion]]** (social persuasion and evaluative feedback)
4. **[[physiological-and-affective-states]]** (somatic and emotional indicators)

## Key Findings

- The four sources rarely operate in isolation -- people simultaneously experience their own performances, observe others, receive social evaluations, and read their physiological states
- Each mode of conveying information about personal capabilities has its distinctive set of efficacy indicators -- the array of factors selected provides the information base upon which the self-appraisal process operates
- The cognitive processing of efficacy information involves two separable functions: (a) what types of information people attend to and use as indicators of personal efficacy, and (b) the combination rules or heuristics people use to weight and integrate information from different sources
- Any given influence, depending on its form, may operate through one or more of these sources of efficacy information
- Personal, social, and situational factors affect how direct and socially mediated experiences are cognitively interpreted
- A host of factors moderate the impact of each source: preexisting self-schemata, task difficulty, effort expenditure, selective self-monitoring, performance similarity, attribute similarity, model competence, persuader credibility, construal biases, mood states ([[summary-3-11-se-ch3-sources]])

## Mechanisms

The four sources operate through a common cognitive processing pathway:

1. **Information selection:** Each source generates a distinctive set of efficacy indicators that people selectively attend to
2. **Cognitive interpretation:** Raw information is filtered through preexisting self-schemata, creating confirmatory biases -- information consistent with existing beliefs is preferentially noticed and remembered; inconsistent information is minimized or forgotten
3. **Integration:** People use combination rules (additive, relative weighting, multiplicative, or configural) to weight and integrate information from different sources into overall efficacy judgments
4. **Bias vulnerability:** Under emotional states, people are particularly poor at weighting and integrating multidimensional information, tending to rely on a few salient factors and simplified heuristics (connects to [[bounded-rationality]], [[availability]])

The sources interact bidirectionally with each other and with efficacy beliefs themselves -- efficacy beliefs are both products and constructors of experiences.

## Applications

- **Intervention design:** The most effective interventions use multiple sources simultaneously -- [[mastery-experience]] as the foundation, supplemented by [[vicarious-experience]] (modeling), [[verbal-persuasion]] (feedback), and management of [[physiological-and-affective-states]]
- **Coaching and mentoring:** Skilled efficacy builders structure activities for initial success, provide competent models, frame feedback in terms of gains rather than shortfalls, and teach people to read their arousal as energizing rather than debilitating
- **Self-development:** Understanding the four sources allows individuals to strategically seek out efficacy-building experiences across all modalities
- **Assessment:** When diagnosing why someone has low efficacy, identifying which sources are deficient (no mastery experiences? poor models? undermining feedback? anxiety-driven somatic cues?) guides the intervention
- **Organizational training:** The three-element mastery modeling method (instructive modeling, guided practice, self-directed transfer) is the most effective approach for building occupational competencies -- consistently outperforming lecture-based training and computerized tutorials (Ch.10, pp.440-445) ([[summary-3-11-se-ch10-organizational-functioning]])
- **Academic domain:** Coping peer models boost [[academic-self-efficacy]] more than mastery models (Schunk & Hanson, 1985); verbal self-guidance (metacognitive strategy verbalization) enhances efficacy and performance (Schunk, 1982b) ([[summary-3-11-se-ch6-cognitive-functioning]])
- **Collective enablement:** At the group level, all four sources operate through similar mechanisms -- vicarious experience, positive/negative feedback, and group performance all predicted [[collective-efficacy]] and group performance in experimental demonstrations (Prussia & Kinicki, 1996) ([[summary-3-11-se-ch11-collective-efficacy]])

## Open Questions
- How do the relative weights of the four sources shift across different domains (e.g., academic vs. athletic vs. social efficacy)?
- How do the sources interact over the lifespan -- does the relative importance of vicarious experience decrease as mastery experience accumulates?
- Can digital/virtual experiences (VR, simulations) serve as a fifth source or a variant of vicarious experience?
- How does the integration of efficacy information from multiple sources relate to dual-process models of cognition ([[system-1-system-2]])?
