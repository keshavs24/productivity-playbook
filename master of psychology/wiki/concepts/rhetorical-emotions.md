# Rhetorical Emotions

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

Rhetorical emotions (*pathe*) are the systematic catalog of human emotions analyzed by [[aristotle]] in [[on-rhetoric]] Book 2, chapters 2-11. This is the earliest known systematic psychology of emotions in Western thought. Aristotle defines emotions as "those things through which, by undergoing change, people come to differ in their judgments, and which are accompanied by pain and pleasure" (2.1.8).

For each emotion, Aristotle provides a structured analysis of:
1. **Definition** — what the emotion essentially is
2. **State of mind** — what mental conditions make a person susceptible to feeling it
3. **Targets** — toward whom the emotion is typically directed
4. **Causes** — what specific triggers produce it

The emotions are arranged in chiastically ordered pairs (positive/negative, then negative/positive): anger/calmness, friendliness/enmity, fear/confidence, shame/shamelessness, kindliness/unkindliness, pity/indignation, envy/emulation.

## Key Findings

### The Ten Emotion-Pairs

| Emotion | Opposite | Definition (Aristotle) | Core Trigger |
|---------|----------|----------------------|--------------|
| **Anger** (*orge*) | Calmness (*praotes*) | Desire for apparent retaliation for an apparent slight | Perceived belittling — contempt, spite, or insult (*hybris*) |
| **Calmness** | Anger | Settling down and quieting of anger | Recognition that the slight was unintentional, or that the offender repents |
| **Friendliness** (*philia*) | Enmity (*echthra*) | Wanting good things for someone for their sake | Shared pleasures, shared enemies, benefaction, lack of reproachfulness |
| **Enmity/Hatred** | Friendliness | Wanting the destruction of a detested type | Perception that someone belongs to a hated class (thieves, sycophants) |
| **Fear** (*phobos*) | Confidence (*tharsos*) | Pain from imagining a future destructive evil | Proximity of danger; power of potential harmer; one's own vulnerability |
| **Confidence** | Fear | Hope of safety, accompanied by imagining that dangerous things are far off | Past success, resources, allies, lack of wrongdoing |
| **Shame** (*aiskhyne*) | Shamelessness | Pain concerning evils that seem to bring one into disrespect | Signs of cowardice, injustice, licentiousness; visibility before those one respects |
| **Shamelessness** | Shame | Belittling about the same things that cause shame | Indifference to others' opinions |
| **Kindliness** (*kharis*) | Unkindliness | Feeling of benevolence that moves one to do a service gratuitously | Recognition of another's great need; opportune timing of the service |
| **Pity** (*eleos*) | Indignation (*nemesis*) | Pain at apparently undeserved destructive evil happening to another | Proximity of suffering; identification with the sufferer; belief it could happen to oneself |
| **Indignation** | Pity | Pain at apparently undeserved *good* fortune of another | Sense that outcomes should match desert; newly rich cause more indignation than old wealth |
| **Envy** (*phthonos*) | Emulation (*zelos*) | Distress at apparent success of one's peers — not to get the good, but because they have it | Proximity in time, place, age, reputation to the envied person |
| **Emulation** | Envy (contempt) | Distress at lacking things honored and possible to acquire, motivating effort to attain them | Presence of honored goods among similar others; belief the goods are attainable |

### Key Psychological Insights

1. **Anger vs. hatred:** Anger is personal (directed at individuals), painful, temporary, and seeks the target's suffering. Hatred is categorical (directed at types — "all thieves"), painless, permanent, and seeks the target's destruction. "One who is angry wants the one he is angry at to suffer; one who hates does not care" (2.4.31). This distinction anticipates the [[fundamental-attribution-error]] — the tendency to attribute behavior to types rather than individuals.

2. **Fear requires proximity:** "All know they will die, but because that is not near at hand they take no thought of it" (2.5.1). Fear is only felt when the danger seems close. This has implications for [[framing-effects]] — making threats vivid and proximate is necessary to arouse fear.

3. **Fear motivates deliberation; hopelessness paralyzes:** "No one deliberates about hopeless things" (2.5.14). For fear to be rhetorically useful, it must leave room for hope that action can avert the danger.

4. **Shame is socially constructed:** People feel shame before those whose opinion they value — admirers, rivals, the wise — not universally. "Shame is in the eyes" (2.6.18). This is purely relational, not internal guilt.

5. **Pity requires identification:** People pity those who are like themselves — similar in age, character, habits, rank. The pitiable must seem undeserving of their suffering. Pity is destroyed when the suffering seems deserved (which triggers indignation instead).

6. **Envy vs. emulation:** Both arise from comparing oneself with near-equals who have desirable things. The difference is moral: emulation motivates self-improvement ("I want to attain what they have"), while envy motivates destruction ("I want them not to have it"). "Emulation is a good thing and characteristic of good people, while envy is bad and characteristic of the bad" (2.11.1).

7. **Emotions are cognitive, not merely physiological:** Every emotion in Aristotle's account involves a judgment or appraisal — anger involves perceiving a slight, fear involves imagining a future harm, pity involves judging suffering as undeserved. This aligns with modern appraisal theories of emotion.

## Mechanisms

Emotions alter rhetorical judgment through two pathways:

1. **Dispositional framing:** Emotions create a mental frame that filters how new information is processed. An angry audience applies harsher standards; a sympathetic audience applies more lenient ones. "We do not give the same judgment when grieved and rejoicing" (1.2.5).

2. **Motivational direction:** Emotions motivate specific actions — anger motivates retaliation, fear motivates caution, pity motivates mercy, shame motivates conformity. A speaker who arouses the right emotion orients the audience toward the desired action.

## Applications

- **Arousing emotions:** For each emotion, present the specific triggers Aristotle identifies — to arouse anger, show the audience they have been belittled; to arouse fear, make the danger seem near and the audience vulnerable; to arouse pity, present suffering that seems undeserved and that the audience could imagine experiencing
- **Calming emotions:** Equally important — show that the slight was unintentional (calms anger), that danger is remote (calms fear), that outcomes are deserved (counters pity)
- **Audience analysis:** Different character types (young, old, wealthy, powerful) are susceptible to different emotions — the young to anger and shame, the old to fear and pity, the wealthy to contempt
- **Emotional intelligence:** Understanding Aristotle's emotion definitions provides a diagnostic vocabulary for identifying emotions in oneself and others — cf. [[emotional-intelligence]]

## Open Questions

- Aristotle's emotion chapters provide no examples of rhetorical application — they describe the psychology but not the technique. How should a speaker actually integrate emotional appeals into an enthymematic argument?
- How do Aristotle's emotion definitions compare with modern emotion taxonomies (Ekman's basic emotions, Plutchik's wheel)? Aristotle's list is more socially oriented — most of his emotions are relational (directed at specific others) rather than purely internal states.
- The relationship between Aristotle's *pathe* and [[emotional-intelligence]] (Salovey & Mayer): both emphasize understanding emotions as a foundation for effective social action, but EI focuses on self-awareness while Aristotle focuses on audience-awareness.
