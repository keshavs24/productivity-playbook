# Three Genres of Rhetoric

**Phase(s):** 4
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

[[Aristotle]] divides rhetoric into three species (*eide*) based on the role of the audience, the time orientation, and the end goal of the speech ([[on-rhetoric]] 1.3):

| Genre | Greek Term | Audience Role | Time | End (*Telos*) | Modern Examples |
|-------|-----------|---------------|------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Deliberative** | *Symbouleutikon* | Judge of future action | Future | The advantageous (*sympheron*) | Political speeches, policy debates, board presentations, sermons |
| **Judicial (Forensic)** | *Dikanikon* | Judge of past action | Past | The just (*dikaion*) | Courtroom speeches, apologies, investigations, accountability hearings |
| **Epideictic (Demonstrative)** | *Epideiktikon* | Observer | Present | The honorable (*kalon*) | Funeral orations, award speeches, commencement addresses, eulogies |

## Key Findings

- The division is exhaustive — every speech situation falls into one of these three categories based on the audience's relationship to the subject (1.3.1-3)
- Each genre has its own pair of functions: deliberative = exhortation/dissuasion; judicial = accusation/defense; epideictic = praise/blame
- Each genre's audience makes a different kind of judgment: deliberative audiences judge whether to act; judicial audiences judge whether someone acted justly; epideictic audiences judge the quality of the speaker and subject
- **Deliberative rhetoric** is the "finest" form (1.1.10) — it deals with the most important matters (public policy, war, legislation) and is hardest because the future is uncertain
- **Judicial rhetoric** was the focus of the early handbooks, which Aristotle criticizes for neglecting deliberative and epideictic (1.1.3-10)
- **Epideictic rhetoric** is the most problematic category — Aristotle thinks of it primarily as funeral oratory and praise of individuals, but it has become a catch-all for discourse that is neither specifically deliberative nor judicial (Kennedy intro, p. 22)
- The genres can overlap: a deliberative speech may draw on epideictic techniques (praise of past heroes); a judicial speech may use deliberative arguments (what would be best for the city)
- Praise (epideictic) and advice (deliberative) share a common species — changing the form of expression converts one into the other (1.9.35-36)

## Mechanisms

The three genres work as an analytical framework because every communicative situation involves:
1. A **speaker** making a case
2. An **audience** whose relationship to the subject determines what kind of judgment they will make
3. A **subject** that is oriented to a particular time (past, present, or future)

The genre determines which topics, proofs, and emotional appeals are most appropriate — what is "useful" in deliberation differs from what is "useful" in prosecution or praise.

## Applications

- **Speech preparation:** Identify which genre your situation calls for before selecting arguments. A pitch to investors is deliberative (future action); a performance review is partly judicial (past action) and partly epideictic (character assessment).
- **Argument selection:** Each genre has specific topoi — use political/ethical topics for deliberative, justice/injustice topics for judicial, virtue/honor topics for epideictic
- **Emotional calibration:** Different emotions serve different genres — fear and confidence for deliberative; anger and pity for judicial; admiration and emulation for epideictic
- **Modern application:** Most real-world persuasion involves genre-mixing. Understanding the three pure types helps diagnose which mode of argument is most effective at each moment.

## Open Questions

- Aristotle's concept of epideictic is notably narrow — limited to praise and blame of individuals. It does not adequately account for religious discourse, cultural transmission, advertising, or brand communication, all of which function as modern epideictic.
- How do the three genres map onto modern communication contexts? Social media posts, for instance, often function as epideictic (displaying values and identity) even when nominally deliberative or informational.
