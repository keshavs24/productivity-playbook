# Iowa Gambling Task

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Iowa gambling task (Bechara, Damasio, Damasio, & Anderson, 1994) is an experimental paradigm designed to study decision making under uncertainty. Participants draw cards from four decks: "bad" decks (A and B) offer high rewards ($100/card) but catastrophic penalties (-$1,250), producing a net loss of $250 per 10 cards; "good" decks (C and D) offer modest rewards ($50/card) with small penalties (-$250), producing a net gain of $250 per 10 cards. The task reveals the critical role of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex in learning from emotional feedback to guide advantageous decisions.

## Key Findings

- Normal participants are initially attracted to the high-reward bad decks but eventually learn to avoid them -- they develop "gut feelings" (galvanic skin responses) warning them before choosing from dangerous decks ([[summary-2-09-anderson-ch11]])
- Patients with ventromedial prefrontal cortex damage keep choosing from the high-reward/high-penalty decks and do not show galvanic skin responses when choosing from dangerous decks -- they fail to develop the emotional signals that guide healthy decision making
- This finding connects to the Phineas Gage case: damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex impairs the ability to integrate emotional feedback into decision making, producing choices that are "against one's best interest"
- The task demonstrates the dissociation between two neural systems:
  - **Nucleus accumbens** (basal ganglia): dopamine neurons encode reward magnitude (responds differently to $5 vs. $1 rewards but not to probability differences)
  - **Ventromedial prefrontal cortex**: integrates probability with magnitude -- the key region for weighing risks against rewards

## Mechanisms

The ventromedial prefrontal cortex integrates emotional/somatic feedback (the "somatic marker hypothesis" -- Damasio, 1994) with deliberative analysis to guide decision making. When this region is damaged, patients can still reason about probabilities abstractly but cannot learn from the emotional consequences of their choices. Dopamine neurons in the nucleus accumbens encode **reward prediction errors** (Schultz, 1998): they fire more when reward exceeds expectation and less when reward falls short. This signal drives reinforcement learning but operates independently of probability evaluation, which depends on the prefrontal cortex.

## Applications

- **Understanding impulsive behavior:** The Iowa gambling task framework helps explain why some individuals persistently make choices against their long-term interest -- their emotional feedback system may be impaired or overridden
- **Addiction research:** Substance abuse is associated with deficits on the Iowa gambling task, consistent with ventromedial prefrontal cortex dysfunction
- **Adolescent decision making:** Anderson notes that the ventromedial prefrontal cortex continues to mature into the early 20s, which may explain why adolescents are more prone to risky decisions (Reyna & Farley, 2006)
