# Rule of Three

**Phase(s):** 5 — Negotiation
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition

The Rule of Three is a negotiation technique in which you get the other party to agree to the same thing three times in the same conversation, using three different methods. It triples the strength of whatever dynamic you're reinforcing and uncovers problems before they happen. It is designed to distinguish genuine commitment from counterfeit agreement, because it is "really hard to repeatedly lie or fake conviction" ([[chris-voss]]).

## Key Findings

- **Three kinds of "Yes"** exist in negotiation: Commitment (genuine buy-in), Confirmation (reflexive agreement), and Counterfeit (saying "yes" to escape or manipulate). The Rule of Three specifically targets detecting and preventing Counterfeit Yes ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])
- **Vary your tactics across the three confirmations** to avoid sounding like a broken record ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]]):
  1. **First**: The initial agreement or commitment
  2. **Second**: A label or summary that elicits "That's right" — e.g., summarize what they said and get them to affirm it
  3. **Third**: A [[calibrated-questions|calibrated question]] about implementation — e.g., "What do we do if we get off track?"
- **It's really hard to repeatedly lie or fake conviction** — the three-times test exploits this cognitive difficulty ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])

## Mechanisms

1. **Cognitive load of deception**: Maintaining a consistent lie across three different framings of the same question requires significant mental effort. Incongruence tends to surface.
2. **Behavioral consistency pressure**: Each agreement creates a small commitment, and the accumulation of three commitments makes backing out psychologically harder (related to [[commitment-and-consistency]])
3. **Incongruence detection**: When words don't match tone or body language across three attempts, the [[seven-thirty-eight-fifty-five-rule]] helps you spot the mismatch
4. **Proactive problem discovery**: By asking about implementation on the third pass, you uncover practical obstacles, hidden stakeholders, and potential deal-killers before they become crises

## Warning Signs of Fake Buy-In

Two phrases that signal the counterpart hasn't truly committed:
- **"You're right"** (not "That's right") — means they're not vested in the agreement, just trying to get you to stop talking
- **"I'll try"** — really means "I plan to fail." When you hear this, dive back in with calibrated "How" questions until they define successful implementation in their own words ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])

## Applications

- **Hostage negotiation**: Confirming a kidnapper's commitment to a surrender plan three different ways before executing it ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])
- **Business deals**: Getting a client to agree to terms, then summarize the agreement for a "That's right," then answer "How will we address things if we find we're off track?" — ensuring real commitment ([[summary-5-20-nstd-ch8-execution]])
- **Sales**: Detecting counterfeit "Yes" from prospects who have no intention of buying but are too polite to say no
