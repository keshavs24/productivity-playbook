# Conflicts of Interest

**Phase(s):** 2
**Confidence:** 0.7

## Definition
Situations in which a professional's personal incentives (financial gain, career advancement, relationship maintenance) conflict with their duty to provide objective advice or judgment. A key manifestation of [[bounded-ethicality]], the psychology of conflicts of interest shows that even honest, well-intentioned professionals cannot provide unbiased judgment when they stand to benefit from a particular outcome.

## Key Findings
- Financial advisers earn fees on transactions they recommend. Surgeons earn more when they operate. Doctors receive payment for clinical trial referrals. Commission-paid lawyers recommend settling more than hourly lawyers. Real-estate agents earn fees from transactions. Most professionals assume they are immune to these conflicts -- research shows they are not.
- **Moore, Cain, Loewenstein, and Bazerman (2005):** When people are motivated to interpret data in a self-serving direction, they are psychologically incapable of providing objective assessments.
- **The Disclosure Paradox (Cain, Loewenstein, & Moore, 2005):** In a coin-jar estimation game, advisers whose conflicts of interest were disclosed to estimators gave MORE biased advice than those whose conflicts were not disclosed. Estimators failed to sufficiently discount the biased advice. Disclosure led advisers to make more money and estimators to make less. This challenges the widespread assumption that transparency solves the problem.
- **Motivated blindness:** When people have an incentive not to notice data, they systematically overlook it.
  - Arthur Andersen accepted Enron's flawed accounting because auditors depend on clients for future contracts
  - When NASDAQ lost 60% of its value, 99.1% of brokerage analyst recommendations remained "Strong Buy," "Buy," or "Hold"
  - MLB management ignored steroid use because enhanced performance boosted attendance and revenue
  - Cardinal Bernard Law overlooked priest abuse because acknowledging it would damage the Church's reputation
  - Credit-rating agencies gave AAA ratings to risky mortgage-backed securities because they were paid by the issuers
- **Bazerman, Loewenstein, and Moore (2002):** Creating true auditor independence would require fundamental changes to the auditor-client relationship so auditors have no motivation to please clients. Society has not yet confronted this challenge.
- **Scalia/Cheney case (2004):** Justice Scalia dismissed the idea that a duck-hunting trip with the Vice President could bias his judgment, citing the Supreme Court's conflict-of-interest rules. But those rules were designed to guard against intentional corruption, not the unconscious bias that research documents.

## Mechanisms
- **Self-serving interpretation:** When we benefit from a particular interpretation of data, we unconsciously lean toward that interpretation
- **Moral licensing:** Disclosure can paradoxically increase bias by giving advisers a feeling of moral permission ("I told them about my conflict, so I'm off the hook")
- **Motivated blindness:** The incentive not to notice creates genuine blindness, not deliberate suppression -- the overlooking is unconscious
- **Belief in personal immunity:** The "bias blind spot" -- most people believe they personally are immune to conflicts of interest while recognizing the problem in others

## Applications
- **Avoid biased sources:** When possible, seek advice from people who have no stake in the outcome
- **Don't trust honesty alone:** Recognize that honesty does not solve the problem because even honest people are biased by conflicts
- **Don't assume immunity:** Neither you nor your advisers are immune to conflicts of interest
- **Structural solutions over disclosure:** Rather than relying on transparency (which can backfire), restructure incentives to eliminate conflicts
- **Policy design:** Broad institutional reforms (random auditor assignment, mandatory rotation, fee structures that decouple advice from transactions) are more effective than individual vigilance
