# Ch.1 Theoretical Perspectives — Summary

**Author(s):** Albert Bandura
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Self-efficacy is defined as beliefs in one's capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to produce given attainments** -- it is not a measure of skill but a belief about what one can do with whatever skills one possesses.
2. **Human agency operates within triadic reciprocal causation** -- personal factors (cognitive, affective, biological), behavior, and environmental events all influence each other bidirectionally, rejecting both pure environmentalism and pure personalism.
3. **Self-efficacy is conceptually and empirically distinct from self-esteem, self-concept, locus of control, outcome expectations, effectance motivation, and control beliefs** -- Bandura systematically differentiates his construct from every rival framework.
4. **Freedom and determinism are compatible** in social cognitive theory -- determinism operates probabilistically through reciprocal causation, and self-influence (through reflective thought, generative use of knowledge, self-regulation) is a genuine causal contributor, not an illusion.
5. **Self-efficacy is not culturally bound to Western individualism** -- efficacy beliefs regulate functioning in both individualistic and collectivistic social systems, and collective efficacy extends the construct to group-level agency.

## Detailed Notes

### The Nature of Human Agency (pp.1-3)

Bandura opens by distinguishing the power to originate actions for given purposes (agency) from the mechanical power to make things happen. Agency refers to acts done intentionally. A key distinction: one must separate the personal production of action from the effects that course of action produces -- agency is about intentional action, not about whether the consequences are intended.

Beliefs of personal efficacy constitute the key factor of [[human-agency]]. If people believe they have no power to produce results, they will not attempt to make things happen. In social cognitive theory, a sense of personal efficacy is represented as propositional beliefs embedded in a network of functional relationships with other factors.

People's beliefs in their efficacy have diverse effects: they influence courses of action chosen, effort invested, perseverance in the face of obstacles and failures, resilience to adversity, whether thought patterns are self-hindering or self-aiding, stress and depression levels, and accomplishment levels.

### Human Agency in Triadic Reciprocal Causation (pp.5-7)

The term causation is used to mean functional dependence between events. In [[reciprocal-determinism]], human agency operates within an interdependent causal structure involving triadic reciprocal causation (Bandura, 1986a):
- **P** (Person): Internal personal factors -- cognitive, affective, and biological events
- **B** (Behavior): Behavioral patterns
- **E** (Environment): External environment

These three classes of determinants influence one another bidirectionally (Figure 1.1). Reciprocity does not mean the three sets are of equal strength -- their relative influence varies for different activities and circumstances. Nor do the mutual influences spring forth simultaneously -- there are time lags that allow analysis of different segments.

Human adaptation and change are rooted in social systems. Personal agency operates within a broad network of sociostructural influences. People are both producers and products of social systems. Social structures (rules, sanctions, customs) are created by human activity but in turn impose constraints and provide resources. Neither structural constraints nor enabling resources foreordain what individuals become and do.

Sociostructural and psychological theories should not be regarded as rival conceptions but as different levels of causation. A full understanding requires an integrated causal perspective in which social influences operate through self-processes that produce the actions.

### Conceptions of Agent Causality (pp.6-7)

Social cognitive theory extends agent causality from individual agency to collective agency. People's shared belief in their capabilities to produce effects collectively is a crucial ingredient of collective efficacy. Collective efficacy is not simply the sum of individual efficacy beliefs -- it is an emergent group-level attribute that is the product of coordinative and interactive dynamics.

### Determinism and the Exercise of Self-Influence (pp.7-10)

The term **determinism** is used to signify the production of effects by events rather than the doctrinaire sense that actions are completely determined by a prior sequence of causes independent of the individual. Because most behavior is codetermined by many factors operating interactively, events produce effects probabilistically rather than inevitably within the reciprocally deterministic system.

**Freedom** is not conceived negatively as exemption from social influences or situational constraints. Rather, it is defined positively as the exercise of self-influence to bring about desired results. This agentic causation relies heavily on cognitive self-regulation -- reflective thought, generative use of knowledge and skills, and other tools of self-influence.

Self-influence affects not only choices but the success with which chosen courses of action are executed. People construct personal standards that they use to guide, motivate, and regulate their own behavior. After self-reactive capabilities are developed, behavior usually produces two sets of consequences -- external outcomes and self-evaluative reactions -- that can operate as complementary or opposing influences.

Bandura critiques Skinner's (1971) environmental determinism at length. In Skinner's view, people are repositories for past stimulus inputs and conduits for external stimulation -- they can add nothing to their performance. Bandura argues this involves a conceptual sleight of hand: the determinants of human action are regressed to an "initiating cause" located in the environment, rendering human thought entirely externally implanted, acausal, and redundant. The sociocognitive analysis of reciprocal causation does not invite an infinite regression of causes, because individuals originate actions from their experiences and reflective thought rather than merely undergoing actions as implants of the past.

### Related Views of Personal Efficacy (pp.10-35)

#### Self-Concept (pp.10-11)
Self-concept is a composite view of oneself formed through direct experience and evaluations from significant others. Bandura argues self-concept theories are too global -- combining diverse attributes into a single index creates confusion. In comparative tests of predictive power, efficacy beliefs are highly predictive of behavior, whereas self-concept is weaker and equivocal. Self-concept loses most of its predictiveness when the influence of perceived efficacy is factored out -- suggesting self-concept largely reflects people's beliefs in their personal efficacy.

#### Differentiating Self-Efficacy from Self-Esteem (pp.11-13)
Self-efficacy and self-esteem refer to entirely different things. Perceived self-efficacy is concerned with judgments of personal capability, whereas self-esteem is concerned with judgments of self-worth. There is no fixed relationship between beliefs about one's capabilities and whether one likes or dislikes oneself. People need much more than high self-esteem to do well. Perceived personal efficacy predicts goals and performance attainments, whereas self-esteem affects neither (Mone, Baker & Jeffries, 1995).

#### Effectance Motivation (pp.13-16)
White (1959, 1960) postulated an intrinsic effectance motive -- a need to deal effectively with the environment. Bandura critiques it: the theory is insufficiently detailed, focuses narrowly on exploratory behavior, and the relationship between attainments and self-satisfaction is more complex than effectance theory allows. Yarrow et al. (1983) recast it as "mastery motivation," but behavioral indices are weakly related and not consistently linked to actual competence.

#### The Exercise of Personal Control: Inborn Drive or Prevalent Incentive? (pp.16-17)
Bandura argues control is not a drive in its own right -- people exercise control for the benefits they gain by it. Control is not universally desired or routinely exercised and carries onerous responsibilities.

#### Proxy Control (p.17)
People often relinquish personal control to free themselves from performance demands, seeking well-being through proxy control. A low sense of efficacy fosters dependence on proxy control, which further reduces opportunities to build skills.

#### Inadvertent Relinquishment of Personal Control (pp.17-18)
Bandura discusses Langer's (1979) research on illusory incompetence -- people give up personal control by making erroneous inferences or by inadvertent action. **Mindlessness** is hypothesized by Langer and Park (1990) to underlie illusory incompetence. Trivial situational factors can nevertheless influence efficacy beliefs and exert strong effects on performance motivation. Biasing external influences impair performance through their influence on efficacy beliefs rather than directly.

#### Outcome Expectancy Theories (pp.19-21)
**Perceived self-efficacy and [[locus-of-control]] are entirely different phenomena.** Beliefs about whether one can produce certain actions (perceived self-efficacy) cannot be considered the same as beliefs about whether actions affect outcomes (locus of control). The conceptual distinction is corroborated empirically (Bandura, 1991b) -- they bear little or no relationship to each other. Self-efficacy is a uniformly good predictor of diverse behaviors, whereas locus of control is generally weak or inconsistent.

#### Self-Efficacy, Outcome Expectancies, and Control (pp.21-24)
The distinction between [[efficacy-expectations-vs-outcome-expectations]] is central. Perceived self-efficacy is a judgment of one's ability to organize and execute given types of performances; an outcome expectation is a judgment of the likely consequence such performances will produce (Figure 1.3).

Outcome expectations take three forms: (1) physical -- pleasant/aversive sensory experiences; (2) social -- approval, recognition, rejection, censure; (3) self-evaluative -- self-satisfaction, pride, self-censure.

Where performance determines outcome, efficacy beliefs account for most of the variance in expected outcomes. When efficacy beliefs are controlled, outcome expectations make little or no independent contribution to prediction across diverse spheres.

Expected outcomes are independent of efficacy beliefs only when contingencies are restrictively structured so no level of competence by certain groups can produce desired outcomes (e.g., racial segregation in professional sports).

#### Self-Guidance by Envisioned Possible Selves (pp.25-26)
Markus and Nurius (1986) propose possible selves as self-images of future successes and failures. Bandura critiques: the fractionation of selves poses conceptual problems, requiring an infinite regress of selves. There is only one self that can visualize different futures and select courses of action.

#### Control Beliefs (pp.26-29)
Skinner et al. divide the control sequence into agency beliefs, means-ends beliefs, and control beliefs. Bandura critiques: the tripartite scheme includes only three of four essential elements (missing performance attainments), treats means as fixed entities rather than dynamic capabilities, and in tests of predictive power, only agency beliefs predict performance.

Langer (1983) has shown people are easily misled into believing they exercise control over chance outcomes if skill-related cues are present -- connecting to [[illusion-of-control]].

#### Primary versus Secondary Control (pp.29-31)
Primary control involves changing realities; secondary control involves accommodation. Bandura argues the distinction is useful but should not carry differential inherent status. Human functioning is not neatly compartmentalized.

#### Self-Efficacy in Individualistic and Collectivistic Social Systems (pp.31-33)
Self-efficacy is sometimes inappropriately equated with individualism. But a high sense of personal efficacy is just as important to group-directedness as to self-directedness. Gandhi provides an example. Efficacy beliefs regulate functioning in both types of systems. Earley (1993, 1994) attests to the cultural universality of the functional value of efficacy beliefs.

#### Enablement versus Moralization (pp.33-35)
Self-efficacy is concerned with human enablement, not moral judgment. The overriding message is enablement, not personal blame. Personal and social change are complementary rather than rival approaches.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Self-efficacy vs. [[illusion-of-control]]:** Bandura discusses Langer's (1979) work on illusory incompetence and illusory control. He treats Langer's findings as compatible with self-efficacy theory -- illusory efficacy beliefs (even when produced by trivial situational factors like anchoring) exert genuine causal influence on performance motivation. The illusion of control literature focuses on overestimation of control over chance events; Bandura focuses on how beliefs about personal capabilities (even if inaccurate) causally regulate behavior.
- **Self-efficacy vs. [[locus-of-control]]:** Bandura argues these are entirely different phenomena that are empirically uncorrelated. Locus of control concerns whether actions affect outcomes; self-efficacy concerns whether one can perform the actions.
- **Self-efficacy vs. self-esteem:** Systematically distinguished -- efficacy is about capability judgments, esteem is about worth judgments. One can have low self-efficacy without low self-esteem and vice versa.
