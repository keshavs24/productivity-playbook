# Pre-Suasion Ch.6: Commanders of Attention 2 — The Magnetizers — Summary

**Author:** Robert Cialdini
**Source:** *Pre-Suasion: A Revolutionary Way to Influence and Persuade* (2016), Chapter 6
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Magnetizers hold attention rather than just drawing it.** While attractors (Ch.5) initially seize attention, magnetizers *keep* it there — and holding an audience's focus on favorable elements means opposing points of view get locked out of the attentional environment.
2. **Three types of information combine initial pulling power with staying power: the self-relevant, the unfinished, and the mysterious.**
3. **Self-relevance is the superglue of attention.** Information tailored to the individual (by age, sex, health history, or simply using the word "you") is more likely to be attended to, found interesting, taken seriously, remembered, and saved for future reference. An Ohio State study showed that replacing "people" and "they" with "you" in ad copy enhanced audience attitudes — but only when the subsequent case was strong.
4. **The Zeigarnik effect shows that unfinished tasks are more memorable and attention-holding than finished ones.** Bluma Zeigarnik discovered this from observing a waiter who could remember complex orders perfectly while serving but couldn't recall them at all once the task was complete. The drive for cognitive closure keeps attention magnetized to incomplete activities.
5. **Mystery stories are the most powerful teaching and persuasion device because they combine the unfinished (the unsolved puzzle) with the inherently interesting.** Cialdini discovered that the most successful popular science writing began with a mystery. In his own classroom, beginning lectures with mysteries kept students engaged past the bell — and produced better test scores because mysteries require *explanations*, which are deeper than descriptions or arguments.

## Detailed Notes

### The Self-Relevant

- **Health communications**: Self-relevant messages (tailored by age, sex, health history) are more effective across weight loss, exercise initiation, smoking cessation, and cancer screening
- **The word "you"**: Replacing externalizing words ("people," "they") with the personalizing pronoun "you" enhances audience attention and attitude toward a product — but only when followed by a strong case (self-relevant cues bring attention, not automatic approval)
- **Next-in-line effect**: People focused on their own upcoming turn to speak miss what others say — both immediately before and after their own contribution. Cialdini's advice for meetings: sit across from the key decision-maker (not next to them) to ensure both visual prominence and temporal distance from their contribution
- **Group photos**: When passed a group photo, everyone looks at themselves first, longest, and last

### The Unfinished

- **Beer garden origin**: Kurt Lewin and Bluma Zeigarnik observed a veteran waiter who could remember complex orders perfectly while serving — but couldn't recall them at all once the last dish was placed. Once a task shifts from unfinished to finished, attentional resources are diverted elsewhere.
- **Zeigarnik effect** (600+ studies): Two key conclusions:
  1. We remember unfinished tasks better because our attention remains drawn to them
  2. Interruption from an unfinished task produces a "discomforting, gnawing desire to get back to it" — the craving for cognitive closure
- **Unfinished ads**: Commercials stopped 5-6 seconds before their natural ending produced greater recall than completed ads — and the advantage *grew* over two weeks
- **Romantic uncertainty**: College women were most attracted to men whose ratings of them remained *unknown* — because the uncertainty kept them thinking about those men, and regular refocusing made the men seem more attractive
- **Writing productivity trick**: A colleague never finishes a writing session at the end of a paragraph — she stops mid-thought, knowing precisely what comes next but refusing to write it. The unfinished thought pulls her back to write the next day.

### The Mysterious

- **Popular science writing analysis**: Cialdini analyzed the most and least successful popular science articles. The unsuccessful ones had stilted prose and jargon. The successful ones had clarity, logical structure, vivid examples, and humor — plus one unexpected quality: they all began with a **mystery story** that invited readers into the material to dispatch the enigma.
- **Classroom mysteries**: Beginning each lecture with a mystery produced remarkable engagement:
  - When Cialdini accidentally ended a lecture before resolving the mystery, students wouldn't let him leave: "They would not let me stop until I had given them closure on the mystery"
  - Test scores went up because mysteries require *explanations* (deeper processing), not just arguments or descriptions
- **Mystery story format** (six steps): (1) Pose the mystery, (2) Deepen it, (3) Home in on the proper explanation by considering and offering evidence against alternatives, (4) Provide a clue to the proper explanation, (5) Resolve the mystery, (6) Draw the implication for the phenomenon under study
- **Tobacco counterargument case study**: Cialdini uses the mystery format to explain why tobacco companies lobbied to ban their *own* TV ads in 1969 — the answer being that the FCC's fairness doctrine required equal time for anti-tobacco counter-ads, which were devastating (slashing consumption by 10%). By banning their own broadcast ads, tobacco companies eliminated the free counter-ads while shifting to unregulated print, sponsorships, and product placements.

### Counterarguments vs. Arguments

A key teaching point embedded in the mystery format: **counterarguments are typically more powerful than arguments**. A counterargument that shows the rival communicator to be dishonest or untrustworthy wins not just that battle but *all future battles* with that opponent. Arguments merely address the specific issue at hand.
