# Resonate Ch.8-9: Improve & Change Your World — Summary

**Author:** Nancy Duarte
**Source:** *Resonate* (2010), Chapters 8 and 9
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Amplify the signal, minimize the noise.** Communication has four types of noise: credibility noise (negative first impression, no common ground, imbalance in emotional appeal), semantic noise (too much jargon, filler words, distracting visuals), experiential noise (presentation goes long, distracting body language, poor environment), and bias noise (both sender and receiver filter through their own biases and dogmatism). Your job is to reduce noise at every step.
2. **Balance emotion appropriately for the audience.** There is another Greek word beyond [[ethos]], [[pathos]], and [[logos]]: *kairos* — "timing" or "timeliness," meaning speaking the right moment in the right way. Modify the emotional/analytical balance to match the audience: highly analytical audiences find too much emotion manipulative; emotionally driven audiences find too many facts boring. Taking either appeal too far in either direction hurts credibility.
3. **Preparation is proportional to impact.** Rehearse extensively, host screenings with honest critics, practice and re-rehearse. Markus Covert rehearsed his NIH Pioneer Award presentation twenty times with scientists from various disciplines — and won. Leonard Bernstein worked on Young People's Concert scripts for days, editing through up to ten revisions, and even made notations about anticipated audience reactions in alternate sections of his scripts. "The first draft of anything is shit." — Hemingway
4. **Presentations can change the world — but they must be used ethically.** Duarte devotes significant space to Enron as a cautionary tale where presentations were used as propaganda tools, contributing to a massive fraud. Don't use presentations for evil. The right presentation could have prevented the Enron disaster. Be transparent: be honest, be unique, don't compromise. Passion for your idea should drive you to invest in its communication. Case studies of Martin Luther King Jr. ("I Have a Dream") and Martha Graham (founder of modern dance) show how sustained, courageous presentation of ideas can change entire cultures.

## Detailed Notes

### Ch.8: Amplify the Signal, Minimize the Noise
A presentation broadcasts information like a radio. The signal-to-noise ratio determines how well the message gets through. Four types of noise occur in communication: credibility, semantic, experiential, and bias noise (both sender and receiver). Minimize noise at every step.

### Give a Positive First Impression
Within the first few seconds, people classify you in their minds and judge whether they will connect with you. First impressions do not have to be overly dramatic — they are about revealing character, motivations, abilities, and vulnerabilities. Consider all pre-presentation communications: the invitation, the agenda, your bio, the email.

### Hop Down from Your Tower
Specialized jargon carries penalties. Innovation generates new terms weekly, but if your audience does not share your vocabulary, your idea will not be adopted. Barbara McClintock's groundbreaking genetics research was overlooked for decades partly because her communication style was too rapid and leapt between observations and conclusions in single sentences. Your idea's perceived value will be judged not so much on the idea itself but on how well you can communicate it.

### Value Brevity
Presentations fail because of too much information, not too little. Lincoln's Gettysburg Address was 278 words delivered in just over two minutes — and follows the presentation form perfectly: what is (national values, current war), call to action (the dead shall not have died in vain), new bliss (a new birth of freedom). "If I am to speak for ten minutes, I need a week for preparation; if fifteen minutes, three days; if half an hour, two days; if an hour, I am ready now." — Woodrow Wilson

### Wean Yourself from the Slides
People can only process one inbound message at a time — they will either listen to you or read your slides, not both. Your goal is to move away from projecting a document and toward giving a presentation. One idea per slide. Keep it simple. Slides should serve as stage setting, not sole focus. When used appropriately, slides work with the presenter like a dance partner.

### Balance Emotion
Persuasive presentations should appropriately balance analytical and emotional appeal. *Kairos* means "timing" or "timeliness" — speaking the right moment in the right way. Modify the emotional/analytical balance to match audience and situation. Imbalance in either direction hurts credibility. For analytical audiences: keep emotional appeal modest but present. For broad audiences: lean toward visceral, story-weighted structure.

### Host a Screening with Honest Critics
The best way to test messages is to host a screening. Choose naysayers who will scrutinize and challenge your perspective. Each screener should have a printout and run through the entire presentation, then revisit each section. A solid review should last about three times as long as the presentation. Conway's Law: "Any organization that designs a system will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure." Therefore, a presentation's quality will not exceed the quality of the planning process.

Six negative organizational systems that limit communication quality: Conceited Captain (leader forces team into time-crunched output), Political Paranoia (fear of risk prevents progressive decisions), Message Magic (no strategy, imaginary messages become norm), Vacuum Visionary (no room for alternative perspectives), Lackey Leader (flattery-driven consensus), Customer Cold-Shoulder (self-focused over customer insight).

### Case Study: Leonard Bernstein
Bernstein's Young People's Concerts (Carnegie Hall) held the attention of small children for an hour while teaching complex music theory. He used metaphors children understood ("like a three-stage rocket going into space"), collaborated with his team through multiple revisions, used a special two-colored pencil ("red-ee blue-ee") to annotate scores from both conductor and audience perspectives, and anticipated multiple audience responses in his scripts. He put as much rehearsal energy into his presentation scripts as into his musical scores. Connects to [[deliberate-practice]].

### Ch.9: Change Your World
Ideas are not really alive if they are confined to only one person's mind. They become alive when adopted by another, then another, reaching a tipping point. JFK's lunar speech janitor story: "What do you do?" "I'm putting the first man on the moon, sir."

### Don't Use Presentations for Evil
Enron is a cautionary tale. Jeff Skilling was sentenced to fifty-two months for each of five counts involving presentations. Presentations were used as propaganda to spread lies. Oral communications have built and toppled kingdoms — they should be used to build up, not tear down.

### Case Study: Martin Luther King Jr.
King's "I Have a Dream" speech demonstrates the presentation form at its most powerful. Key insights: the contour moves rapidly between what is and what could be; dramatic pauses at the end of each line create a sense of spoken delivery; repetition as a rhetorical device (the phrase "I Have a Dream" repeats like a hymn refrain); metaphor/visual words create images in the mind; he references familiar songs, Scripture, and political documents to establish common ground; applause approximately every 35 seconds; three distinct bursts/crescendos building to a passionate ending.

### Case Study: Martha Graham
Graham invented a revolutionary new language of dance. She stood out by moving against the grain of society. Three keys to being transparent: be honest (be the authentic you), be unique (share your unique perspective), don't compromise (speak confidently even when it is scary). "There is a vitality, a life force, an energy, a quickening that is translated through you into action, and because there is only one of you in all of time, this expression is unique. And if you block it, it will never exist through any other medium and it will be lost." — Martha Graham
