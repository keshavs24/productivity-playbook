# Age of Propaganda Ch.7: Pictures in Our Heads — Summary

**Authors:** Anthony Pratkanis & Elliot Aronson
**Source:** *Age of Propaganda: The Everyday Use and Abuse of Persuasion* (Revised Edition), Chapter 7
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Walter Lippmann's "pictures in our heads" thesis (1922):** The mass media paint an imagined world, and these mental pictures influence our thoughts and actions more than we realize. We are more like the young girl who constructed a fiction from a broken window than we would admit.
2. **George Gerbner's cultivation analysis** shows that television systematically misrepresents reality — and heavy viewers absorb these distortions as truth. Crime on TV is 10x more prevalent than reality; 67% of workers are white-collar on TV vs. 25% in reality; scientists are portrayed as dangerous and mad.
3. **Heavy TV viewers** express more racially prejudiced attitudes, overestimate professional occupations, perceive women as having more limited abilities, believe science is dangerous, hold exaggerated views of violence prevalence, and see the world as more sinister — all reflecting television's distorted portrayals rather than reality.
4. **"Television criminology"** — Craig Haney and John Manzolati found that TV crime shows present a consistent worldview: police are infallible, criminals act from psychopathology or greed (not poverty or circumstance), and the wrong person is never in jail. Heavy viewers internalize this, taking harder stances on juries and reversing the presumption of innocence.

## Detailed Notes

### Lippmann's Thesis

Walter Lippmann's *Public Opinion* (1922) argued that the mass media create a "pseudo-environment" — pictures in our heads — that shapes what we think about and how we act. His key insight: we often act not on direct experience but on our mental models of reality, which are heavily influenced by media portrayals.

### Gerbner's Cultivation Analysis

George Gerbner and colleagues conducted the most extensive analysis of television content, videotaping and analyzing thousands of prime-time programs since the late 1960s. Their findings on television's distortion of reality:

| Reality on TV | Actual Reality |
|---|---|
| Males outnumber females 3:1 | Roughly equal |
| 67% white-collar/professional workers | 25% white-collar |
| Scientists are dangerous, mad, out of control | Scientists rarely commit homicide |
| Crime 10x more prevalent | Much lower crime rates |
| 50% of characters in violent confrontation weekly | <1% of real population victimized annually |

**Cultivation effects on heavy viewers (4+ hours/day vs. <2 hours/day):**
1. Express more racially prejudiced attitudes
2. Overestimate number of physicians, lawyers, and athletes
3. Perceive women as having more limited abilities and interests
4. Believe science is dangerous and that scientists are odd
5. Hold exaggerated views of violence prevalence
6. Believe old people are fewer and less healthy than reality

Heavy viewers also tend to see the world as more sinister and are more likely to agree that "most people are just looking out for themselves."

### Television Criminology

Haney and Manzolati's analysis revealed that TV crime shows dispense remarkably consistent images: police are amazingly effective (solving almost every crime), criminals act from psychopathology or greed (ignoring situational pressures like poverty), and the wrong person is never in jail. Heavy viewers internalize this belief system — they are more likely to reverse the presumption of innocence and take hard-line stances on juries.

Additional media effects documented:
- Heavy readers of sensational newspaper crime accounts report higher fear of crime
- Repeated viewing of violent "slasher" films is associated with less sympathy for rape victims
- Heavy exposure to ads portraying women as sex objects induces females to believe their bodies are too large
- When television is introduced into an area, theft increases (possibly due to televised consumerism)

### The Correlation Problem

The authors note the important caveat that Gerbner's research is correlational — it shows an association between heavy viewing and distorted beliefs, but cannot prove causation. However, they cite recent experimental studies showing that heavy viewing *does* causally determine the pictures we form of the world.
