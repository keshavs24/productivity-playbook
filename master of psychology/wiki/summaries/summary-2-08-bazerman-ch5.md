# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.5 — Framing and the Reversal of Preferences

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.5, pp.82-90)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. The same objective problem produces opposite risk preferences depending on whether outcomes are framed as gains (risk aversion) or losses (risk seeking) -- demonstrated through the Asian Disease Problem (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981)
2. Framing individual decisions sequentially creates irrational portfolios: 73% of people chose options (a) and (d) in Tversky & Kahneman's (1981) concurrent decision experiment, producing a dominated portfolio that was inferior to the 3% who chose (b) and (c)
3. Pseudocertainty -- the illusion of certainty created by framing a subset of risk as "eliminated" -- powerfully distorts choices even when overall probabilities remain unchanged
4. The word "insurance" triggers social norms that override rational analysis, making people systematically overpay for certainty
5. Framing effects generalize broadly: to taxi drivers' labor supply, golfers' putts, insurance purchasing, investment decisions, and even capuchin monkey behavior

## Detailed Notes

### The Asian Disease Problem
Tversky and Kahneman (1981) presented the classic demonstration. 600 people face a deadly disease:
- **Gain frame (Programs A vs. B):** Most choose A (200 saved for sure) over B (1/3 chance all 600 saved) -- risk aversion
- **Loss frame (Programs C vs. D):** Most choose D (1/3 chance no one dies) over C (400 die for sure) -- risk seeking
- Programs A=C and B=D objectively. The frame alone reverses the modal choice.

### Framing and the Irrationality of the Sum of Our Choices
Problem 4 (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981, n=150): concurrent decisions where Decision A involves gains ($240 sure vs. 25% chance of $1,000) and Decision B involves losses ($750 sure loss vs. 75% chance of losing $1,000).
- 84% chose the sure gain (a); 87% chose the gamble to avoid loss (d)
- Combined, (a)+(d) = 25% chance to win $240 and 75% chance to lose $760
- The inferior portfolio (b)+(c) = 25% chance to win $250 and 75% chance to lose $750 -- dominates (a)+(d)
- Only 3% chose the dominant portfolio. Sequential framing produces systematically worse aggregate outcomes.
- **Managerial implication:** Sales departments frame as gains (risk averse), credit offices frame as losses (risk seeking). Organizations need integrated risky decision procedures.

Paul Samuelson's (1963) coin-toss story: colleague rejected a single bet (win $200 / lose $100) but would accept 100 such bets. The aggregated risk of many positive-EV small gambles becomes infinitesimal. Kahneman and Lovallo (1993) argue for an expected-value rule for most decisions.

### Pseudocertainty (We Like Certainty, Even Pseudocertainty)
Russian Roulette comparison: people pay far more to remove the last bullet (1/6 to 0 = certainty) than one of two bullets (2/6 to 1/6) despite identical probability reduction.

Kahneman and Tversky (1979): people overweight certain outcomes and underweight high-probability ones. Slovic, Fischhoff, and Lichtenstein (1982): "any protective action that reduces the probability of harm from .01 to zero will be valued more highly than an action that reduces the probability of the same harm from .02 to .01."

**Slovic et al. (1982) vaccination study:** Disease expected to afflict 20% of population.
- Version 1 (probabilistic): vaccine protects half of vaccinated individuals -- 40% would get vaccinated
- Version 2 (pseudocertainty): two strains, vaccine eliminates one strain completely -- 57% would get vaccinated
- Same objective risk reduction, but pseudocertainty framing increased uptake by 43%

**Tversky & Kahneman (1981) two-stage game (Problems 6-8):** $30 sure vs. 80% chance at $45.
- Problem 6 (simple): 78% chose $30 sure
- Problem 7 (two-stage with 75% elimination first): 74% chose $30 "sure" -- but actual probability is .25 x 1.0 = .25
- Problem 8 (single stage, same math): 42% chose $30
- Problems 7 and 8 are mathematically identical, yet perceived differently because Problem 7 creates pseudocertainty

### Framing and the Overselling of Insurance
Insurance = certain loss (premium) to reduce probability of larger loss. Virtually all insurance provides negative expected value to the buyer.

Slovic et al. (1982): Describing a sure loss as an "insurance premium" vs. a "certain loss" dramatically increased preference for it. The word "insurance" triggers social norms ("How can you not carry insurance?").

**Extended warranties example:** Nissan lawsuit revealed that of a typical $795 extended warranty, only $131 went to repairs, $109 to admin, and $555 was pure dealer profit. Consumers would be better off declining all extended warranties and self-insuring.

### The Evolutionary Explanation
Rayo and Becker (2007): evolution programmed us with [[reference-dependence]] and [[framing-effects]] because the subjective utility scale has limited sensitivity (declining marginal utility). The reference point must readjust to maintain motivation -- creating the hedonic treadmill (Brickman & Campbell, 1971; Gilbert, 2006). Evidence: people adapt to both positive and negative changes with surprising speed (Brickman, Coates, & Janoff-Bulman, 1978).
