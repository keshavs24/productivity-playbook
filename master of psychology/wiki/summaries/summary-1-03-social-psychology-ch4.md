# Social Psychology Ch.4: Social Perception — Summary

**Author(s):** [[aronson|Elliot Aronson]], Timothy Wilson, Robin Akert
**Type:** Textbook chapter (Chapter 4: Social Perception)
**Published:** *Social Psychology*, Pearson (10th edition)
**Phase:** 1 — Cognitive Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-13

## Key Takeaways

1. **Social perception is how we form impressions of and make inferences about other people** — relying on nonverbal communication, first impressions, and causal attribution.
2. **The fundamental attribution error (FAE)** is the central bias in social perception: we overestimate dispositional causes and underestimate situational causes of others' behavior. This is arguably the most important single concept in social psychology.
3. **First impressions form in under 100 milliseconds** and persist through primacy effects and belief perseverance — making the opening moments of any interaction disproportionately powerful.
4. **Culture profoundly shapes social perception** — Western individualistic cultures favor dispositional attributions (analytic thinking), while East Asian collectivistic cultures favor situational attributions (holistic thinking). This is not inborn but learned.
5. **People are systematically bad at recognizing their own biases** — the bias blind spot means we see others as biased but ourselves as rational.

## Detailed Notes

### Section 1: Nonverbal Communication (LO 4.1)

**[[nonverbal-communication]]** — how people communicate intentionally or unintentionally without words. Channels include facial expressions, tone of voice, gestures, body position, eye gaze, touch, and personal space.

**Facial Expressions of Emotion:**
- Darwin (1872) proposed facial expressions are universal, evolutionary vestiges of once-useful physiological reactions
- Paul Ekman and Walter Friesen (1971) confirmed this with the South Fore tribe in New Guinea — a preliterate people with no Western contact could match facial expressions to emotional stories with the same accuracy as Western subjects
- **Six universal emotions:** anger, happiness, surprise, fear, disgust, sadness (possibly also pride, contempt, anxiety, shame, embarrassment)
- **Affect blends:** one part of the face registers one emotion, another part registers a different emotion — making decoding harder
- Susskind et al. (2008): fear face *enhances* sensory input (wider eyes, more air intake); disgust face *reduces* it — functional evolutionary design

**Complications in decoding:**
- Context matters: the same facial expression has different implications depending on eye gaze direction (Adams & Kleck, 2003) and surrounding people's expressions (Masuda et al., 2008)
- Culture matters: Japanese participants interpreted emotion in context (holistic); Americans focused on the individual face alone (analytic)

**Culture and Nonverbal Channels:**
- **Display rules** (Ekman): culturally determined norms about which emotional expressions are appropriate. Japanese norms suppress negative expressions; American norms suppress male grief/crying.
- **Emblems:** gestures with well-defined meanings within a culture (OK sign, thumbs up) — but these are NOT universal and can cause cross-cultural miscommunication
- Personal space norms vary dramatically across cultures (Hall, 1969)

### Section 2: First Impressions — Quick But Long-Lasting (LO 4.2)

**[[thin-slicing]]** (Ambady & Rosenthal, 1992, 1993):
- People form impressions from extremely brief behavioral samples
- Impressions form in **under 100 milliseconds** based on facial appearance alone (Bar, Neta, & Linz, 2006; Willis & Todorov, 2006)
- Children as young as 3 show this tendency (Cogsdill et al., 2014)
- Thin-sliced impressions of teachers from 10-second silent video clips **correlated significantly** with end-of-semester evaluations from students who spent months with those teachers
- Baby-face effect: people with childlike features (big eyes, small chin) are perceived as naive, warm, submissive
- Political candidates who *look* more powerful are more likely to win elections (Rule & Ambady, 2010; Todorov et al., 2008)

**[[primacy-effect]]** (Asch, 1946):
- The classic demonstration: "intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious" produces a more positive impression than the same traits in reverse order
- First information creates a **schema** through which all subsequent information is filtered
- "What is beautiful is good" — physical attractiveness creates a halo effect (Dion, Berscheid, & Walster, 1972)

**[[belief-perseverance]]:**
- People stick with initial judgments even when the evidence is contradicted or retracted (Anderson, 1995; Ross, Lepper, & Hubbard, 1975)
- Explains why jurors can't disregard ruled-inadmissible evidence, why discredited research persists, why debunked political misinformation keeps working
- Connection to cognitive dissonance: "we find inconsistent thoughts unpleasant and uncomfortable"

**Practical implications:** First impressions in presentations, job interviews, and meetings are disproportionately important. The opening moments set a frame that is extremely hard to overcome.

### Section 3: Causal Attribution — Answering the "Why" Question (LO 4.3)

**[[attribution-theory]]** (Heider, 1958):
- Fritz Heider: people are "naive scientists" who try to understand behavior by inferring causes
- The fundamental dichotomy: **internal attribution** (disposition, personality, character) vs. **external attribution** (situation, context, circumstance)
- Which attribution we make profoundly shapes our impression of the person
- In relationships: happy spouses make internal attributions for partner's positive behavior and external attributions for negative behavior; distressed spouses do the opposite

**[[covariation-model]]** (Kelley, 1967):
- To determine internal vs. external attribution, we assess three dimensions:
  - **Consensus:** Do other people behave the same way toward this stimulus?
  - **Distinctiveness:** Does this person behave this way toward other stimuli?
  - **Consistency:** Does this person behave this way toward this stimulus across time?
- **Internal attribution** when: low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency (only this person, acts this way with everyone, does it every time)
- **External attribution** when: high consensus + high distinctiveness + high consistency (everyone reacts this way, only to this stimulus, every time)
- Limitation: people underuse consensus information and don't always have all three dimensions available

**[[fundamental-attribution-error]]** (Ross, 1977) / Correspondence Bias:
- The tendency to **overestimate internal, dispositional factors and underestimate situational factors** when explaining others' behavior
- **Jones & Harris (1967) Castro essay study:** Even when told the essay writer was *assigned* a position (pro- or anti-Castro), participants still believed the essay reflected the writer's true beliefs. Knowing the situation didn't eliminate the dispositional inference.
- **Why it happens — [[perceptual-salience]]:** People are perceptually prominent; situations are invisible. We see the person, not the context. Taylor & Fiske (1975) showed that whichever actor observers could see better was judged as more influential — same conversation, different attributions based on viewing angle.
- **Lassiter et al. (2007):** Camera angle on videotaped confessions affects perceived voluntariness — even judges and police officers rated confessions as more voluntary when the camera focused only on the suspect. New Zealand adopted equal-focus camera rules in direct response.
- **Two-step attribution process (Gilbert, 1989):** Step 1 is automatic — make an internal attribution. Step 2 is effortful — adjust for the situation. We often don't complete Step 2, especially when distracted, busy, or cognitively loaded. This connects directly to [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] from 1-01.

**[[self-serving-attribution]]:**
- Take credit for successes (internal attribution); blame failures on external factors
- Athletes: 80% of attributions for wins were internal; losses were attributed to external causes (Lau & Russell, 1980)
- Less experienced athletes make stronger self-serving attributions; experienced athletes are more balanced
- Motivated by: self-esteem maintenance, impression management, and differential information access (you know your own effort and ability better than an observer does)

**[[just-world-belief]]** (Lerner, 1980):
- The assumption that people get what they deserve and deserve what they get
- A **defensive attribution** — believing the world is just protects the belief that bad things won't happen to us
- Leads to **victim blaming** — victims of crimes and accidents are seen as somehow responsible for their fate
- More common in cultures with extreme wealth inequality; connected to karma in Hindu/Buddhist traditions

**[[bias-blind-spot]]** (Pronin et al., 2002):
- People rate themselves as "somewhat" susceptible to biases but rate the "average American" as much more susceptible
- An ironic self-serving belief: "I'm rational, but other people are biased"
- Implications: awareness of biases doesn't automatically protect against them

### Section 4: Culture and Social Perception (LO 4.4)

**Individualistic vs. Collectivistic Cultures:**
- Western cultures stress individual autonomy → **analytic thinking style** (focus on the object/person, ignore context)
- East Asian cultures stress group harmony → **holistic thinking style** (focus on the whole picture, object + context + relationships)
- Masuda et al. (2008): Americans judged a cartoon character's emotion from the character alone; Japanese were significantly influenced by surrounding characters' expressions. Eye-tracking confirmed Japanese scanned the background more.
- Neuroscience (Hedden et al., 2008): fMRI showed Americans needed more brain activation to attend to context; East Asians needed more to ignore context — the default mode differs by culture.

**Cultural differences in FAE:**
- Miller (1984): Americans made dispositional attributions for Indian-generated behavior examples; Indians made situational attributions for the same behaviors. Children from both cultures were indistinguishable — the difference is learned, not inborn.
- Hong et al. (2003): Bicultural Hong Kong students primed with American images made more dispositional attributions; primed with Chinese images, more situational. Cultural thinking styles can be activated by priming.

**Cultural differences in self-serving bias:**
- Meta-analysis of 266 studies (Mezulis et al., 2004): Self-serving bias strongest in US, Canada, Australia, NZ. Weaker in Japan, Pacific Islands, India.
- In collectivistic cultures: success is attributed to others (teachers, parents, situation); failure is attributed to self (self-criticism as social glue)
- Media coverage reflects this: American Olympic coverage focused on individual talent; Japanese coverage included coaches, family, and circumstances

## Contradictions or Tensions
- **With 1-01 (Heuristics paper):** The FAE can be understood as a specific instance of [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — the initial dispositional attribution is the anchor, and situational adjustment is insufficient (Gilbert's two-step model explicitly makes this connection).
- **Cultural moderation of biases:** 1-01 and 1-02 present biases as universal cognitive features. This chapter shows that the FAE and self-serving bias vary significantly by culture — the biases are not equally strong everywhere. This doesn't invalidate them but adds an important moderator.
- **Thin-slicing accuracy vs. biases:** There's a tension between the finding that thin-sliced impressions can be remarkably accurate (Ambady) and the finding that first impressions are biased by irrelevant features (baby-face, attractiveness halo). Thin-slicing works when the cues are genuinely informative but misleads when they trigger stereotypes.
