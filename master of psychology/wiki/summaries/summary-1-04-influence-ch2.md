# Influence Ch.2: Reciprocation — Summary

**Author:** Robert B. Cialdini
**Source:** *Influence: Science and Practice*, 5th Edition (2009), Chapter 2
**Phase:** 1 — Cognitive Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **The rule of reciprocation is universal across all human cultures** (Gouldner, 1960). It requires that we try to repay what another person has provided us. It is one of the most potent weapons of influence.
2. **The rule is overpowering** — it can overcome liking as a determinant of compliance. Even people we dislike can trigger obligation through unsolicited favors.
3. **The rule enforces uninvited debts** — you don't have to ask for a favor to feel obligated to repay it. This is what makes it exploitable.
4. **The rule can trigger unequal exchanges** — a small initial favor can produce a substantially larger return favor, because the discomfort of indebtedness outweighs the cost of the larger favor.
5. **Reciprocal concessions** (rejection-then-retreat / door-in-the-face) is a second, more subtle application — making a concession triggers an obligation to concede in return.

## Detailed Notes

### Universality and Evolutionary Basis
- **Gouldner (1960)**: After intensive study, concluded that *all* human societies subscribe to the reciprocity rule
- **Richard Leakey**: Reciprocity is what makes us human — our ancestors learned to share food and skills "in an honored network of obligation" (Leakey & Lewin, 1978)
- **Marcel Mauss (1954)**: Three obligations — to give, to receive, and to repay
- **Vartan Bhanji**: Institutionalized gift exchange in Pakistan/India that painstakingly prevents the total elimination of outstanding obligations
- The rule enabled division of labor, diverse exchange, and interdependencies — conferring extraordinary advantages on cultures that possessed it

### The Rule Is Overpowering
**Regan's Coke Study (1971)** — the foundational experiment:
- Subjects rated paintings alongside "Joe" (actually a research assistant)
- In one condition, Joe brought back an unsolicited Coke during a break
- Later, Joe asked subjects to buy raffle tickets at 25¢ each
- **Result**: Subjects who received the Coke bought **2x as many tickets** (500% ROI — a 10¢ Coke yielded 50¢ in ticket sales)
- **Critical finding**: For subjects who received the Coke, **liking had zero effect** on compliance. Those who disliked Joe bought just as many tickets as those who liked him. The reciprocity rule completely overwhelmed the liking factor.

### The Hare Krishna Strategy
The Krishnas solved their fundraising problem by engaging the reciprocity rule instead of relying on liking (which they couldn't generate — the public found them off-putting):
- Press a flower or book into someone's hands as a "gift"
- Refuse to take it back: "No, it is our gift to you"
- *Then* ask for a donation
- The benefactor-before-beggar strategy was wildly successful, funding 321 centers in the US
- Eventually declined not because the rule weakened, but because people learned to avoid Krishna solicitors in airports

### The Rule Enforces Uninvited Debts
- **American Disabled Veterans**: Simple mail appeal produces ~18% response. Add an unsolicited gift (personalized address labels) → response **nearly doubles to 35%**
- **The obligation to receive** is what makes the rule exploitable. In the Regan study, no subject refused the Coke. Joe chose the form of the initial favor *and* the form of the return favor. The subject's "genuinely free choices were Joe's."
- **WWI bread story** (Eibl-Eibesfeldt, 1975): A German soldier capturing enemies was stopped cold when his captive shared bread — he couldn't complete his mission and returned empty-handed to face his superiors' wrath
- **Diane Louie / Jonestown**: She survived the mass suicide precisely because she had *refused* Jim Jones's special favors. "I knew once he gave me those privileges, he'd have me."

### The Rule Can Trigger Unequal Exchanges
- **Jump-start → car loan story**: A woman who received a small favor (jump-start) felt obligated to lend her new car to a teenager she barely knew. He totaled it.
- Two forces drive unequal exchanges:
  1. **Internal discomfort**: The feeling of indebtedness is deeply unpleasant. We're conditioned from childhood to feel uncomfortable when beholden.
  2. **External shame**: People who violate the reciprocity rule are labeled moochers, ingrates — scrupulously shunned (Wedekind & Milinski, 2000)
- People also avoid asking for needed favors if they won't be in a position to repay (De Paulo, Nadler, & Fisher, 1983)
- **Cross-cultural note**: Those who give without allowing the recipient to repay are *also* disliked — found in Americans, Swedes, and Japanese (Gergen et al., 1975)

### Reciprocity in Politics
- **LBJ's legislative success**: Political analysts were amazed at how many programs Johnson pushed through Congress. The cause was not political savvy but the massive stockpile of favors from his years in the House and Senate. Carter and Clinton, as Washington outsiders who owed no one, struggled with their early agendas.
- **Roger Tamraz** at congressional hearings on campaign finance reform, asked if his $300,000 contribution got him a good return: "I think next time, I'll give $600,000."
- **Calcium-channel blocker study** (Stelfox et al., 1998): 100% of scientists who found the drugs safe had received industry support (travel, funding, employment). Only 37% of critics had received such support.
- **AP study**: Congressional representatives receiving the most special-interest money on six key issues were over **7x more likely** to vote in the group's favor (Salant, 2003). Those groups got the win 83% of the time.

### The Not-So-Free Sample
- Supermarket samples create obligation — people buy products they don't even like after accepting a sample
- **Vance Packard (1957)**: Indiana supermarket operator sold 1,000 pounds of cheese in a few hours via free samples
- **Amway's BUG**: A tray of products left at a customer's home "for 24, 48, or 72 hours, at no cost or obligation." Since few customers use the entire product, the BUG circulates. Customers purchase ~50% of the BUG's contents on average.
- **Restaurant candy study** (Strohmetz et al., 2002): One candy with the bill → tips up 3.3%. Two candies → tips up **14%**.

### Reciprocal Concessions (Rejection-Then-Retreat)
Cialdini's **Boy Scout encounter**: An 11-year-old asked him to buy $5 circus tickets. He declined. The boy then retreated: "Well, how about buying some of our chocolate bars? They're only $1 each." Cialdini bought two — despite not liking chocolate bars, not liking dollars being spent, and walking away with unwanted candy.

The mechanism: A concession by the requester triggers an obligation to make a reciprocal concession. The retreat from a larger request *is itself* a kind of gift that demands reciprocation.

**The Zoo Trip Experiment** (Cialdini, Vincent, Lewis, Catalan, Wheeler, & Darby, 1975):
- Direct request to chaperon juvenile delinquents to the zoo → **17%** compliance
- First ask for 2 hours/week for 2 years as a counselor (refused), *then* retreat to the zoo trip → **50%** compliance (3x increase)
- The second request doesn't need to be small — it just needs to be *smaller* than the first

**The Watergate Mystery Explained**:
G. Gordon Liddy's proposals to CREEP:
1. **First proposal**: $1 million — chase plane, break-ins, kidnapping, mugging squads, yacht with "high-class call girls"
2. **Second proposal**: $500,000 — reduced program
3. **Third proposal**: $250,000 — "bare-bones" plan (the Watergate break-in)

Mitchell, Magruder, and Dean approved plan #3. As Magruder later testified: "After starting at the grandiose sum of $1 million, we thought that probably $250,000 would be an acceptable figure... We were reluctant to send him away with nothing."

**Critical detail**: Frederick LaRue, the *only* member who hadn't attended the first two meetings, was the only one who objected. Without the contrast of the earlier proposals, the break-in plan looked as stupid as it was.

### Why Rejection-Then-Retreat Works So Well (Three Reinforcing Mechanisms)
1. **Reciprocity**: The concession triggers a counter-concession
2. **Contrast principle**: The second request seems smaller by comparison to the first
3. **Sweet side effects**:
   - **Responsibility**: Targets feel they "dictated" the final agreement, making them more likely to follow through. In one study (Miller et al., 1976), 85% of rejection-then-retreat targets showed up vs. 50% of direct-request targets
   - **Satisfaction**: Targets feel more satisfied with the arrangement (Benton, Kelley, & Liebling, 1972). Concession-strategy targets gave the most money to their opponent *and* were the most satisfied
   - Together, these mean rejection-then-retreat victims not only comply — they **fulfill their agreements and agree to further requests**

### Boundary Condition
Research at Bar-Ilan University (Schwarzwald, Raz, & Zvibel, 1979): If the initial request is so extreme as to be seen as unreasonable, the tactic backfires. The retreat isn't viewed as a genuine concession. The truly gifted negotiator's initial position is "exaggerated just enough" to allow room for reciprocal concessions.

### Defense
- **Don't reject all initial offers** — blanket rejection means missing genuine generosity and creating social friction (the 10-year-old girl with flowers story)
- **The mental redefinition maneuver**: Accept favors in good faith, but if they later prove to be compliance tactics, mentally redefine them as tricks, not favors. "The rule says that favors are to be met with favors; it does not require that tricks be met with favors."
- **Counter-exploitation**: If someone uses gifts as sales tools, take the gifts, thank them politely, and show them the door. The reciprocity rule asserts that exploitation attempts should be exploited.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- Reciprocation vs. liking: The Regan study shows reciprocation can completely override liking. This is a direct tension with the liking principle (Ch.5) — suggesting a hierarchy where reciprocation may be the stronger force.
- The rule applies even to *uninvited* favors, which creates an asymmetry of power favoring the initiator. This tension between the rule's social benefit (enabling exchange) and its exploitability is a central theme.
