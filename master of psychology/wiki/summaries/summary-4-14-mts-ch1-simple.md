# Made to Stick: Chapter 1 (Simple) — Summary

**Author(s):** Chip Heath and Dan Heath
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Simple = Core + Compact.** Simplicity does not mean "dumbing down" or reducing to sound bites. It means finding the essential core of an idea and expressing it compactly. A sound bite is compact without being core; a proverb is both compact *and* core. The goal is the proverb, not the sound bite. See [[commanders-intent]].
2. **Commander's Intent forces prioritization.** The U.S. Army developed [[commanders-intent]] (CI) in the 1980s because detailed battle plans become useless on contact with the enemy. CI is a crisp, plain-talk statement of the desired end-state that allows soldiers at all levels to improvise toward the goal. "If you say three things, you don't say anything."
3. **Burying the lead kills ideas.** Journalism's "inverted pyramid" puts the most important information first. Communicators who get lost in details often bury their core message. "Forced prioritization" — the discipline of choosing a single lead — is painful but essential.
4. **Decision paralysis results from too many choices.** Research by [[tversky]] and Shafir shows that even irrelevant uncertainty can paralyze decisions, and adding a second good option (a foreign film alongside a lecture) can paradoxically increase the choice of a less appealing alternative (studying). Core messages rescue people from decision paralysis by reminding them of priorities.
5. **Schemas and analogies compress meaning.** A "pomelo" described as "a supersized grapefruit with a thick, soft rind" is instantly understood by leveraging the grapefruit schema. High-concept pitches ("*Jaws* on a spaceship") and generative analogies (Disney's "cast members") pack enormous meaning into tiny packages by referencing what the audience already knows. See [[representativeness]].

## Detailed Notes

### A. Commander's Intent (pp. 33-37)

- Every Army operation begins with an order traceable to the president, cascading through Joint Chiefs, generals, colonels, and captains — plans specify "scheme of maneuver," "concept of fires," equipment, and specific actions
- "No plan survives contact with the enemy" — Colonel Tom Kolditz, West Point behavioral sciences
- In the 1980s, the Army adapted by creating [[commanders-intent]] — a crisp statement of the operation's goal appearing at the top of every order
- CI at high levels may be abstract ("Break the will of the enemy in the Southeast region"); at tactical levels it is concrete ("My intent is to have Third Battalion on Hill 4305, cleared of enemy, so we can protect the flank of Third Brigade")
- CI never specifies so much detail that it risks being made obsolete by unpredictable events
- "You can lose the ability to execute the original plan, but you never lose the responsibility of executing the intent"
- **Key insight:** CI aligns behavior without play-by-play instructions. When people know the destination, they improvise the route.

### B. Southwest Airlines: Finding the Core (pp. 38-39)

- Southwest's CI is "We are THE low-fare airline" — Herb Kelleher's core idea that guided thirty years of employee decisions
- Kelleher's parable: Marketing suggests chicken Caesar salad on the Houston-to-Las Vegas flight. Response: "Will it help us become THE low-fare airline? No? Then we're not serving any damn chicken salad."
- The core idea works as concentric circles: center = "THE low-fare airline"; next ring = "Have fun at work." Employees understand hierarchy of priorities
- "Simple" in this chapter doesn't mean easy words — it means elegance and prioritization, not dumbing down

### C. Burying the Lead (pp. 39-41)

- News reporters are taught to start with the lead — the most important information. The inverted pyramid puts the most important info at the top, decreasing in importance
- The inverted pyramid may have originated during the Civil War, when reporters using military telegraphs could be cut off at any moment
- "Burying the lead" = letting the most important element slip too far down in the story structure
- Finding a lead and finding the core both involve *forced prioritization*: "There's only one lead, and there's only one core. You must choose."
- Ed Cray (USC journalism professor): "The longer you work on a story, the more you can find yourself losing direction. No detail is too small. You just don't know what your story is anymore."

### D. "If You Say Three Things, You Don't Say Anything" (pp. 41-42)

- James Carville wrote three phrases on a whiteboard for the Clinton campaign of 1992; one was "It's the economy, stupid"
- The word "stupid" was a taunt to campaign workers — a self-effacing reminder not to get too clever
- Clinton himself had to be restrained from talking about balanced budgets: "There has to be message triage"
- Core messages rescue people from decision paralysis by providing a clear priority

### E. Decision Paralysis (pp. 43-45)

- L. J. Savage's "sure-thing principle" (1954): a businessman who would buy property regardless of which party wins the election buys it now even without knowing the outcome
- [[tversky]] and Eldar Shafir showed the sure-thing principle doesn't always hold — uncertainty *per se* can paralyze even when it's irrelevant to the decision
- Students told they passed an exam chose Hawaii (57%); told they failed chose Hawaii (54%); told results unknown: 61% paid $5 to wait — even though they'd go regardless
- Donald Redelmeier study: adding a second good alternative (foreign film) alongside a lecture *doubled* the number of students who chose to study (21% to 40%) — two good options made people less likely to choose either
- Prioritization rescues people from "the quicksand of decision angst"

### F. Idea Clinics: Sun Exposure (pp. 46-49)

- Ohio State University health educators needed to communicate sun-damage risks
- Message 1 buried the lead — started with tanning as a status symbol and mechanics of melanin before reaching the core: "skin damage is cumulative and irreversible"
- Message 2 unburied the lead, added an analogy (sun damage is like aging), and introduced an unexpected/concrete image (sunburns as signals of deeper damage)
- Scorecard: Message 2 earned checks for Simple, Unexpected, and Concrete

### G. Names, Names, and Names (pp. 50-52)

- The *Daily Record* in Dunn, North Carolina has 112% community penetration — the highest of any newspaper in the country
- Publisher Hoover Adams's Commander's Intent: "Names, names, and names" — relentlessly local focus
- Adams communicates his core with vivid, extreme statements: "I'd happily hire two more typesetters and add two more pages in every edition if we had the names to fill them up"
- "If an atomic bomb fell on Raleigh, it wouldn't be news in Benson unless some of the debris and ashes fell on Benson"
- Finding the core is not the same as communicating it — Adams both found and shared the core brilliantly

### H. Simple = Core + Compact (pp. 53-54)

- Compactness alone is insufficient — a pithy slogan can be compact without being core ("Goats like sprouts")
- Compactness can seem like dumbing down, especially for experts deep in nuance — this is the [[curse-of-knowledge]] at work
- The goal is ideas that are both compact AND core — what Cervantes called "short sentences drawn from long experience"
- Adams's "Names, names, and names" is a journalistic proverb: concrete enough for anyone to use, core enough to guide thousands of decisions across fifty-five years

### I. The Palm Pilot Wood Block (pp. 55-56)

- Jeff Hawkins fought feature creep by carrying a wooden block the size of the Palm Pilot everywhere
- Whenever someone suggested a new feature, he'd pull out the block and ask where it would fit
- "The Palm Pilot became a successful product almost because it was defined more in terms of what it was not than in terms of what it was" — Trae Vassallo, Palm V design team
- Parallel to the Clinton campaign: both teams had brilliant people with the *desire* to do too much, and needed a simple reminder to fight scope creep
- "When you say three things, you say nothing. When your remote control has fifty buttons, you can't change the channel anymore."

### J. Using What's There: Schemas (pp. 57-58)

- Memory exercise: "J FKFB INAT OUP SNA SAI RS" vs. "JFK FBI NATO UPS NASA IRS" — same letters, dramatically different recall
- The letters become easier to remember when grouped into known acronyms because we're not "lifting" the letters — we're posting a single flag on existing memory terrain
- To pack meaning into compact messages, tap the existing memory terrain of your audience — use what's already there

### K. The Pomelo Schema (pp. 62-64)

- Explanation 1 (from scratch): "A pomelo is the largest citrus fruit. The rind is very thick but soft and easy to peel away..." — you might remember "fruit" or "citrus fruit"
- Explanation 2 (schema-based): "A pomelo is basically a supersized grapefruit with a very thick and soft rind" — you remember "grapefruit" and can infer properties (blends with OJ, yellow-pink)
- Schemas are collections of generic properties associated with a concept or category — "sports car" instantly evokes small, fast, red, two-door, convertible
- Schemas *enable* profound simplicity — they let you convey complexity by layering on existing knowledge
- Good teachers intuitively use schemas: economics teachers start with "I grow apples and you grow oranges" to teach trade

### L. Complexity from Simplicity (pp. 65-66)

- Schemas help create complex messages from simple materials — pulleys, inclines, and frictionless surfaces in physics; the solar system model of the atom
- Sometimes schemas trade accuracy for accessibility (electrons don't orbit like planets — they move in "probability clouds"), but accessibility at the expense of accuracy is often better than accuracy at the expense of uselessness
- "An accurate but useless idea is still useless"
- The [[curse-of-knowledge]] drives the preference for accuracy — "maximizing shareholder value" is accurate for a CEO but useless for a flight attendant deciding whether to serve chicken salad

### M. High-Concept Pitches (pp. 67-69)

- Hollywood uses high-concept pitches as core ideas: *Speed* = "Die Hard on a bus"; *13 Going on 30* = "Big for girls"; *Alien* = "Jaws on a spaceship"
- These pitches use schemas (existing movies) to compress enormous amounts of meaning into five words
- "Die Hard on a bus" instantly generates dozens of decisions: action director, $100M budget, big star, summer release
- Production designer for *Alien* hears "Jaws on a spaceship" and immediately knows: not the cool, immaculate Enterprise from Star Trek — it's rushed, slapdash, claustrophobic, sweaty. The ship is dingy and oppressive.
- High-concept pitches are Hollywood's version of proverbs — they tap existing schemas to accelerate understanding

### N. Generative Analogies (pp. 70-72)

- Some analogies are so useful they become platforms for novel thinking — "generative" metaphors (term from psychologist Donald Schon)
- Disney calls employees "cast members" — a generative analogy applied consistently throughout the organization: interviews are auditions, jobs are performances, uniforms are costumes, visitors are guests
- The analogy generates predictions about appropriate behavior: cast members don't take cigarette breaks in costume; street sweepers answer park questions because they're part of the performance
- Contrast with Subway's "sandwich artists" — the evil twin of Disney's analogy. The defining trait of an "artist" is individual expression, which is exactly what Subway does not want its employees to exhibit
- Generative metaphors and proverbs substitute something easy to think about for something difficult, providing guidance in complex situations

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The tension between accuracy and accessibility is a recurring theme. The [[curse-of-knowledge]] pushes experts toward accuracy at the expense of understanding. The Heaths argue that accessibility should generally win — an accurate but useless idea is still useless. This connects to [[framing-effects]]: the same information framed differently (37 grams of fat vs. a day's worth of fatty foods) produces dramatically different responses.
