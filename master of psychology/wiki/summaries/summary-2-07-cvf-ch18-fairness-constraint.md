# Fairness as a Constraint on Profit Seeking: Entitlements in the Market — Summary

**Author(s):** [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]], Jack L. Knetsch, and [[thaler|Richard H. Thaler]]
**Type:** Research paper (telephone survey study)
**Published:** Originally in *The American Economic Review*, Vol. 76, No. 4, 1986, pp. 728--41. Reprinted as Ch. 18 in *Choices, Values, and Frames* (2000).
**Phase:** 2 — Decision Science
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Community standards of fairness constrain profit seeking.** Firms do not simply maximize short-run profits; perceptions of fairness from customers, tenants, and employees impose real economic constraints on pricing and wage-setting behavior.
2. **Dual entitlement is the governing principle.** Transactors are entitled to the terms of the reference transaction, and firms are entitled to their reference profit. Fairness violations occur when a firm increases its profit by imposing a loss on a transactor.
3. **Cost increases may be passed on; demand increases may not be exploited.** The public considers it acceptable for a firm to raise prices to protect its profit when costs rise, but unacceptable to raise prices merely because demand increases -- even though both are equally rational from an economics perspective.
4. **Framing and loss aversion shape fairness judgments.** Actions coded as imposing losses on transactors (wage cuts, price increases above reference) are judged more harshly than economically equivalent actions coded as reducing gains (eliminating bonuses, canceling discounts). This is a direct application of [[loss-aversion]] to social judgments.
5. **Fairness is enforced through willingness to punish.** People will incur personal costs to avoid or punish firms perceived as unfair -- switching to a less convenient store, rejecting offers in [[ultimatum-game]] experiments, and preferring to share money with fair strangers over unfair ones.

## Detailed Notes

### Method: Telephone Surveys

Data were collected between May 1984 and July 1985 via telephone surveys of randomly selected residents of Toronto and Vancouver. Equal numbers of adult female and male respondents were interviewed for about ten minutes during evening hours. No more than five questions concerned with fairness were included in any interview, and contrasting questions (variants of the same scenario) were never put to the same respondents. Respondents rated actions on a four-point scale: Completely Fair, Acceptable, Unfair, Very Unfair. Results are reported as the proportion judging an action "acceptable" (combining Completely Fair and Acceptable) vs. "unfair" (combining Unfair and Very Unfair).

### I. Reference Transactions

A central concept is the **reference transaction** -- a relevant precedent characterized by a reference price or wage and a positive reference profit. Fairness judgments are made relative to this baseline.

**What sets the reference transaction:**
- When there is a history of transactions between firm and transactor, the most recent price, wage, or rent becomes the reference (unless explicitly temporary)
- For new transactions, prevailing competitive prices or wages provide the natural reference
- The reference transaction derives its force from being *normal*, not necessarily from being *just* -- psychological adaptation means any stable state of affairs tends to become accepted

**Question 2A** (N=98): A photocopying shop employee earns $9/hr. Unemployment rises. Other shops hire at $7/hr. The owner cuts the employee's wage to $7. **17% Acceptable, 83% Unfair.** The existing wage is the reference; cutting it exploits the employee.

**Question 2B** (N=125): Same scenario, but the current employee leaves and the owner hires a replacement at $7/hr. **73% Acceptable, 27% Unfair.** A new worker does not inherit the prior employee's entitlement to $9/hr.

**Question 3** (N=94): A house painter pays assistants $9/hr, switches to landscaping where the going wage is $7/hr, and reduces their wages to $7/hr. **63% Acceptable, 37% Unfair.** A change in the line of business creates a new reference transaction.

Parallel results for residential tenancy: a rent increase judged fair for a new lease may be unfair for a renewal. However, the fairness obligation to absorb opportunity costs is narrowly defined -- few respondents object to a landlord *selling* to another landlord who intends to raise rents.

### II. The Coding of Outcomes

Fairness judgments depend heavily on whether outcomes are coded as gains or losses relative to the reference transaction:

- **An action by a firm is more likely to be judged unfair if it causes a loss to its transactor than if it cancels or reduces a possible gain.**
- **An action by a firm is more likely to be judged unfair if it achieves a gain for the firm than if it averts a loss.**

This directly parallels [[loss-aversion]] and [[reference-dependence]] in individual choice.

#### The Money Illusion in Fairness

**Question 4A** (N=125): A company in a community with no inflation and substantial unemployment cuts wages 7%. **38% Acceptable, 62% Unfair.**

**Question 4B** (N=129): Same company, but with 12% inflation, increases wages only 5%. **78% Acceptable, 22% Unfair.**

The real income change is approximately the same (-7% vs. -7% in real terms), but fairness judgments are strikingly different. A nominal wage cut is coded as a loss; a below-inflation raise is coded as a gain (relative to the nominal reference wage). This demonstrates how [[money-illusion]] interacts with fairness perception.

#### Losses vs. Eliminated Gains -- Prices

**Question 5A** (N=130): A dealer has been selling a popular car at list price. A shortage develops. The dealer now charges $200 above list price. **29% Acceptable, 71% Unfair.**

**Question 5B** (N=123): Same dealer has been selling the car at a $200 discount. Now sells at list price (eliminating the discount). **58% Acceptable, 42% Unfair.**

The $200 price increase is identical in both cases, but eliminating a discount is more acceptable than imposing a surcharge (chi-squared=20.91). In 5A the increase is clearly a loss relative to list price; in 5B the reference price is ambiguous between the discounted price and list price.

#### Losses vs. Eliminated Gains -- Wages

**Question 6A** (N=100): Declining business; owners reduce wages by 10%. **39% Acceptable, 61% Unfair.**

**Question 6B** (N=98): Declining business; owners eliminate a 10% annual bonus. **80% Acceptable, 20% Unfair.**

Eliminating a bonus (reducing a gain) is far more acceptable than cutting base wages (imposing a loss), even though the economic impact on workers is identical.

### III. Occasions for Pricing Decisions

Three classes of occasions for reconsidering exchange terms: (i) profit reductions (rising costs, decreased demand), (ii) profit increases (efficiency gains, reduced costs), (iii) increases in market power (excess demand).

#### A. Protecting Profit -- Passing on Cost Increases

Respondents overwhelmingly endorse the fairness of passing on cost increases. Firms are entitled to protect their reference profit.

**Question 7** (N=101): Transportation mixup causes shortage; grocer's wholesale lettuce price rises 30 cents/head. Grocer raises retail price by 30 cents. **79% Acceptable, 21% Unfair.**

**Question 8** (N=151): Landlord's costs increase substantially; raises rent to cover when lease is up for renewal, even though tenant is on a fixed income and would have to move. **75% Acceptable, 25% Unfair.** Fairness rules do not correspond to norms of charity or distributional concerns.

**Question 9A** (N=195): Company is making money. Severe unemployment. Cuts workers' wages by 5%. **23% Acceptable, 77% Unfair.**

**Question 9B** (N=195): Same scenario but the company has been losing money. Cuts workers' wages by 5%. **68% Acceptable, 32% Unfair.** The firm's profitability dramatically shifts acceptability.

**Mr. Green scenario** (open-ended format): A gardener (Mr. Green) who employs two workers at $7/hr learns equally competent workers are available at $6/hr. When business was "doing poorly," 67% of respondents said replacing or bargaining for lower wages was fair; when business was "doing well," only 25% said it was fair. The proportion guessing Mr. Green *would* try to reduce labor costs was 75% (doing poorly) vs. 49% (doing well).

Costs must pertain directly to the transaction. It is unfair for a landlord to raise rent to make up for losses on another property. But 62% found it acceptable for a landlord to charge higher rent in one of two identical buildings because it had a more costly foundation -- the cost is assigned to the specific good.

**Question 10** (N=147): Grocer raises price of existing peanut butter stock immediately upon learning wholesale price has increased. **21% Acceptable, 79% Unfair.** Naive accounting uses FIFO; raising price on old stock is judged by the old wholesale cost, not replacement cost.

#### B. The Allocation of Gains -- Cost Decreases

A strict cost-plus rule would require price reductions when costs decline. The dual-entitlement view predicts asymmetry: retaining cost savings does not violate the transactor's entitlement.

**Question 11A** (N=102): Factory's costs decrease by $40. Factory reduces table price by $20 (passing on half). **79% Acceptable, 21% Unfair.**

**Question 11B** (N=100): Same scenario but factory does not change price at all (retains entire $40 savings). **53% Acceptable, 47% Unfair.**

About half of respondents accept the firm keeping all the savings. The rules of fairness permit a firm not to share losses it imposes, without imposing an unequivocal duty to share gains.

#### C. Exploitation of Increased Market Power

Respondents strongly oppose exploiting increased market power:

**Question 1** (the famous snow shovel question, N=107): Hardware store raises price of snow shovels from $15 to $20 the morning after a blizzard. **18% Acceptable, 82% Unfair.** This is the paper's opening and most iconic example.

**Question 12** (N=102): Severe shortage of Red Delicious apples. Grocer raises price by 25% (at regular wholesale cost). **37% Acceptable, 63% Unfair.** Even when close substitutes are available and the item is not essential, price rationing is opposed. Similar aversion to price rationing held for luxury items -- a majority thought it unfair for a popular restaurant to impose a $5 Saturday night surcharge.

**Question 13** (N=101): Grocery chain has no competition in one community; sets prices 5% higher there despite identical costs and volume. **24% Acceptable, 76% Unfair.** Insensitive to the extent of the increase (10% and 15% versions yielded similar results).

**Question 14** (N=157): Landlord learns tenant has taken a job close to the house, unlikely to move; raises rent $40/month more than planned. **9% Acceptable, 91% Unfair.** Exploiting an individual's special dependence is *exceptionally* offensive.

**Question 15** (N=101): Store auctions off a discovered Cabbage Patch doll. **26% Acceptable, 74% Unfair.** But when told "the proceeds will go to UNICEF," unfairness dropped from 74% to 21%.

**Question 16** (N=154): Business in high-unemployment community asks four qualified candidates to bid down their salary. **36% Acceptable, 64% Unfair.**

**The auction rule:** Goods for which an active resale market exists (paintings, houses, securities) can be sold by auction freely. When resale is realistic, the purchaser is not perceived as sustaining a loss. But for consumer goods, dinner reservations, jobs, and tickets, auctions are considered unfair.

### IV. Enforcement

Three mechanisms deter firms from violating fairness standards:

1. **Reputation and trust:** Unfair dealing drives transactors to alternatives.
2. **Willingness to punish at personal cost:** Demonstrated via [[ultimatum-game]] experiments (Guth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze, 1982). In the authors' more detailed study (1986), a majority of participants were willing to forsake $2 rather than accept an unfair allocation of $10. In another experiment, 75% chose to share $10 evenly with a stranger who had been fair, rather than share $12 with an unfair allocator.
3. **Intrinsic motivation to be fair:** Survey respondents believed unenforced compliance with fairness norms is common.

**Telephone survey evidence on punishment:**
- 68% said they would switch to a drugstore five minutes further away if the closer one had raised prices when a competitor was temporarily forced to close
- 69% would switch if the more convenient store discriminated against older workers

**Tipping as evidence of intrinsic fairness:**

**Question 17A** (N=122): Expected tip for $10 meal at a restaurant visited frequently. Mean = $1.28.

**Question 17B** (N=124): Same meal at a restaurant in another city, never to be visited again. Mean = $1.27. Virtually no difference, suggesting enforcement opportunity is not a significant factor in tipping behavior.

**Mechanic scenario (Questions 18A/18B):** Mechanic discovers an expensive part replacement is unnecessary; a cheap repair would do. Expected to "make more money by replacing the part": 60% for regular customer, 63% for tourist. Expected to "save the customer money": 40% for regular customer, 37% for tourist. Mean estimate of how many out of 10 mechanics would repair fairly: 3.62 (regular customer), 3.72 (tourist). Respondents expect a tourist and regular customer to be treated alike despite the obvious enforcement difference.

### V. Economic Consequences

The paper derives four propositions about the effects of fairness on market behavior:

**A. Fairness in Customer Markets**

**Proposition 1:** *When excess demand in a customer market is unaccompanied by increases in suppliers' costs, the market will fail to clear in the short run.* Evidence from Phillip Cagan (1979) and a detailed case of Standard Oil of California during the 1920 gasoline shortage -- SOCal imposed allocation/rationing rather than raising prices, despite no government price controls, because it was "clearly concerned with their public image."

**Proposition 2:** *When a single supplier provides a family of goods for which there is differential demand without corresponding variation of input costs, shortages of the most valued items will occur.* Supported by sports/entertainment ticket pricing where popular events have excess demand; resort hotels face excess demand during peak weeks despite in-season/off-season pricing.

**Proposition 3:** *Price changes will be more responsive to variations of costs than to variations of demand, and more responsive to cost increases than to cost decreases.* Well-documented that prices are more sensitive to short-run cost variations than demand variations. The prediction of asymmetric price rigidity (faster upward adjustment of import prices than downward) is testable via exchange rate changes.

**Proposition 4:** *Price decreases will often take the form of discounts rather than reductions in the list or posted price.* Strongly supported by Stigler and Kindahl's data. Discounts have the advantage that their subsequent cancellation elicits less resistance than an increase in posted price. A temporary surcharge is especially aversive because it can only be coded as a loss.

**B. Fairness in Labor Markets**

The same rules apply to wages. Wage stickiness is predicted even in occupations offering no long-term employment prospects. The dual-entitlement model differs from a Keynesian model of sticky wages by allowing nominal wage cuts when a firm is losing money or threatened with bankruptcy. Wage changes in a firm will be more sensitive to recent firm profits than to local labor market conditions.

The model predicts: salary dispersion within occupations will be insufficient to clear the labor market (excess demand for the most valued, excess supply of the least valued). Firms that frame compensation as bonuses or profit sharing will encounter less resistance to reductions during slack periods. Adjustments of real wages will be substantially greater in inflationary periods (nominal cuts are avoided because they are coded as losses).

The discussion concludes that perceptions of transactors' entitlements affect the substantive outcomes of exchanges and that firms have an incentive to frame the terms of exchanges so as to make them appear "fair."

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Standard economics vs. fairness constraints:** The paper directly challenges the assumption that firms set prices solely by supply and demand. Fairness acts as a friction that prevents market clearing, contradicts perfect price discrimination, and produces asymmetric price rigidity.
- **Self-interest vs. intrinsic fairness:** The evidence on tipping and mechanic scenarios suggests firms and individuals sometimes act fairly even when enforcement is impossible, challenging the pure self-interest assumption.
- **Cost-plus rule vs. dual entitlement:** The data reject a simple cost-plus rule of fair pricing. Firms are not required to pass on cost decreases to consumers, creating an asymmetry between cost increases (must be absorbed or passed through proportionally) and cost decreases (may be retained).
