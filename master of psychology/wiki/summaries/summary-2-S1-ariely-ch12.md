# Predictably Irrational Ch.12: The Cycle of Distrust — Summary

**Author(s):** Dan Ariely
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Consumer distrust has grown so deep that people reject obviously true statements when attributed to companies or political parties, and even refuse free money
2. Trust is a public good — like clean air or water — and its erosion follows the same dynamics as the tragedy of the commons
3. Distrust from marketing information runs so deep it colors direct firsthand experience, causing people to enjoy products less
4. Trust, once eroded, is extremely hard to restore; a few bad actors poison the well for everyone

## Detailed Notes

### The "Free" Cable Trap
Ariely accepted an offer of "one month of free digital cable" and received a $60 bill. The customer service representative explained the terms were in seven-point font at the bottom of the ad. Researchers Marian Friestad and Peter Wright found that people increasingly understand that company offers are in the company's interest, not theirs — producing a paranoid, distrustful mindset.

### Free Money Experiment (Gneezy, Spiller, and Ariely)
**Setup:** A booth at a commercial center in Cambridge, Massachusetts with a large sign reading "Free Money" and piles of bills ($1, $5, $10, $20, or $50 denominations).

**Results:**
- $1 offered: Only 1% of passersby stopped
- $5: A few more
- $50: Only 19% stopped and took a bill

**Behavior pattern:** People would warily approach, ask "Is this a trick?", "Is there something I have to sign?", "There must be a catch." Even after reassurance, they would take a bill hesitantly, hold it up waiting for something to happen, then walk away with increasing speed.

**Interpretation:** The vast majority believed it was some kind of scheme. When researchers approached people who had clearly seen the booth but walked past, respondents said they believed it was a trick. This represents deep evidence of mistrust — an economists' favorite claim is that there are no $100 bills on the sidewalk because someone would have already picked them up.

### The Tragedy of the Commons
**Origin:** Oxford professor William Forster Lloyd (1833) — medieval English parishes had common grazing land. When some farmers increased their animals beyond the sustainable limit, all livestock became malnourished.

**Modern applications:** Wild salmon overfishing, energy crisis, clean air/land/trees/water depletion. A small number of misbehaving entities can devastate shared resources.

### The Public Goods Game
**Setup:** Four players each get $10. They can put any amount into a group pot, which doubles and is split evenly among all four.

**Round 1:** Three players contribute $10 each. "Bernie" keeps his $10 and still gets $15 from the split. Bernie ends with $25; contributors get $15 each.

**Round 2:** Others contribute less ($4 each) because they don't trust Bernie. Bernie still free-rides. Contributors end with $12 each.

**Subsequent rounds:** Trust erodes completely. Nobody contributes. Everyone ends with their original $10 — but if they had cooperated, each could have had $20.

**Key insight:** Trust is infectious — but so is distrust. When people see defection (lying ads, scams), they start acting similarly. Trust deteriorates and everybody loses, including the original defectors.

### Erosion of Trust over Time
- Snake-oil salesmen (Rexall Americanitis Elixir, 1903) were mild offenders compared to modern ones
- "Wonder pills" that promise weight loss, hair regrowth, enhanced sex life — unsuspecting buyers get placebo benefits at best while dishonest purveyors erode overall trust
- Online dating: Both men and women cheat on profiles (men add height and income, women subtract weight). An honest 5'9" man is penalized because women assume he's really 5'7". This creates a race to the bottom.
- Resume inflation: "Fluent in statistical analysis" means took intro to stats. Situation is so severe Ariely says when research assistants show him their resumes, "I sometimes feel as though the projects we have worked on together are actually theirs and that I've been assisting them."

### How Deep Is Your Mistrust? (Gneezy, Spiller, and Ariely)
**Experiment 1:** People were asked whether "the sun is yellow" and "a camel is bigger than a dog" were true. When unattributed, 100% agreed. When attributed to Procter & Gamble, the Democratic Party, or the Republican Party, participants became more likely to question even these obviously true statements. ("Sure it's yellow, but it also has red spots on the surface and sometimes it looks white, so is it really *just* yellow?")

**Experiment 2 — Stereo evaluation:** University of Chicago students listened to a Cambridge Audio stereo for 30 minutes (Bach). Some read a product brochure attributed to Cambridge Audio; others read the identical brochure attributed to *Consumer Reports*.

**Result:** Those told the info came from *Consumer Reports* liked the stereo more and would pay ~$407 for it, vs. ~$282 for those who read the Cambridge Audio brochure. Marketing-sourced information, even when identical in content, reduces enjoyment of direct firsthand experience.

### Bait-and-Switch Cable Study
Ariely showed a "free digital cable" ad to hundreds of people. Very few were naive enough to expect it was truly free — most estimated the real cost at $10-$70. People with prior experience with that cable company estimated even higher costs. Past burns revise trust downward for all future interactions.

### Is There Hope?
- **Johnson & Johnson Tylenol crisis (1982):** Seven people died from cyanide-laced capsules. J&J halted production, withdrew all Tylenol, urged returns, provided replacement tablets — at a cost of over $100 million. Market share recovered within months. This transparency-and-sacrifice approach is still the gold standard but rarely replicated.
- **Comcast's digital care:** Frank Eliason searched internet for "Comcast" and "Comcrap" to find unhappy customers and responded before formal complaints. JetBlue, GM, Kodak, Dell, Domino's followed with Twitter monitoring.
- **Timberland:** CEO Jeff Swartz openly invested in reducing CO2 emissions, recycling, sustainable materials, fair employee treatment — not for economic return but for moral principles. This commitment device forces trustworthy behavior.
- **Transparency as commitment:** Companies that make themselves publicly vulnerable (open forums, warts and all) create incentives to behave well.

### A Moral Tale — The Boy Who Cried Wolf
**Two morals for markets:**
1. People forgive a bit of lying (they came when the boy cried wolf the first and second time)
2. Trust, once eroded, is very hard to restore (the third time, no one came, and the wolf ate the boy)

When market defectors spread false claims, distrust diffuses into everything. The 2008 banking crisis destroyed public trust; bailouts and Wall Street behavior only ground salt into the wounds.
