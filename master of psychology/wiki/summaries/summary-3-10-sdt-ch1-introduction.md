# Self-Determination Theory: An Introduction and Overview — Summary

**Author(s):** Richard M. Ryan and Edward L. Deci
**Type:** Book chapter (Ch. 1 in *Self-Determination Theory: Basic Psychological Needs in Motivation, Development, and Wellness*, 2017)
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **SDT is an organismic, empirical macro-theory of human motivation** that assumes humans are inherently active, growth-oriented beings who require satisfaction of three [[basic-psychological-needs]] -- [[autonomy-need]], [[competence-need]], and [[relatedness-need]] -- to thrive. When these needs are supported, people flourish; when thwarted, they show fragmentation, defensiveness, and psychopathology.

2. **Motivation is not a unitary concept -- it differs in type, not just amount.** SDT's central innovation is the [[controlled-vs-autonomous-motivation]] continuum. Autonomous motivation (intrinsic motivation, identified and integrated regulation) yields superior persistence, performance, creativity, and well-being compared to controlled motivation (external and introjected regulation). This fundamentally challenges expectancy-value and self-efficacy theories that treat motivation as varying only in quantity.

3. **The theory comprises six mini-theories**, each addressing a different aspect of motivation and personality: Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET), Organismic Integration Theory (OIT), Causality Orientations Theory (COT), Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT), Goal Contents Theory (GCT), and Relationships Motivation Theory (RMT). Together they form the formal propositions of SDT.

4. **Social contexts are the critical lever for motivation.** Environments that are (1) autonomy-supportive, (2) effectance-supporting, and (3) relationally supportive facilitate intrinsic motivation, internalization of extrinsic motivation, and well-being. Controlling, rejecting, or overly challenging environments produce the opposite -- controlled motivation, amotivation, and ill-being.

5. **SDT is not a relativistic theory -- it posits universal psychological needs** that operate across cultures, developmental periods, and domains. Like vitamin C, the functional effects of need satisfaction and frustration obtain regardless of whether individuals or their cultures consciously value those needs.

## Detailed Notes

### The Organismic, Empirical Approach

SDT is explicitly an *organismic perspective*, meaning it assumes humans have evolved to be inherently curious, physically active, and deeply social. Individual development is characterized by proactive engagement, assimilation of information, and behavioral regulation. SDT contrasts sharply with behaviorist and social-cognitive approaches that view the organism as essentially passive or reactive.

The two core active tendencies of human nature within SDT:
1. **[[intrinsic-motivation]]** -- the inherent propensity to explore, manipulate, and understand (discussed in Chapters 5, 6, and 7)
2. **Active internalization and integration** -- the propensity to assimilate social norms and regulations (discussed in Chapter 8)

SDT focuses on the conditions under which these developmental processes optimally proceed and how contexts can interfere with or compromise them.

The principle of *organization* in theoretical biology is central: living entities, under supportive conditions, progress toward increased differentiation and integration (Jacob, 1973; Kauffmann, 2000; Maturana & Varela, 1992; Mayr, 1982). SDT applies this principle to psychological development. This is consistent with cognitive-developmental perspectives (Werner, 1948; Piaget, 1971), humanistic psychology (Goldstein, 1939; Rogers, 1963), and psychodynamic approaches (Freud, 1923; Loevinger, 1976; White, 1963; Winnicott, 1965).

SDT is deliberately empirical. Unlike prior organismic theories, it places emphasis on explicit hypotheses, operational definitions, observational methods, and statistical inferences. Its theoretical propositions have been primarily formulated, sustained, and refined using empirical evidence from thousands of laboratory and field studies.

### A Psychological Theory

SDT is fundamentally a *psychological* theory -- it is concerned with behavior as a function of conscious or nonconscious motives. Psychological constructs (whether conscious or nonconscious) are considered the *regnant causes* of most intentional behaviors. The level of analysis most needed for understanding motivation and behavior change is the level encompassing the psychological processes operating within the individual and the variables within social contexts that activate or diminish those processes.

SDT draws on Heider's (1958) argument that it is *naive psychology* -- people's perceptions of their social environments -- that guides their behaviors and actions.

### Supporting and Impairing Human Development

SDT defines the self phenomenologically, following Heider (1958) and de Charms (1968). The self is focused on the experiences underlying autonomous actions -- those involving a sense of volition and self-endorsement -- rather than on self-concept, self-evaluations, or appraisals. Acting with a sense of autonomy requires integration, as experiences of full volition are characterized by lack of inner conflict and willing engagement.

Within each individual, there is a natural tendency toward growth and development -- an ongoing tendency toward [[organismic-integration]]. Yet this integrative propensity is *conditional*: it requires social and environmental support for persons to satisfy [[basic-psychological-needs]] -- the needs for [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], [[competence-need|competence]], and [[relatedness-need|relatedness]].

**Growth and Defense:** SDT begins with the assumption that human nature is deeply designed to be active and social and, when afforded a "good enough" (basic-need-supportive) environment, will move toward thriving, wellness, and integrity. Yet the same features that make people vulnerable to thriving also make them vulnerable to being derailed when environments are need-thwarting. Social contexts can be *basic need-thwarting*, with various developmental costs including defensive or compensatory strategies. When individuals experience need-thwarting environments (controlling, rejecting, critical, overly challenging), they become self-focused, defensive, amotivated, aggressive, and antisocial. According to SDT, manifest human nature is to a large degree *experience dependent*.

### Human Needs

SDT falls within the tradition that conceptualizes needs at the psychological level (alongside Baumeister & Leary, 1995; McClelland, Atkinson, Clark, & Lowell, 1953; Murray, 1938). However, SDT differs critically: it posits a *core set* of psychological needs that, like physiological needs, are universally essential for optimal functioning regardless of developmental epoch or cultural setting.

**Definition of needs in SDT:** Needs are specifically defined as *nutrients that are essential for growth, integrity, and well-being*. Basic physiological needs pertain to bodily health and safety. Basic psychological needs must be satisfied for psychological interest, development, and wellness to be sustained.

**The three basic psychological needs:**

1. **[[autonomy-need|Autonomy]]** -- the need to self-regulate one's experiences and actions. Autonomy is a form of functioning associated with feeling volitional, congruent, and integrated (de Charms, 1968; Friedman, 2003; Ryan, 1993; Shapiro, 1981). Autonomy is *not* independence or self-reliance -- it is the sense that one's behaviors are self-endorsed, congruent with authentic interests and values. When acting with autonomy, behaviors are engaged wholeheartedly; when lacking autonomy, one experiences incongruence and conflict. In SDT's view, only some intentional actions are truly self-regulated or autonomous; others are regulated by external forces or by relatively nonintegrated aspects of personality.

2. **[[competence-need|Competence]]** -- the basic need to feel effectance and mastery. People need to feel able to operate effectively within their important life contexts (Bandura, 1989; Deci, 1975; Harter, 2012; White, 1959). The need for competence is evident as an inherent striving, manifested in curiosity, manipulation, and a wide range of epistemic motives (Deci & Moller, 2005). Competence is readily thwarted when challenges are too difficult, negative feedback is pervasive, or feelings of mastery and effectiveness are diminished or undermined by person-focused criticism and social comparisons.

3. **[[relatedness-need|Relatedness]]** -- concerns feeling socially connected (Bowlby, 1979; Baumeister & Leary, 1995; Ryan, 1995). Relatedness is about belonging and feeling significant among others. Equally important is experiencing oneself as giving to or contributing to others -- what Angyal (1941) described as *homonomy*. People experience relatedness and belonging through both feeling connected to close others and being a significant member of social groups.

These three needs were initially identified functionally because they served well to integrate behavioral experiments on intrinsic motivation (Chapters 6 and 7) and the internalization of extrinsic regulations (Chapter 8). Need satisfaction is strongly linked with vitality, whereas need-frustration predicts motivational depletion.

**Needs as objective phenomena:** The three needs are said to be *objective* in that their deprivation or satisfaction has clear and measurable functional effects that obtain regardless of one's subjective goals or values. Whether or not one subjectively values vitamin C, extended deprivation will still lead to scurvy. Similarly, whether or not one's culture values autonomy, thwarting it will produce observable decrements in growth, integrity, and wellness.

### The Importance of Social Contexts

SDT characterizes social environments in terms of three dimensions:
1. **Autonomy supportive** (versus demanding and controlling)
2. **Effectance supporting** (versus overly challenging, inconsistent, or discouraging)
3. **Relationally supportive** (versus impersonal or rejecting)

Autonomy support includes affordances of choice and encouragement of self-regulation. Competence supports include provisions of structure and positive informational feedback. Relatedness supports include the caring involvement of others.

The same contextual supports that maintain intrinsic motivation also promote the internalization and integration of extrinsic motivations. While autonomy and competence are the main proximal factors in intrinsic motivation, relational supports play an invariant and far more salient role in the internalization of extrinsic motivation. People naturally tend to internalize the values and goals of those with whom they are or wish to be connected.

### Motivation and Self-Determination

#### The Differentiation of Motivation

SDT's central departure from prior motivation theories: motivation differs not just in *amount* but in *type*. SDT distinguishes between [[intrinsic-motivation]] and various forms of [[extrinsic-motivation]] based on the degree to which the regulation has been internalized.

**The [[controlled-vs-autonomous-motivation|autonomy-control continuum]]:** Varied types of motivation can be characterized by the extent to which they represent *autonomous* versus *controlled* regulations:

- **Autonomous motivation:** The person experiences volition -- assents to, concurs with, and is wholly willing to engage in the behaviors. Behaviors are experienced as emanating from one's *self*.
- **Controlled motivation:** The person feels externally or internally pressured or compelled to act. Motivations are based in feeling coerced by external persons or forces to act in ways incongruent with the person's sense of self.

**[[intrinsic-motivation|Intrinsic motivation]]:** Behaviors performed out of interest and for which the primary "reward" is the spontaneous feelings of effectance and enjoyment that accompany them. Intrinsically motivated behaviors are, by definition, autonomous -- experienced as volitional and emanating from one's self (de Charms, 1968).

**[[extrinsic-motivation|Extrinsic motivation]]:** Behaviors that are instrumental for some separable consequence (external reward, social approval, avoidance of punishment, or attainment of a valued outcome). Extrinsic motivation varies widely in its degree of autonomy:

- **External regulation** -- directly controlled by external contingencies of rewards and punishments. Relatively controlled.
- **Introjected regulation** -- the person has taken in but not fully accepted external controls. Motivated by guilt, shame, contingent self-esteem, and fear of disapproval. Internally controlling; associated with internal pressure, tension, and conflict. Related to [[want-should-conflict]] -- the tension between what one *wants* to do and what one feels one *should* do.
- **Identified regulation** -- motivated through one's identification with and acceptance of the value of the extrinsic behavior. More autonomous.
- **Integrated regulation** -- identifications have been integrated with one's other values and beliefs. Most autonomous form of extrinsic motivation. Experienced as volitional; quality of persistence and performance is higher than with controlled motives.

Each form of extrinsic motivation has its own dynamic causes, supports, and character. They are phenomenologically "ordered" in their degrees of autonomy (Ryan & Connell, 1989). The more autonomous the motivational form, the more the individual has access to organismic supports for acting, explaining the energetic, affective, and cognitive advantages of autonomy.

#### Amotivation

**Amotivation** describes people's lack of intentionality and motivation -- the extent to which they are passive, ineffective, or without purpose. Three types:
1. **Competence-based amotivation** -- feeling unable to effectively attain outcomes (universal helplessness or personal incompetence)
2. **Interest/value-based amotivation** -- lack of interest, relevance, or value; the activity fails to connect with need fulfillment
3. **Defiance-based amotivation** -- a motivated nonaction or oppositional behavior to defy demands that are thwarting autonomy or relatedness

### Motivation in Social Contexts

Autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, and amotivation are theorized to *mediate* between social contexts and outcomes such as effective performance and well-being. Social contexts that support all three psychological needs facilitate autonomous functioning, yielding more effective performance and greater wellness.

Factors that engender perceptions of being externally regulated and/or incompetent undermine intrinsic motivation. Factors that support perceptions of autonomy and feelings of competence maintain or enhance intrinsic motivation. These include: opportunities for choice, positive feedback, and acknowledgment of people's internal frame of reference. Additional research, particularly with children, showed that feelings of relational security are also necessary for curiosity and intrinsic exploration.

### SDT Applied to Life's Domains

Ryan and Deci detail SDT's application across multiple real-world domains:

**Schools and Learning:** Controlling motivational climates for learning foster external regulation and more impoverished, superficial learning. Autonomy-supportive school climates foster self-motivation, persistence, and quality of learning. Structure (scaffolding and support for competence) complements autonomy support.

**Workplace Motivation:** SDT research investigates managerial styles -- why some engender alienation and apathy while others produce committed and energized employees. Incentives, evaluations, and directives can backfire when they undermine autonomy (Gagne, Deci, & Ryan, 2017). This connects to [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]] -- Ariely's finding that introducing monetary incentives into social contexts can destroy intrinsic motivation.

**Sport and Exercise:** Coaching climates heavily impact athletes' enjoyment, persistence, and performance through their effects on autonomy and competence.

**Health Care and Psychotherapy:** SDT emphasizes conditions that lead not just to short-term behavior change but to change that becomes internalized. Autonomy-supportive practitioner interactions inspire behavior change more effectively than controlling approaches.

**Cultural and Religious Socialization:** SDT predicts that religious practices (Ryan, Rigby, & King, 1993) and cultural norms (Chirkov, Ryan, Kim, & Kaplan, 2003) can be externally regulated/introjected or more fully internalized and integrated. Greater integration produces positive effects on health and well-being.

**Virtual Worlds:** SDT explains how factors within media and game worlds enhance or detract from intrinsic motivation and how violence relates to psychological need satisfaction (Przybylski, Rigby, & Ryan, 2010).

### Fields of Psychology and SDT's Mini-Theories

The six mini-theories of SDT address different phenomena but together constitute the formal propositions of SDT:

1. **Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET)** -- formulated by Deci & Ryan (1980a). Describes how social environments influence (facilitate or undermine) [[intrinsic-motivation]] and performance. The first formal proposition set within SDT. Covers the effects of rewards, feedback, and other external events on intrinsic motivation. Discussed in Chapters 6 and 7.

2. **Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)** -- formulated by Ryan, Connell & Deci (1985). Concerns the development of [[extrinsic-motivation]] through the process of integration -- how extrinsically motivated behaviors become autonomous. Deals with internalization and integration of social and cultural regulations. Discussed in Chapter 8.

3. **Causality Orientations Theory (COT)** -- a personality/individual-difference approach. Three general causality orientations: the autonomy orientation, the controlled orientation, and the impersonal orientation -- paralleling autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, and amotivation at a more global level. Assessed via the General Causality Orientations Scale (Deci, 1985a). Discussed in Chapter 9.

4. **Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT)** -- details how the dynamics of [[basic-psychological-needs]] affect well-being across all levels of human development and across cultural backdrops. Need satisfaction predicts wellness; need thwarting explains psychopathology and physical health outcomes. Advanced by multilevel modeling research. Discussed in Chapter 10.

5. **Goal Contents Theory (GCT)** -- concerns people's goals and their relations to basic need satisfaction and wellness. Life goals fall into two categories: *intrinsic aspirations* (personal growth, meaningful relationships, community contributions) and *extrinsic aspirations* (wealth, fame, power, attractiveness). Intrinsic aspirations relate more directly to need satisfaction and well-being; extrinsic aspirations show weaker or negative relations to wellness. This connects to [[hyperbolic-discounting]] -- extrinsic goals often involve short-term contingent satisfactions at the cost of long-term well-being. Discussed in Chapter 11.

6. **Relationships Motivation Theory (RMT)** -- the most recent mini-theory. Frames the finding that high-quality interpersonal relationships depend on individuals experiencing not only positivity/regard but also respect for autonomy. Relatedness fuels internalization of social practices and is reciprocally facilitated or undermined by autonomy. Discussed in Chapter 12.

### Between Biology and Culture

SDT positions itself between biological reductionism and cultural relativism. Ryan and Deci argue against:
- Reductionist neuroscientists who dismiss the self as merely "fanciful homunculi" (Hofstadter & Dennett, 1981, p. 12)
- Radical behaviorists who insist organisms are entirely controlled by environments (Cameron & Pierce, 1994; Skinner, 1953, 1971)
- Postmodernists and cultural relativists who portray self and autonomy as merely Western intellectual preoccupations (Gergen, 1991; Markus & Kitayama, 1991a)

SDT argues that the phenomenal senses of self and autonomy have a direct relationship to the organization of behavior and are emergent properties of reflective processing and regulation. Autonomous actions are biologically distinct from controlled behaviors, and both are dependent on specific mechanisms (Ryan, Kuhl, Deci, 1997). The degree of autonomy in behavioral regulation has enormous ramifications for performance, persistence, and well-being.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **SDT vs. expectancy-value theories (Bandura, Vroom):** Traditional cognitive theories of motivation treat motivation as a unitary quantity (more vs. less). SDT fundamentally rejects this, arguing that *type* of motivation matters more than *amount*. This will be critical to examine when ingesting Bandura's self-efficacy work in Phase 3.
- **SDT vs. cultural relativism:** SDT's claim that autonomy is universally essential clashes with cultural psychology's position (Markus & Kitayama) that autonomy is a Western construct. SDT explicitly argues this is a mischaracterization -- autonomy (as volition) is not independence, and it is as relevant for collectivists as individualists.
- **SDT and [[illusion-of-control]]:** Langer's illusion of control concept and de Charms's concept of personal causation (which SDT builds on) address different levels of the same phenomenon. SDT would argue that the desire for personal causation is a genuine need, not an illusion, though people can certainly be mistaken about the *object* of their control.
- **Rewards and motivation:** SDT's finding that extrinsic rewards can *undermine* intrinsic motivation (the "overjustification effect") directly connects to and supports [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]] -- Ariely's demonstration that monetary incentives destroy social-norm-based motivation.
