# Difficult Conversations Ch.3: Don't Assume They Meant It — Summary

**Author(s):** Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton, Sheila Heen
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Intentions strongly influence our judgments.** We react differently to being blocked by an ambulance versus a BMW. But in difficult conversations, we routinely assume we know the other person's intentions when we actually don't.
2. **Two key mistakes drive the battle over intentions.** Lori's mistake: assuming she knows Leo's intentions (she doesn't). Leo's mistake: assuming that once he clarifies his good intentions, Lori should no longer feel hurt (she still does).
3. **We assume intentions from impact — and assume the worst.** When we feel hurt, we automatically conclude the other person intended to hurt us. This leap from "I was hurt" to "you intended to hurt me" is so fast that we don't even realize it's an assumption. And we consistently assume the worst: that traffic cop is a "control freak," that boss is "manipulative."
4. **We treat ourselves more charitably than others.** When your husband forgets the dry cleaning, he's irresponsible. When you forget to book airline tickets, you're overworked. This asymmetry — charitable self-attribution, hostile other-attribution — is the [[fundamental-attribution-error]] in action.
5. **Good intentions don't sanitize bad impact.** Even if Leo was genuinely trying to help Lori with her diet, she was still hurt and embarrassed. His good intentions don't erase her pain. The father who misses his son's basketball game because of work may not intend to hurt his son, but the son still hurts.

## Detailed Notes

### The Battle Over Intentions

Lori and Leo's recurring fight illustrates the intention trap. At a party, Leo told Lori to "lay off the ice cream." Lori, who struggles with her weight, was humiliated. Later:
- Lori accuses Leo of trying to control or put her down
- Leo says he was just trying to help her stick to her diet
- Lori hears this as dismissal of her pain
- Leo hears her accusation as an attack on his character
- Both feel misunderstood; the cycle repeats endlessly

### The First Mistake: Our Assumptions About Intentions Are Often Wrong

**We assume intentions from impact on us.** We feel hurt, therefore they intended to hurt us. We feel slighted, therefore they intended to slight us. This leap is so automatic we don't recognize it as an assumption.

**We assume the worst.** Margaret, recovering from hip surgery, imagined her doctor vacationing in the Caribbean with his wife or girlfriend when he extended his absence. In reality, he was setting up a hospital in Bosnia. Knowing his true motivation — generous, not selfish — substantially changed how she felt about waiting.

**We treat ourselves more charitably.** When we're the ones acting, we know our intentions aren't malicious — we're wrapped up in our own worries, often unaware of any negative impact. But when we're acted upon, our story "too easily slides into one about bad intentions and bad character."

**We jump from bad intentions to bad character.** Perhaps the biggest danger: "they had bad intentions" quickly becomes "they are a bad person." This character judgment colors every subsequent interaction and becomes a lens through which all future behavior is interpreted.

**Accusations create defensiveness.** When we express our assumption about their intentions as an accusatory question ("How come you wanted to hurt me?"), we trigger a defensive reaction. From their point of view, they are defending themselves against a false accusation. Both parties end up feeling victimized. "Those are the two classic characteristics of the cycle: both parties think they are the victim, and both think they are acting only to defend themselves."

**Attributions become self-fulfilling.** If you think your boss doesn't trust you, you feel demotivated, your work suffers, and your boss actually does start worrying — confirming your initial (originally wrong) assumption.

### The Second Mistake: Good Intentions Don't Sanitize Bad Impact

**We don't hear what they are really trying to say.** When someone says "You intended to hurt me," they are communicating two things: (1) I think you had bad intentions, and (2) I got hurt. When we're accused, we focus on defending against message 1 and completely miss message 2 — the hurt.

**The important distinction: "intended to hurt" vs. "didn't care enough."** The father who misses his son's basketball game for work didn't intend to hurt his son. But his desire not to hurt was weaker than his desire to work. From the son's perspective, "He didn't want to hurt me, but he didn't make me a priority" is the real complaint, and "I didn't intend to hurt you" doesn't address it.

**We ignore the complexity of human motivations.** Leo's intentions in the ice cream incident are probably mixed. Maybe he was trying to help, but also maybe he was embarrassed by Lori's eating, or wanted to see her as under control. Intentions are rarely purely "good" or "bad." The willingness to investigate one's own complex motivations sends a profoundly positive message about the importance of the relationship.

**This dynamic is especially destructive between groups.** The newspaper example: African American and Hispanic reporters complained about exclusion. White editors met behind closed doors to discuss inclusion strategies. Minority reporters felt excluded *again*. The editors clarified their good intentions, expecting the issue to be resolved. But the impact — feeling excluded — still stood, regardless of intent.

### Avoiding the Two Mistakes

**Avoiding Mistake 1: Disentangle Impact and Intent.** Three diagnostic questions:
1. **Actions:** What did the other person actually say or do?
2. **Impact:** What was the impact of this on me?
3. **Assumption:** Based on this impact, what assumption am I making about their intentions?

The key move: hold your assumption about their intentions as a hypothesis, not a certainty. Share the impact on you and *inquire* about their intentions, rather than asserting what they intended.

The improved Lori-Leo conversation: Lori begins with "When you said 'lay off the ice cream,' I felt hurt" (impact) and "I wonder whether you said it on purpose to embarrass me" (hypothesis about intent, not accusation). This opens the conversation rather than closing it.

**Don't pretend you don't have a hypothesis.** The authors are not suggesting you hide your assumptions or pretend you have none. Instead, share them as guesses — "this is what I'm thinking when it happens" — for the purpose of testing whether they make sense.

**Avoiding Mistake 2: Listen for Feelings, Reflect on Your Intentions.**

- **Listen past the accusation for the feelings.** When accused of bad intentions, remember: the accusation always contains two parts — the attribution of intent *and* the expression of hurt. Acknowledge the hurt first. Returning to intentions after feelings are acknowledged makes the conversation substantially easier.
- **Be open to reflecting on the complexity of your intentions.** Resist the reflex of "My intentions were pure." They rarely are. Leo's willingness to wonder if perhaps he was embarrassed, or if he saw Lori as "out of control," transforms the conversation from defensive combat into genuine inquiry.

The chapter's rewritten Lori-Leo dialogue demonstrates both people applying both fixes: Lori leads with impact, Leo acknowledges the hurt, then explores his own complex motivations. Leo discovers something about himself ("I could have seen you as out of control, which is a big issue for me"), Lori acknowledges her own contribution ("I probably was a little out of control"), and they agree to discuss what Leo *should* do in similar future situations.

## New Concepts Introduced

- [[intent-impact-gap]] — The systematic difference between what someone intended and the impact they had; and the error of inferring intent from impact

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The "Intention Invention" maps directly onto the [[fundamental-attribution-error]]: attributing others' behavior to dispositional factors (bad character, bad intentions) rather than situational ones. The book addresses this without using the academic term, making it accessible but missing the connection to decades of attribution theory (Heider, Jones & Harris, Ross).
- The self-serving asymmetry in attribution (charitable to self, hostile to other) is closely related to the actor-observer bias from [[attribution-theory]]. When we act, we see situational constraints; when we observe, we see character.
- The self-fulfilling attribution dynamic connects to [[confirmation-bias]] and motivated reasoning from Phase 1.
