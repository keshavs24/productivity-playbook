# Never Split the Difference Ch.2: Be a Mirror — Summary

**Author(s):** Chris Voss, Tahl Raz
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 5
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Assumptions are the enemy of good negotiation.** Good negotiators prepare for surprises; great negotiators aim to reveal the surprises they are certain exist. Treat assumptions as hypotheses and use the negotiation to test them rigorously.
2. **Negotiation is not a battle of arguments -- it is a process of discovery.** People who view negotiation as argument become overwhelmed by the voices in their head. Quiet the voices by making your sole focus the other person and what they have to say.
3. **Slow. It. Down.** Going too fast is a cardinal error. Speed undermines rapport and makes people feel unheard. When you slow down, you calm things down. If someone is talking, they're not shooting.
4. **Your voice is your most powerful tool.** Three tones available: (a) the [[late-night-fm-dj-voice]] -- calm, slow, downward-inflecting, conveying authority without triggering defensiveness; (b) the positive/playful voice -- default mode, light and encouraging, smile while talking; (c) the direct/assertive voice -- rarely used, causes pushback.
5. **[[mirroring-technique]] is a Jedi mind trick.** Repeat the last 1-3 critical words of what someone just said. This triggers an instinct to elaborate, sustains connection, and reveals strategy. Waiters who mirrored got 70% higher tips than those who used positive reinforcement (Wiseman study). The four-step process: (1) Use the DJ voice, (2) Start with "I'm sorry...", (3) Mirror, (4) Silence (at least four seconds), (5) Repeat.
6. **Wants vs. needs:** Wants are aspirations (easy to talk about); needs imply survival (harder to surface). Neither is where you start -- you start with listening, validating emotions, and creating enough trust for a real conversation to begin.

## Detailed Notes

### I. The Chase Manhattan Bank Robbery (September 30, 1993)

Voss's first major hostage situation. Two masked robbers storm a Chase Manhattan Bank in Brooklyn. They pistol-whip the security guard, lock him in the men's room, and put a gun in a teller's mouth (pulling the trigger on an empty chamber). Voss and partner Charlie Beaudoin race to the scene. NYPD, FBI, SWAT all converge.

The lead hostage-taker (later identified as Chris Watts) runs a "counterintelligence operation" -- feeding misinformation, claiming many co-conspirators from different countries, trying to disguise his voice, constantly putting the phone on hold. In reality it was a two-man operation; Watts had tricked his partner Bobby Goodwin into what Bobby thought was an ATM burglary.

### II. Assumptions Blind, Hypotheses Guide

- Great negotiators question assumptions others accept on faith or arrogance
- Hold multiple hypotheses simultaneously about the situation, the counterpart's wants, the variables
- Use new information in the moment to test and winnow hypotheses
- Really smart people often make bad negotiators -- they think they have nothing to discover
- People make assumptions about others before meeting them, then ignore perceptions that contradict foregone conclusions

### III. Calm the Schizophrenic

- George A. Miller's research: we can only process ~7 pieces of information in our conscious mind at any given moment
- Negotiation as "battle of arguments" = four people talking at once (two real, two inner voices)
- **Solution:** Instead of prioritizing your argument, make your sole and all-encompassing focus the other person and what they have to say
- In that mode of true **active listening**, you disarm your counterpart and make them feel safe; the voice in their head quiets down
- The FBI used teams of 4-5 listeners: one on the phone, others analyzing mood, listening for clues, passing notes

### IV. Slow. It. Down.

- Joe (the NYPD negotiator) was using a half-MSU ("Making Shit Up"), half-sales approach -- trying to problem-solve too fast
- Going too fast makes people feel unheard and undermines rapport
- Research validates time as one of the most important negotiator tools
- When you slow the process down, you also calm it down
- "If someone is talking, they're not shooting"

### V. The Voice

After five hours, Voss replaces Joe on the phone. He switches into his **[[late-night-fm-dj-voice]]**: deep, soft, slow, reassuring. A downward-inflecting statement, not a question: "Joe's gone. This is Chris. You're talking to me now."

**Three voice tones for negotiators:**

1. **Late-night FM DJ voice** -- downward inflection, calm and slow. Conveys "I'm in control." Creates authority and trustworthiness without triggering defensiveness. Use selectively to make a point.
2. **Positive/playful voice** -- should be your default. Light, encouraging. Relax and smile while talking. A smile transmits tonally even over the phone. When people are in a positive frame, they think more quickly, are more likely to collaborate and problem-solve (instead of fight and resist).
3. **Direct/assertive voice** -- used rarely. Signals dominance; counterpart will push back aggressively or passive-aggressively. Like slapping yourself in the face while trying to make progress.

**Cross-cultural effectiveness:** These voices work universally. A Black Swan Group instructor's girlfriend got better deals than him at Istanbul spice markets because she approached each encounter with a playful smile.

### VI. Mirroring

**[[mirroring-technique]]** (also called isopraxism): repeating the last 1-3 critical words (or the critical one to three words) of what someone has just said. Not body language, not accent, not tone -- just the words.

**How it works:**
- Follows a biological principle: we fear what's different and are drawn to what's similar
- A mirror signals to another's unconscious: "You and I -- we're alike"
- Triggers an instinct to elaborate, sustain connection, reveal strategy
- It is "the closest one gets to a Jedi mind trick" in the FBI's skill set

**Research support:** Psychologist Richard Wiseman studied waiters. Those who mirrored customers' orders back (repeating them verbatim) received **70% higher tips** than those who used positive reinforcement ("Great choice!", "Sure!", "No problem!").

**Voss's use with Chris Watts:**
- "We chased your driver away?" -- mirrored Watts's blurt about the getaway driver
- This triggered a cascade of damaging admissions and information about an unknown accomplice
- Later: "No? You said 'okay.'" -- mirrored after Watts denied being Chris Watts; his voice went way up, he started blurting information

**The four-step mirroring process:**
1. Use the late-night FM DJ voice
2. Start with "I'm sorry..."
3. Mirror (repeat last 1-3 words)
4. Silence -- at least four seconds, let the mirror work its magic
5. Repeat

**Workplace application:** A student's boss demanded two copies of all paperwork from a consulting engagement (thousands of documents). She mirrored: "I'm sorry, two copies?" The boss elaborated, then self-corrected over two exchanges. A day later he emailed: "The two digital backups will be fine." A week of unnecessary work avoided.

### VII. The Resolution

Bobby Goodwin came out first with hands in the air. The female hostages were released unharmed one by one. Chris Watts eventually came out on his own -- still looking around as if he might escape, right up until the handcuffs clicked. Nobody was hurt. The bad guys were in custody.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Complements [[three-conversations-framework]] (Stone/Patton/Heen):** The emphasis on listening, slowing down, and making it about the other person aligns with the "learning stance" from *Difficult Conversations*. However, Voss's approach is more tactical and less dialogic -- he uses listening as a tool to extract information and shift the frame, not just to understand.
