# Guide to Managerial Communication Ch.1: Communication Strategy — Summary

**Author(s):** Mary Munter, Lynn Hamilton
**Source:** *Guide to Managerial Communication*, 10th Edition (Pearson, 2014), Chapter 1
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Managerial communication is circular, not linear.** Instead of thinking about communication as a straight line from sender to receiver, visualize it as a circle with your success measured by the desired audience response. The most brilliant message is worthless if it doesn't achieve the intended outcome.
2. **Five interactive variables govern strategic communication.** Every communication effort should be analyzed through five lenses: (1) [[communication-strategy-framework|communicator strategy]], (2) [[audience-analysis|audience strategy]], (3) message strategy, (4) channel choice strategy, and (5) culture strategy. These variables interact and do not follow a lockstep order.
3. **Communication style exists on a spectrum from Tell to Join.** The [[tell-sell-consult-join]] model (adapted from Tannenbaum and Schmidt) maps communicator control against audience involvement. Tell and Sell are speaker-centered; Consult and Join are audience-centered. The right style depends on your objective, not personal preference.
4. **Credibility is the foundation of persuasion.** Five factors affect [[credibility-in-communication|credibility]]: rank, goodwill, expertise, image, and common ground -- paralleling Aristotle's [[ethos]]. Initial credibility (what the audience already believes about you) functions as a "bank account" that can be drawn down or built up. Acquired credibility comes from the quality of the communication itself.
5. **Direct approach should be the default.** State your conclusion first (BLUF -- Bottom Line Up Front) in over 90% of business communications. Reserve the [[direct-vs-indirect-organization|indirect approach]] only for situations with hostile audiences, low credibility, sensitive messages, or cultural norms requiring it.

## Detailed Notes

### I. Communicator Strategy

The first variable focuses on you -- the communicator. Three questions to answer before communicating:

#### 1. What Is Your Objective?

Objectives exist at three levels of specificity:
- **General objective:** Broad overall goal (e.g., "keep management informed")
- **Action objectives:** Specific, measurable, time-bound steps (e.g., "sign 20 new clients each month")
- **Communication objective:** The result of a single communication effort. Always formulate as: "As a result of this communication, my audience will..."

The communication objective is the most precise and actionable. It forces you to identify exactly what you want your audience to do, know, or think after receiving your message.

#### 2. What Communication Style Do You Choose?

The [[tell-sell-consult-join]] continuum (adapted from Tannenbaum and Schmidt):

| Style | Purpose | Communicator Control | Audience Involvement |
|-------|---------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Tell** | Inform, explain | High | Low |
| **Sell** | Persuade, advocate | High | Some needed |
| **Consult** | Confer, give-and-take | Moderate | Moderate |
| **Join** | Collaborate, brainstorm | Low | High |

**When to use tell/sell:** You have sufficient information, don't need input, and want to control message content.

**When to use consult/join:** You lack information, want to understand others' opinions, and want audience buy-in. Sometimes called the "inquiry style."

**Combining styles:** In an ongoing project, you may *join* to brainstorm ideas, *consult* to choose among them, *sell* to get your boss to adopt the choice, and *tell* to write it up as policy.

#### 3. What Is Your Credibility?

Five factors (based on French, Raven, and Kotter):

| Factor | Based On | Stress Initial | Build Acquired |
|--------|----------|----------------|----------------|
| **Rank** | Hierarchical power | Emphasize title/rank | Associate with high-ranking people |
| **Goodwill** | Relationship, track record, trustworthiness | Refer to relationship history | Emphasize audience benefits, "what's in it for them" |
| **Expertise** | Knowledge, competence | Share expert understanding | Cite authoritative sources |
| **Image** | Attractiveness, authenticity, sincerity | Emphasize attractive attributes | Communicate openly, show emotion |
| **Common ground** | Shared values, problems, needs | Establish shared values | Acknowledge similarities, tie message to common ground |

**Initial credibility** = audience's perception before you communicate. Functions as a "bank account" -- high initial credibility allows you to make unpopular decisions, but each draw-down requires replenishment through goodwill and expertise.

**Acquired credibility** = perception built through the communication itself. Even with zero initial credibility, good ideas and persuasive delivery earn credibility.

This maps directly to [[ethos]] in Aristotle's [[three-modes-of-persuasion]]: credibility is the character-based dimension of persuasion, complementing [[logos]] (logical argument) and [[pathos]] (emotional appeal).

### II. Audience Strategy

Audience strategy -- gearing your message toward the audience's needs and interests -- is "perhaps the most important aspect of your communication strategy." Peter Drucker: "Communication takes place in the mind of the listener, not of the speaker."

#### 1. Who Are They?

**Primary audience:** Those who directly receive your message. For unknown audiences, investigate (1) demographics, (2) knowledge and beliefs, (3) preferences (formality, channel). For familiar audiences, also consider their typical behavior, preferred level of detail, and tendency to be challenging or withdrawn.

**Key influencers:** Three types:
- **Decision-makers** -- make the call directly
- **Opinion leaders** -- shape opinion through credibility
- **Gatekeepers** -- can expedite or block your message (e.g., a boss who must approve your slides)

**Secondary audiences:** People who receive your message indirectly -- cc'd colleagues, people who see forwarded emails, audiences who might comment on your blog or website.

#### 2. What Do They Know and Expect?

- Beware the **"problem of knowing too much"** -- you may forget to share necessary background after working months on a project
- **Identify and define jargon** -- your lingo may be invisible to you
- **Deal with mixed backgrounds** -- provide background material for novices, acknowledge experts ("As those of you who have been through this know..."), aim your message toward the key decision-maker
- **Consider format expectations** -- timing, visual aids, formality level

#### 3. What Do They Feel?

- **Emotions:** Consider both their current situation and emotions about your message specifically (pride, anxiety, fear, jealousy)
- **Interest level:** High interest -- get to the point, build logical argument; Low interest -- use consult/join style, keep message short, use persuasion techniques, act quickly on attitude changes (they may not last)
- **Probable bias:** Positive/neutral -- reinforce with benefits; Negative -- (1) convince there's a problem first, (2) start with points of agreement, (3) limit request to smallest scope, (4) respond to anticipated objections yourself
- **Difficulty of desired action:** If hard -- break into smallest possible request, make action as easy as possible (provide questionnaire, checklist)

#### 4. What Will Persuade Them?

Three persuasion levers:

**Audience benefits (WIIFT -- "What's In It For Them"):**
- *Tangible benefits* -- profit, results, cost savings (also symbolic: offices, furnishings)
- *Career/task benefits* -- make their job easier, offer challenge, emphasize advancement
- *Ego benefits* -- enhance self-worth, recognition, achievement
- *Personality benefits* -- thinkers want data, skeptics want credibility proof, emotional people want energy
- *Group benefits* -- group worth, consensus, "everyone else is doing it"
- *Consistency benefits* -- get public/written commitment, then leverage the drive to be consistent

**Credibility-based persuasion techniques:**
- *Shared values/common ground* -- establish early, before the controversial recommendation
- *Goodwill/reciprocity* -- people feel obligated to reciprocate gifts, favors, concessions (connects to [[reciprocation]])
- *Goodwill/liking* -- meet one-to-one, find real similarities, offer genuine praise (connects to [[liking-principle]])
- *Image/emotionality* -- connect emotionally, show your commitment (connects to [[pathos]])
- *Rank and expertise by association* -- cite credible experts, get high-status introductions
- *Rank/punishment* -- threats produce tension, counter-aggression, fear, and dislike; they work only when you're present and may not produce the desired behavior. **Inappropriate for most situations.**

**Message structure persuasion techniques:**
- *Opening and closing* -- always emphasize audience benefits
- *Problem/solution* -- create cognitive imbalance per "balance theory" (connects to [[cognitive-dissonance]])
- *One-sided vs. two-sided* -- two-sided for major/controversial topics, sophisticated audiences, or audiences who will hear opposing arguments
- *Pro/con order* -- pros first if credibility is high; cons first if credibility is low
- *Inoculation* -- present and refute a mild opposing view (people fight harder for objections they raise themselves vs. objections stated by others)
- *Ascending vs. descending order* -- strongest arguments first for interested audiences; strongest last for less-engaged audiences
- *"Ask for less" (foot-in-the-door)* -- start with smallest request to build toward larger one
- *"Ask for more" (door-in-face)* -- start with extreme request, then offer moderate one

### III. Message Strategy

Avoid the "data dump" -- presenting ideas in the order they occurred to you. Instead, stand back and organize strategically.

#### 1. Harness the Power of Beginnings and Endings

The **Audience Memory Curve** (primacy/recency effect): people remember what's first and last, not what's in the middle. Two implications:
- Never bury important conclusions in the middle
- State main conclusion emphatically at beginning *and* end

**Direct approach (BLUF):** State conclusion early. Benefits: (1) improves comprehension and retention, (2) saves audience time, (3) focuses on what they want to know. Appropriate 90%+ of the time in U.S. business culture.

**[[direct-vs-indirect-organization|Indirect approach]]:** Delay main point to the end. Use only when: (1) audience is negatively biased, your credibility is low, and the message is sensitive; or (2) cultural norms demand it.

#### 2. Overcome the Retention Dip in the Middle

- **Chunking** -- break information into no more than 5 major sections, creating "mini-memory curves" (connects to [[chunking]])
- **Repetition** -- repeat main points in preview, visuals, transitions, headings
- **Flagging** -- verbal flags like "if you remember one thing, remember..." or "here's the critical point..."
- **The unexpected** -- dramatically change delivery style, show powerful video clip, tell startling story
- **Visuals** -- vivid images and well-designed graphics reinforce key points

#### 3. Organize Your Message

**For informative messages (tell style):**
- Key points -- limited number in logical sequence
- Key questions -- content organized around questions to be answered
- Steps in a process -- chronological approach
- Alternatives to compare -- outline and compare options

**For persuasive messages (sell style):**
- List of recommendations (direct approach)
- List of benefits (direct approach)
- Problem and possible solutions (indirect approach)

#### 4. Connect Through Stories

Stories make abstract concepts concrete, increase "stickiness" (citing Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*), engage emotions, and build relationships. Business stories are not fabrications but strategic sharing of events that illustrate concepts or lessons.

**Elements of a good story:** (1) Inciting incident, (2) Protagonist, (3) Obstacles and conflict, (4) Something at stake, (5) Point of choice or decision, (6) Result, (7) Concrete, vivid details.

### IV. Channel Choice Strategy

Choose the channel strategically based on: audience/cultural preference, desired interaction level, need for nonverbal cues, timing control, permanence needs, detail level, and risk.

| Type | Highly Interactive | Moderately Interactive | Minimally Interactive |
|------|-------------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| **Written** | Text/instant messages | Emails, blogs, social media | Hard copies, websites |
| **Oral** | Phone/conference calls | -- | Voicemail, podcasts |
| **Blended** | One-on-one, meetings, video calls | Tell/sell presentations, webinars | Recorded webcasts, media sharing |

**Write** when you want: permanence, precision, complex detail, audience time savings, considered feedback, or to reach an undefined audience.

**Present** when you want: nonverbal feedback, group interaction, community building, ensuring everyone hears the same message.

**Speak individually** when you want: immediate feedback, individual relationship building.

### V. Culture Strategy

Culture influences every variable. Think in terms of **cultural norms** (most people in a group behave a certain way most of the time) rather than stereotypes.

Key cultural dimensions affecting communication:
- **Time:** Precise/future-oriented vs. relaxed/past-oriented
- **Fate:** Human control vs. deterministic fate
- **Communication style:** Group-oriented cultures may favor consult/join; individualistic cultures may favor tell/sell; autocratic cultures may favor tell
- **Credibility:** Goodwill vs. expertise emphasis; age/wisdom vs. youth/innovation; social class vs. meritocracy
- **Audience selection:** Different norms around rank, authority, group definition
- **Persuasion:** Different values (material wealth vs. relationships vs. status); Western logic vs. relational persuasion
- **Message structure:** Ritualistic cultures favor indirect; efficient cultures favor direct; authoritarian cultures favor direct downward and indirect upward
- **Channel choice:** Personal trust cultures favor oral; facts/efficiency cultures favor written
- **Nonverbal behavior:** Posture, gestures, eye contact, touching, personal space, dress codes
- **Greetings and hospitality:** Cultural norms around formality and social rituals

## Contradictions or Tensions

- Munter's five credibility factors (rank, goodwill, expertise, image, common ground) map onto but extend Aristotle's [[ethos]]. Aristotle emphasizes *practical wisdom*, *virtue*, and *goodwill* as the three components of ethos. Munter adds *rank* (positional power) and *image* (attractiveness/charisma) as explicitly separate factors, reflecting the realities of organizational hierarchies.
- The "ask for less" (foot-in-door) and "ask for more" (door-in-face) techniques directly connect to [[commitment-and-consistency]] and [[reciprocation]] from Cialdini's framework. Munter presents them without the underlying psychological theory that Cialdini provides.
- Munter's emphasis on audience involvement (consult/join styles) parallels [[autonomy-support]] from self-determination theory -- both argue that giving people voice and choice increases buy-in and engagement. However, Munter frames it instrumentally (as a tactic to achieve your objective) whereas Ryan and Deci frame it as a fundamental human need.
