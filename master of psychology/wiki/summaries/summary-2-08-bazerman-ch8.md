# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.8 — The Escalation of Commitment

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.8, pp.119-131)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Escalation of commitment is the degree to which an individual continues a previously selected course of action beyond what rational analysis would prescribe -- driven by sunk costs, self-justification, and impression management
2. The unilateral escalation paradigm shows that personal responsibility for the initial decision significantly increases subsequent escalation (Staw, 1976)
3. Competitive escalation (the dollar auction) produces systematically irrational outcomes where both parties lose, as the motive shifts from profit-seeking to "winning"
4. Four additive causes drive escalation: perceptual biases (confirmation), judgmental biases (loss framing), impression management (saving face/appearing consistent), and competitive irrationality
5. Organizations can reduce escalation by separating initial decision-makers from subsequent evaluators, rewarding decision quality over outcomes, and rotating responsibilities

## Detailed Notes

### The Core Problem
Three opening scenarios illustrate universal escalation situations:
1. Hiring a manager who underperforms -- should you fire her or invest more?
2. Staying at a disappointing job because of benefits and years invested
3. A startup needing $1M more after you invested $2M -- "good news is we can fix it with more money"

Common element: a prior decision, significant investment, and things not going as planned. The key is distinguishing rational persistence from nonrational escalation.

### Sunk Costs
Accountants and economists agree: sunk costs are historical, irrecoverable, and should not influence future decisions. The reference point should be the current state, evaluating only future costs and benefits. Example: whether to quit a doctoral program -- irrelevant whether it took 6 months or 4 years to reach this point.

Yet typical training in sunk costs "seldom translates to wise solutions to real-world problems" because it lacks description of WHY we intuitively include sunk costs.

### The Unilateral Escalation Paradigm
**Staw (1976) landmark experiment:**
- High-responsibility group: Made an initial R&D allocation to one of two divisions, then learned it failed, then made a second allocation
- Low-responsibility group: Told another officer made the initial decision, then made the second allocation
- **Result:** When the initial investment failed, high-responsibility participants allocated significantly more to the same failing division than low-responsibility participants did
- When the initial decision succeeded, allocation amounts were roughly equal across groups
- **Conclusion:** The mechanism is self-justification. Negative feedback creates dissonance with the initial decision, and escalation resolves it by "proving" the initial choice will eventually succeed.

**Additional findings on escalation:**
- Staw and Ross (1978): Escalation increases when failure can be attributed to external causes (economy, not poor judgment)
- Bazerman, Giuliano, and Appelman (1984): Groups are less likely to escalate than individuals, but groups that DO escalate do so to a greater degree
- Schoorman (1988): Supervisors who participated in a hiring decision positively biased subsequent performance appraisals of that employee
- Staw and Hoang (1995): NBA teams gave more playing time to higher draft picks regardless of actual performance, and higher picks were less likely to be traded

**Friedman (1996):** Mountain climbers' escalation in life-or-death decisions. Jeffrey Z. Rubin, a noted escalation scholar, died in a 1995 climbing accident. His partner had turned back earlier, believing conditions too dangerous.

### Organizational remedies for unilateral escalation:
- Separate initial decision-makers from subsequent evaluators
- Hedge fund portfolio rotation: different traders buy and sell
- Be cognizant of the natural tendency to escalate after negative feedback

### The Competitive Escalation Paradigm
**The $20 bill auction (Shubik, 1971):**
- Auctioneer sells a $20 bill. Highest bidder wins, but the SECOND-highest bidder also pays their bid and gets nothing.
- Bidding starts fast, slows at $12-$16 as most drop out, leaving two trapped bidders
- The $16 bidder must bid $18 or accept a $16 loss. The uncertain gamble of continuing always looks better than the certain loss of quitting.
- This continues past $20. "The decision to bid $21 is very similar to all previous decisions"
- Bidding typically ends $20-$70, has hit $100. Authors have earned over $30,000 running this in 20 years of classes.
- **Key insight:** The problem is entering the auction. Once in, each incremental bid looks rational. The trap must be identified before entry.

**Corporate escalation -- companies A, B, C:**
Company C (worth $1B standalone, $1.2B if acquired) creates escalation between A and B. If A acquires C, B loses $0.5B in competitive position. This triggers bidding spirals where the acquisition price exceeds standalone value.

**Real-world corporate escalation cases:**
- 1995 USAir: American Airlines avoided escalation through Crandall's open letter signaling mutual destruction
- 2004-2006 Guidant acquisition: J&J and Boston Scientific engaged in classic escalation despite mounting evidence of Guidant's product defects (defibrillator recall). Boston Scientific ultimately paid $27B -- more than J&J's original $25.4B pre-scandal bid. Boston Scientific's stock fell from $35.88 to below $17.
- Reverse-bid auctions in procurement produce the same escalation in the opposite direction

### Why Does Escalation Occur? Four Causes

**1. Perceptual Biases:**
- Confirmation bias (Staw, 1980): After committing to a course of action, decision-makers selectively attend to confirming information
- Caldwell and O'Reilly (1982): People who freely choose a course of action selectively filter information to maintain commitment
- **Remedy:** Search for disconfirming information. Use objective outsiders. Imagine future regret before entering (Ku, 2008 -- reduces escalation in subsequent auctions)

**2. Judgmental Biases:**
- Losses from initial investment frame continuation as a choice between a sure loss (quitting) and a gamble (continuing might recover)
- [[loss-aversion]] and risk seeking in losses drive continued investment
- Nick Leeson / Barings Bank (1995): Leeson hid early trading losses, escalated to ever-larger gambles in the loss domain, and ultimately caused the collapse of the 233-year-old bank ($1.4B in losses)
- **Remedy:** A different decision-maker evaluating from a different reference point would be less likely to continue

**3. Impression Management:**
- Admitting failure (firing the employee you hired) is a public acknowledgment of error
- Staw and Ross (1980): Society perceives consistent leaders as better than those who change course
- Kerry vs. Bush 2004: Kerry's "I voted for it before I voted against it" was used as evidence of indecisiveness. Bush's unwavering positions were lauded as strength of character despite unpopularity.
- Ross and Staw (1986): People are more likely to be rewarded for escalating than for changing course
- **Remedy:** Replace systems that reward impression management. Reward decision quality at each stage (Staw & Ross, 1987), not consistency. Evaluate the process, not just the outcome.

**4. Competitive Irrationality:**
- Unique to the competitive paradigm: the desire to "win" replaces the goal of profit
- Ku, Malhotra, & Murnighan (2005): Art auction bidders bid more than they valued the art
- Ku, Galinsky, & Murnighan (2006): Online auctions with many bidders drive prices above cheaper alternatives
- **Remedy:** Consider the other party's perspective. Recognize that opportunities often prove to be traps.

### Integration
Four additive causes work together. Managers should:
- Take an experimental approach to management: implement, but be ready to drop commitment if it fails
- Constantly reassess the rationality of future commitments
- Focus on future costs and benefits, disregarding sunk costs
- Balance caution about escalation with the value of maintaining commitments that preserve future options
