# Never Split the Difference Ch.7: Create the Illusion of Control — Summary

**Authors:** [[chris-voss]], [[tahl-raz]]
**Source:** *[[5-20 - Never Split the Difference - Voss, Raz]]*, Chapter 7
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Calibrated questions are the most powerful tool for creating the illusion of control** — open-ended "How" and "What" questions that remove aggression, acknowledge the other side, and let you introduce ideas without sounding pushy. They are the tool born from the ashes of the Dos Palmas failure.
2. **The secret to gaining the upper hand is giving the other side the illusion of control** — you don't directly persuade; you ride them to your ideas on the back of their energy, like riding a horse in the direction it's going.
3. **Successful negotiation is coaxing, not overcoming; co-opting, not defeating** — it involves getting your counterpart to do the work for you and suggest your solution himself.
4. **"How am I supposed to do that?" is the greatest calibrated question** — it says "No" without saying the word, invites the other side to participate in your dilemma, and triggers forced empathy.
5. **Avoid "Why" questions** — regardless of language, "Why" is accusatory. Only use it when the defensiveness serves you: "Why would you ever change from your existing supplier?"
6. **Self-control and emotional regulation are prerequisites** — without them, even the best techniques fail. Bite your tongue, pause, think. Train your neocortex to override the reptilian brain and the limbic system.

## Detailed Notes

### The Dos Palmas Disaster — Origin Story

The entire calibrated questions framework emerged from the catastrophic Dos Palmas hostage crisis in the Philippines (2001-2002):

- Abu Sayyaf raided a resort, took twenty hostages including three Americans: Martin and Gracia Burnham, and Guillermo Sobero
- The Philippine president declared "all-out war" — maximally nonconstructive
- Philippine military had turf wars and botched multiple raids
- The military "forgot" to include the FBI in phone calls with kidnappers
- Abu Sabaya beheaded Guillermo Sobero on Philippine Independence Day
- After 9/11, the Abu Sayyaf was linked to Al Qaeda and the case became a top priority
- A ransom of $300,000 was arranged but the deal fell apart over $600 — there was no dynamic of trust
- The "rescue" involved Philippine Scout Rangers forming a skirmish line and pouring indiscriminate fire into the area. Martin Burnham was killed by "friendly" fire. Gracia was wounded. The thirteen-month mission was a complete failure.

**Core lesson learned**: Negotiation was a wrestling match where the point was to exhaust the opponent into submission. Everything was a showdown. This had to change.

### The Pittsburgh Epiphany

While processing the Dos Palmas failure, FBI Pittsburgh had a kidnapping case:

- One Pittsburgh drug dealer kidnapped another drug dealer's girlfriend
- The victim drug dealer came to the FBI for help
- FBI hostage negotiators rode with the drug dealer during negotiations
- The drug dealer, untrained, blurted out: "Hey, dog, how do I know she's all right?"
- **The kidnapper went silent for ten seconds** — then voluntarily put the girlfriend on the phone
- This was Voss's "Holy shit!" moment: an open-ended, calibrated question had accomplished what closed-ended proof-of-life questions never could

The drug dealer didn't owe the kidnapper anything. The kidnapper volunteered to put the victim on the phone, thinking it was his own idea. And that is the illusion of control.

### Suspend Unbelief

- **Kevin Dutton** (*Split-Second Persuasion*): "Unbelief" is active resistance to what the other side is saying — that's where negotiation usually starts
- The goal isn't to get others believing what we say — it's to stop them unbelieving. "Once we achieve that, the game's half-won."
- Calibrated questions are one of the most powerful tools for suspending unbelief — by asking for help, you bypass resistance

### The Medical Patient Example

A senior physician faced a patient who ripped out his IV and was leaving because biopsy results were delayed:
- Instead of saying "You can't leave," the doctor asked what felt important about leaving
- When the patient mentioned errands, the doctor connected him with hospital services
- The patient volunteered to stay
- The doctor turned a showdown into problem-solving by giving the patient the illusion of control

### Calibrate Your Questions — [[calibrated-questions]]

**Rules for constructing calibrated questions:**

1. **Avoid verbs** like "can," "is," "are," "do," "does" — these create closed yes/no questions
2. **Start with reporter's words**: "who," "what," "when," "where," "why," "how"
3. **Best to start with "what" and "how"** — nothing else. These two words can calibrate nearly any question
4. **Avoid "why"** — it's always accusatory in any language. Exception: when you want defensiveness to work for you ("Why would you ever change from your long-standing vendor?")
5. **"Who," "when," and "where"** often just extract a fact without tactical advantage

**Key calibrated questions Voss uses in nearly every negotiation:**

- What about this is important to you?
- How can I help to make this better for us?
- How would you like me to proceed?
- What is it that brought us into this situation?
- How can we solve this problem?
- What's the objective? / What are we trying to accomplish here?
- How am I supposed to do that?

**The implicit message** of any well-designed calibrated question: you want what the other guy wants but need his intelligence to overcome the problem. This appeals to aggressive or egotistical counterparts.

### "How Am I Supposed to Do That?" — The Master Question

- Delivered deferentially, it becomes a request for help
- It invites the other side to participate in your dilemma and solve it with a better offer
- It says "No" without saying the word
- **The PR consultant case**: Her big corporate client wasn't paying bills. She summarized the situation and asked "How am I supposed to do that?" — the client apologized, explained internal problems, gave her a new accounting contact, and she was paid within 48 hours.

### How NOT to Get Paid — The Importance of Self-Control

Without self-control and emotional regulation, calibrated questions don't work:

- **Freelance marketing strategist case**: Designed a perfect 8-step script (No-oriented email, That's Right statement, calibrated questions, labeling, more No questions). The script had a 90% success rate. But the client lost her temper when the CEO called, abandoned the script, and the conversation became a showdown.
- **Bite your tongue** — the first and most basic rule of keeping emotional cool
- The Japanese use translators even when they understand the language perfectly — it forces a step back and gives time to frame responses
- **When verbally assaulted, do not counterattack** — disarm with a calibrated question
- **Hostage mentality**: When people feel they are not in control, they adopt fight-or-flight. The reptilian brain or limbic system overwhelms the neocortex, leading to impulsive overreaction.
- **Lower the hostage mentality** in your counterpart by asking a question or offering an apology

### FBI Boss Negotiation Example

Voss negotiated with his FBI boss about attending a Harvard executive program:
- The boss had approved the expenditure but tried to question it the day before departure
- Voss recognized the boss was trying to assert control
- Asked: "When you originally approved this trip, what did you have in mind?"
- The boss relaxed, made a steeple gesture (superiority), and said: "Just brief everyone when you get back"
- The question acknowledged his power and gave him the illusion of control
