# SDT Ch.6: Cognitive Evaluation Theory, Part I — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ryan-deci|Richard M. Ryan & Edward L. Deci]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. **[[cognitive-evaluation-theory]]** (CET) is the first of SDT's six mini-theories, focused exclusively on how social-contextual events affect [[intrinsic-motivation]]
2. Tangible, expected, contingent rewards **undermine** intrinsic motivation by shifting perceived locus of causality (PLOC) from internal to external -- the [[undermining-effect]]
3. Events have a **[[informational-vs-controlling|functional significance]]** -- they can be experienced as controlling (undermining autonomy), informational (supporting competence), or amotivating (signaling incompetence)
4. The Deci et al. (1999) meta-analysis of 128 studies confirmed that tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation while verbal rewards (positive feedback) enhance it
5. Other external events that undermine intrinsic motivation: threats of punishment, evaluations, surveillance, deadlines, imposed goals, and competition
6. Events that enhance intrinsic motivation: choice, optimal challenge, and positive [[competence-feedback|informational feedback]]
7. Both [[autonomy-need]] and [[competence-need]] satisfactions are necessary for intrinsic motivation -- satisfying competence alone without autonomy is insufficient

## Detailed Notes

### CET: Definition and Scope

CET represents both a **social psychology** of intrinsic motivation (how social inputs affect it) and a **personality perspective** (a core aspect of human nature). Its most general form:
- Events that negatively affect autonomy or competence **diminish** intrinsic motivation
- Events that support autonomy and competence **enhance** intrinsic motivation
- **Both** competence and autonomy satisfactions are necessary to sustain intrinsic motivation
- Relatedness also plays a supporting role, especially for activities with a social element

CET was introduced in the 1970s (Deci, 1975) and refined in the early 1980s (Deci & Ryan, 1980a; Ryan, 1982; Ryan, Mims, & Koestner, 1983).

### The Three Formal Propositions of CET (Ch.6)

**CET Proposition I:** External events relevant to the initiation or regulation of behavior will affect intrinsic motivation to the extent that they influence the perceived locus of causality (PLOC). Events promoting an external PLOC (controlling) thwart autonomy and undermine intrinsic motivation. Events promoting an internal PLOC enhance autonomy and intrinsic motivation.

**CET Proposition II:** External events will affect intrinsic motivation to the extent that they influence perceived competence. Events promoting greater perceived competence enhance intrinsic motivation. Events diminishing perceived competence undermine it.

**CET Proposition III:** External events have three aspects, each with a functional significance:
- **Informational aspect** -- conveys information about self-determined competence, facilitates I-PLOC, supports intrinsic motivation
- **Controlling aspect** -- pressures people to think, feel, or behave in particular ways, facilitates E-PLOC, undermines intrinsic motivation
- **Amotivating aspect** -- signifies incompetence or lack of value, undermines both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, promotes [[amotivation]]

The relative salience of these three aspects for the person determines the event's impact on intrinsic motivation.

### The Free-Choice Behavioral Paradigm

Deci developed this paradigm to operationally measure intrinsic motivation:
- Participants work on an interesting task (e.g., Soma puzzles)
- An experimental manipulation occurs (e.g., rewards introduced)
- A **free-choice period** follows where participants are left alone, free to continue the task or do other things, with no external incentive
- Time spent on the target activity during this period = behavioral measure of intrinsic motivation
- Supplemented by self-report measures such as the **Intrinsic Motivation Inventory** (IMI; Ryan et al., 1983)

### Extrinsic Rewards and Intrinsic Motivation: The Early Experiments

#### Deci's Puzzle Studies (1971)
- Participants worked on interesting Soma puzzles; one group received $1 per puzzle solved, the control received no reward
- Rewarded group showed a **decrease** in subsequent intrinsic motivation (free-choice persistence) compared to controls
- This was counterintuitive to the dominant operant psychology view that rewards should increase motivation
- Deci (1971) also replicated with headline writers in a college newspaper -- intrinsic motivation declined when reward contingency was removed

#### Deci (1972a)
- Monetary rewards given for simply showing up (task-noncontingent) did **not** undermine intrinsic motivation
- Critical insight: it's not money per se, but how rewards are administered and experienced that matters

#### Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett (1973) -- The Drawing Study
- Preschool children told they would receive a "good player award" for drawing showed **less** subsequent free-choice drawing than children with no reward
- A second group given unexpected rewards showed **no** undermining effect
- This became the classic demonstration of the [[overjustification-effect]]
- Unexpected rewards do not undermine because they are not experienced as controlling

#### Ross (1975)
- Demonstrated that rewards had to be **salient** to undermine -- marshmallows hidden from view did not undermine; marshmallows in plain sight did

#### Key Conditions for Undermining
For rewards to undermine intrinsic motivation, they must be:
1. **Expected** (introduced before task engagement)
2. **Salient** (made prominent)
3. **Contingent** on doing the task

### Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC)

Drawing from de Charms (1968) and Heider (1958):
- **Internal PLOC (I-PLOC):** Behavior experienced as autonomous -- "I'm doing this because I want to"
- **External PLOC (E-PLOC):** Behavior experienced as controlled -- "I'm doing this because they're paying me"
- Extrinsic rewards shift PLOC from internal to external, changing the experience from "I enjoy this" to "I do this for the reward"
- Houlfort et al. (2002) confirmed that contingent rewards undermine participants' sense of autonomy
- Lepper, Sagotsky, Dafoe, & Greene (1982): When an interesting activity is made contingent upon another, intrinsic motivation for the first activity is undermined

### Early Studies of Positive Feedback

- Deci (1971, 1972b) and Deci, Cascio, & Krusell (1975): Positive feedback enhanced free-choice persistence
- Positive feedback is less tangible and typically not expected, so less likely to prompt an E-PLOC shift
- Positive feedback satisfies the [[competence-need]] while being less likely to thwart [[autonomy-need]]

#### Positive Feedback and Evaluations
- Smith (1975): When positive feedback was delivered in an **evaluative** context (participants told they'd be evaluated), intrinsic motivation decreased even though feedback was positive. The evaluation itself was controlling
- Ryan (1982): Positive feedback delivered in a controlling way ("you did just as you should") undermined intrinsic motivation. Positive feedback alone is insufficient -- autonomy must also be experienced

### Functional Significance: Controlling vs. Informational

Every external event can be experienced as:
- **Controlling** -- experienced as external pressure toward a specific outcome, shifts to E-PLOC
- **Informational** -- affirms autonomy and/or competence, maintains or strengthens I-PLOC
- **Amotivating** -- signals incompetence or futility

The same reward can function differently depending on:
- How it is administered
- What it is contingent upon
- The interpersonal context
- How the person interprets it

### Reward Contingencies: A Taxonomy (Table 6.1)

Ryan et al. (1983) developed the first comprehensive taxonomy of reward types:

| Contingency Type | Definition | Effect on Intrinsic Motivation |
|---|---|---|
| **Task-noncontingent** | Given for being present, no task engagement required | No significant effect |
| **Engagement-contingent** | Given for spending time on the activity | Significant undermining |
| **Completion-contingent** | Given for completing the target activity | Significant undermining (comparable to engagement-contingent) |
| **Task-contingent** | Umbrella term for engagement + completion contingent | Significant undermining |
| **Performance-contingent** | Given for reaching a specific performance standard | Undermining (strongest controlling + some competence info) |
| **Competitively contingent** | Given only to winners of a competition | Very large undermining for losers |

### The Deci et al. (1999) Meta-Analysis

The definitive meta-analysis: 128 laboratory experiments (1971-1996), using a hierarchical approach:

#### Key Findings (Free-Choice Behavioral Measure):

**All rewards combined:** k=101, d=-0.21* -- significant undermining effect

**Verbal rewards (positive feedback):**
- Overall: k=21, d=0.33* -- significant **enhancement**
- College students: k=14, d=0.43* -- strong enhancement
- Children: k=7, d=0.11 -- nonsignificant (children may experience praise as controlling)

**Tangible rewards:**
- Overall: k=92, d=-0.34* -- significant undermining
- Expected tangible rewards: k=92, d=-0.36*
- Unexpected tangible rewards: k=9, d=0.01 (no effect, as CET predicts)

**By contingency type (expected tangible):**
- Task-noncontingent: k=7, d=-0.14 (nonsignificant)
- Engagement-contingent: k=55, d=-0.40*
- Completion-contingent: k=19, d=-0.44*
- Performance-contingent: k=32, d=-0.28*

**Age differences:** Undermining stronger for children than college students, especially for engagement-contingent rewards

**Longevity:** Effect sizes virtually identical for immediate vs. delayed assessments -- undermining is not transitory

#### Previous Meta-Analyses
- Rummel & Feinberg (1988): 45 studies, strong support for undermining
- Wiersma (1992): 16 tangible-reward studies, confirmed undermining
- Tang & Hall (1995): 50 studies, confirmed task-contingent and performance-contingent undermining
- Cameron & Pierce (1994): Anomalous results, but their analyses were "fraught with errors, inappropriate comparisons, and invalid interpretations" (documented in Deci et al., 1999)

### Neuroscience of the Undermining Effect

#### Murayama et al. (2010) -- fMRI Study
- Japanese students worked on a stopwatch task in an fMRI scanner
- Performance-contingent rewards vs. unexpected task-noncontingent rewards
- Rewarded group showed less free-choice activity in both subsequent periods
- Brain results: In Session 1 (rewards active), reward group showed greater bilateral anterior striatum and midbrain activation. In Session 2 (rewards removed), reward group showed **less** reward-network activation -- expected contingent rewards decreased the intrinsic value of task success in the brain
- The corticobasal ganglia valuation system plays a central role in the undermining effect

#### Lee and Reeve (2013) -- fMRI Study
- Participants deciding to act for intrinsic vs. controlled (reward-based) reasons
- Intrinsic reasons: more anterior insular cortex (AIC) activity, correlated with intrinsic satisfactions
- Controlled reasons: more posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) activity, associated with low agency
- Intrinsic and extrinsic motives involve both common and distinct motivational resources

### Other External Events That Undermine Intrinsic Motivation

#### Threats of Punishment
- Deci & Cascio (1972): Avoidance paradigm with noxious buzzer threat -- participants showed less subsequent intrinsic motivation
- Surprisingly few studies, perhaps because the prediction is considered obvious

#### Evaluations
- Harackiewicz, Abrahams, & Wageman (1987); Maehr & Stallings (1972); Ryan (1982); Smith (1975): Evaluations decreased intrinsic motivation **even when the evaluations were positive**
- Grolnick & Ryan (1987): Telling children they would be tested on reading material diminished interest compared to non-evaluated controls
- Kage & Namiki (1990): Evaluative conditions undermined interest in Japanese elementary school children
- The real-world undermining of evaluations is likely **underestimated** by lab studies, because real evaluative structures convey more negative feedback to the majority

#### Surveillance
- Lepper & Greene (1975): Video surveillance undermined children's intrinsic motivation
- Plant & Ryan (1985): Video camera undermined college students' intrinsic motivation
- Ryan et al. (1991): In-person surveillance similarly undermining
- Pittman, Davey, Alafat, Wetherill, & Kramer (1980): Experimenter watching closely undermined intrinsic motivation
- Amabile (1996): Surveillance by evaluators undermined creativity; non-evaluative coactors did not
- The functional significance matters -- surveillance perceived as evaluative is controlling

#### Deadlines and Imposed Goals
- Amabile, DeJong, & Lepper (1976): Deadlines reduced intrinsic motivation on a word game
- Reader & Dollinger (1982): Time constraints decreased intrinsic motivation
- Mossholder (1980): Assigned goals undermined task interest, persistence, and satisfaction
- Deadlines can be informational or controlling; when controlling, they shift PLOC from internal to external

#### Competition: Trying to Win
- Deci, Betley, Kahle, Abrams, & Porac (1981): Participants instructed to compete spent less free-choice time with puzzles than those told to do their best -- even though all participants "won"
- Competition is expected to be negative only when there is pressure to win or a controlling context
- Reeve & Deci (1996): Competition can be informational when it provides optimal challenges without ego-involving pressures

### External Events as Supports for Intrinsic Motivation

#### Choice
- Zuckerman, Porac, Lathin, Smith, & Deci (1978): Participants given choice of puzzles and time allocation showed significantly more intrinsic motivation than yoked no-choice participants
- Patall, Cooper, & Wynn (2010): Students with homework choice showed higher intrinsic motivation, perceived competence, and performance
- Reeve, Nix, & Hamm (2003): Distinction between **option choice** (which activity) and **action choice** (ongoing decisions during activity) -- action choice was more effective for I-PLOC and intrinsic motivation
- Mouratidis, Vansteenkiste, Sideridis, & Lens (2011): Action choice during physical education increased vitality and intrinsic motivation
- Patall, Cooper, & Robinson (2008): Meta-analysis of 41 studies confirmed choice enhances intrinsic motivation
- Murayama et al. (2015): fMRI showed choice improved performance; participants in choice condition showed resilience to negative feedback (no drop in vmPFC activity)
- Meng & Ma (2015): EEG showed choice enhanced stimulus-preceding negativity (SPN) and feedback-related negativity -- intensified intrinsic motivation

#### Perceived Competence: Optimal Challenge and Informational Feedback
- Intrinsic motivation occurs under conditions of **optimal challenge** (Deci, 1975) -- tasks must stretch existing skills
- Easy tasks with high success rates do not provide growth opportunities; overly difficult tasks produce frustration
- Optimal challenge requires at least some degree of perceived autonomy (Ryan, 1982)
- Danner & Lonky (1981): Children spent most free-choice time on tasks one step ahead of ability level; rewarding optimally challenging activities fostered E-PLOC and undermined interest
- Shapira (1976): College students chose challenging puzzles unless extrinsic rewards were offered, then chose easy ones

#### Feedback Effects
- Positive feedback enhances intrinsic motivation through the mediating role of perceived competence (Grouzet, Vallerand, Thill, & Provencher, 2004; Vallerand & Reid, 1984; Vansteenkiste & Deci, 2003)
- Benefits radiate to feelings of vitality and energy (Mouratidis et al., 2008) and enhanced concentration (Grouzet et al., 2004)
- **Task-inherent feedback** (e.g., crossword letters fitting, climbing progress) is naturally informational and sustains intrinsic motivation
- **Other-mediated feedback** can be informational or controlling depending on delivery style
- Ryan (1982): Positive feedback delivered informationally enhanced intrinsic motivation; delivered controllingly, it undermined it -- regardless of whether it was self- or experimenter-administered
- Kast & Connor (1988): Controlling language in feedback undermined intrinsic motivation
- Positive feedback enhances intrinsic motivation **only if** the person experiences an I-PLOC and ownership over the behavior (Fisher, 1978; Ryan et al., 1991)

#### Negative Feedback
- Negative performance feedback decreases intrinsic motivation relative to both positive and no feedback (Deci & Cascio, 1972; Karniol & Ross, 1977; Vallerand & Reid, 1984)
- Can also decrease extrinsic motivation, leaving people with high amotivation (reformulated helplessness model; Abramson, Seligman, & Teasdale, 1978)
- Style of delivery matters: autonomy-supportive constructive feedback can enhance rather than diminish motivation (Carpentier & Mageau, 2013; Mouratidis et al., 2010)

### Further Considerations on Reward Effects

#### Rewarding Outcomes vs. Behaviors
- Rewarding a **behavior** (engagement-contingent) tends to control that specific behavior but undermine intrinsic motivation for it
- Rewarding an **outcome** (e.g., test scores, grades) reinforces any antecedent behavior that produces the outcome -- people take the "shortest path," choosing easy tasks and even cheating (the "Enron effect"; Ryan & Brown, 2005)
- High-stakes testing (HST) leads to school administrators cheating or misreporting outcomes (Amrein & Berliner, 2002; Ryan & Weinstein, 2009)
- Vansteenkiste et al. (2009): Controlled motivation for studying related to more approving attitudes toward cheating

#### Naturally Occurring Rewards
- Natural consequences of volitional activity (finding berries while exploring, a garden's harvest) do not have controlling functional significance
- They are experienced as endogenous outcomes, affirming competence and maintaining I-PLOC
- The distinction: externally administered contingent rewards vs. natural outcomes of self-directed activity

#### Small or "Insufficient" Rewards
- The [[overjustification-effect]] (Lepper et al., 1973) explains undermining through attribution: too many justifications lead to discounting the internal one
- The **insufficient justification effect** (Bem, 1967, 1972; cognitive dissonance, Aronson, 1969) suggests very small rewards may actually **increase** attributed intrinsic interest
- SDT's alternative explanation: Small rewards are less likely to be experienced as controlling and can have informational significance (acknowledging effort)
- Video games effectively use small rewards as informational feedback without undermining autonomy

## Contradictions or Tensions
- **CET vs. Behaviorist position:** Behaviorists (Cameron & Pierce, 1994; Eisenberg & Cameron, 1996; Catania, 2013) claim undermining effect is a myth or methodological artifact. CET counters with meta-analytic evidence from 128+ studies
- **Positive feedback and children:** Meta-analysis showed verbal rewards enhanced intrinsic motivation for college students but **not** for children -- children may experience praise as controlling
- **Connection to [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]]:** Ariely's finding that people work harder for free than for low pay is essentially the CET undermining effect. Titmuss (1971) on blood donation and Frey (1997) on "crowding out" directly parallel CET. CET provides the psychological mechanism (PLOC shift, autonomy thwarting) for what Ariely describes as incompatibility of social and market norms
