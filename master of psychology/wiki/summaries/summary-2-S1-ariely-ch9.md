# Predictably Irrational Ch.9: Keeping Doors Open — Summary

**Author(s):** Dan Ariely
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. People have an irrational compulsion to keep options open, even when doing so costs them dearly
2. The fear of losing an option (a closing door) drives us to waste resources maintaining worthless alternatives
3. Even when participants understood the cost of keeping doors open, and even when doors could be "resurrected," they still chased disappearing options
4. Choosing between two similarly attractive options can paralyze us (Buridan's donkey problem), and we fail to account for the cost of not deciding

## Detailed Notes

### The Xiang Yu Parable
In 210 BC, Chinese commander Xiang Yu burned his own ships and destroyed cooking pots after crossing the Yangtze to fight the Qin dynasty, eliminating any option of retreat. His troops won nine consecutive battles. This story is remarkable because it is antithetical to normal human behavior: we cannot stand closing the doors on our alternatives.

### Real-World Door-Keeping
We keep all our options open at great cost:
- We buy expandable computer systems "just in case"
- We buy insurance policies for every contingency
- We keep children in every possible activity (gymnastics, piano, French, organic gardening, tae kwon do) rather than letting them specialize
- We buy luxury SUVs not because we need them, but "just in case" we need clearance

### The Door Game Experiment (Shin and Ariely)
**Researchers:** Jiwoong Shin (Yale) and Dan Ariely

**Setup:** A computer game with three doors (red, blue, green). Clicking inside a room earned money (1-10 cents per click, varying by room). Participants had 100 total clicks.

**Condition 1 — No disappearing doors:** Albert (participant) sampled each room, found the best-paying one (green), and stayed there for the remainder. Rational behavior.

**Condition 2 — Disappearing doors:** Any door unvisited for 12 clicks would vanish forever. Sam (participant) frantically raced between doors to keep them all open, clicking on shrinking doors to restore them. He earned substantially less money (about 15% less) than participants without disappearing doors.

**Key finding:** In their frenzy to keep doors from shutting, participants ended up making far less money. They could have made more by picking ANY room and staying there.

### Variations That Did Not Help
- **Adding a cost to switch:** Making each door-opening click cost 3 cents (opportunity cost AND financial loss). Result: No difference. Participants still chased closing doors.
- **Telling participants the exact payoff of each room:** Result: Still the same irrational behavior. They still could not stand to see a door close.
- **Giving hundreds of practice trials:** Result: Even with experience, MIT students could not resist chasing disappearing doors.

### The Reincarnation Experiment
A door that disappeared could be brought back with a single click — meaning there was zero real cost to letting it vanish. **Result:** Participants STILL wasted clicks keeping "reincarnating" doors alive. They could not tolerate even the temporary idea of a closing door.

### Erich Fromm's Insight
In *Escape from Freedom* (1941), Fromm argued that in modern democracy people are beset not by a lack of opportunity but by a dizzying abundance of it. We are continually reminded we can do anything and be anything. The problem is living up to this dream — we spread ourselves too thin.

### The Flip Side: Doors That Really Are Closing
We may work more hours at our jobs without realizing the door to our children's childhood is slipping away. One friend told Ariely the best year of his marriage was when he lived in New York and his wife in Boston — the limited weekend time forced them to prioritize enjoying each other rather than doing chores.

### Buridan's Donkey Problem
A hungry donkey between two identical haystacks cannot decide which to choose and starves to death. Real-world parallels:
- **Congress gridlock:** Parties fixate on minor differences in legislation while major issues stagnate
- **Camera shopping:** A friend spent three months choosing between two nearly identical digital cameras — the cost of indecision (missed photos, wasted time) exceeded the camera's price
- **Ariely's own MIT vs. Stanford decision:** He spent weeks agonizing over two similarly attractive options, neglecting his research in the process

**Key insight:** When focusing on similarities and minor differences between two things, people fail to account for *the consequences of not deciding*. When options are similarly attractive, they should flip a coin and move on.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The rational economic model assumes people will cut losses on worthless options. Ariely's experiments show people are deeply irrational about letting any option disappear, even when they know the costs and payoffs.
- Connects to [[loss-aversion]] and [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] — the pain of losing an option (even a bad one) outweighs the rational benefit of focusing resources.
