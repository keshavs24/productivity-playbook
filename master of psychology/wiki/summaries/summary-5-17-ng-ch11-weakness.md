# Negotiation Genius Ch.11 — Negotiating from a Position of Weakness

**Author(s):** [[malhotra|Deepak Malhotra]], [[bazerman|Max Bazerman]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 5
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. Weakness in negotiation results when the other side's [[batna]] is relatively strong and yours is relatively weak — but this does not mean you are powerless.
2. Five strategies allow you to negotiate effectively even when you lack power: don't reveal weakness, leverage their weakness, identify your distinct value proposition, consider relinquishing power when very weak, and strategize across your entire portfolio of negotiations.
3. Three strategies can shift the balance of power itself: build coalitions with other weak parties, leverage the power of your extreme weakness (they may need you to survive), and understand and attack the source of their power.
4. Those who "think weak" inevitably "act weak" — obsessing over weaknesses makes you less likely to set high aspirations or demand reciprocity.

## Detailed Notes

### Part 1: Negotiating Without Power (Five Strategies)

#### Strategy 1: Don't Reveal That You Are Weak
- Roosevelt's campaign manager: extremely weak position (3M brochures already printed with unlicensed photo), but the photographer did not know this. By framing the situation as a publicity *opportunity*, the manager got the photographer to *pay* $250.
- Avoid language that reveals desperation: "time is of the essence" or "we can meet whenever." Instead: "one of our preferences is to move ahead quickly."

#### Strategy 2: Overcome Your Weakness by Leveraging Their Weakness
- Executive wanting to sell 50% stake focused only on his own weak BATNA. Deepak redirected: the buyer also had much to gain (full control, new directions) — the buyer's BATNA was weak too.
- When both BATNAs are weak, the ZOPA is large, and whoever leverages fundamentals better claims more value.

#### Strategy 3: Identify and Leverage Your Distinct Value Proposition (DVP)
- Your DVP distinguishes you from competitors — it need not be a lower price. It is what the customer *actually* values.
- Four tactics: submit multiple proposals, lower bid to pass first round then negotiate on value ("negotiauction" — Subramanian & Zeckhauser), take the purchasing agent out of the game, educate customers between deals.

#### Strategy 4: If Very Weak, Consider Relinquishing What Little Power You Have
- Deepak's college salary negotiation: no leverage, no alternatives. Instead of fighting, he accepted the offer, expressed genuine enthusiasm, then asked the managing partner for *help* with salary.
- Result: 10% raise the next day. When you cannot outmuscle the other side, stop flexing and ask them to help you. Others reciprocate vulnerability more readily than aggression.

#### Strategy 5: Strategize Across Your Entire Negotiation Portfolio
- A firm with 5% margins feared losing deals by holding firm. Max's arithmetic: "Double margins from 5% to 10% and lose even 49% of business — you are still better off in overall profit."
- Thinking across your portfolio changes the definition of "weakness" — being willing to lose some deals can increase value across all deals.

### Part 2: Upsetting the Balance of Power (Three Strategies)

#### Strategy 1: Build Coalitions with Other Weak Parties
- Lee Iacocca ended the Big Three automakers' destructive rebate war by announcing Chrysler would end rebates but match any competitor's. This eliminated the ability to pit automakers against each other.
- Unions, small business cooperatives, the Group of 21 at WTO Cancun — all examples of weak parties banding together to weaken the powerful side's BATNA.

#### Strategy 2: Leverage the Power of Your Extreme Weakness — They May Need You to Survive
- Romania at 1919 Paris Peace Conference: contributed little to the war but doubled its territory. Reason: Romania bordered Russia, and if Romania fell to communism, the Allies would suffer. Argument: "Give us what we ask or we will remain weak and fall."
- Patent litigation: small firm told larger JV partner, "You can push us into bankruptcy, but you would lose a valuable partner."
- Principle: strength is not just "what you can force others to do" but "what value you can create for others."

#### Strategy 3: Understand — and Attack — the Source of Their Power
- Planned Parenthood "Pledge-a-Picket": turned protesters into a fundraising mechanism. Directly attacked the *source* of protesters' power.
- Real estate developer vs. corrupt politician's son: the son's leverage was timing around an IPO. Solution: go public with the information two months early, neutralizing the timing advantage entirely.
- Everyday application: if a salesperson's power comes from customer relationships, build those relationships yourself first.

### The Power of Genius
- Weakness is sometimes unavoidable — but you negotiate most effectively when you leverage fundamentals (systematic preparation) rather than obsessing over weakness.
- As dangerous as ignoring weaknesses is overlooking your strengths or assuming you have none.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- Fisher and Ury's [[getting-to-yes]] treatment of power focuses primarily on developing and strengthening your BATNA. Malhotra and Bazerman go further with strategies for situations where BATNA improvement is not possible.
- The recommendation to "not reveal that you are weak" sits in tension with Fisher and Ury's emphasis on openness. The authors distinguish between *honesty* (never lie about your BATNA) and *strategic communication* (don't volunteer weakening information).

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "The power of negotiation genius may be most valuable to you in precisely those situations where the alternative is to despair — or to rely on gut instinct." — Malhotra & Bazerman, Ch. 11
