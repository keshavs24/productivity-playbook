# Predictably Irrational Ch.3: The Cost of Zero Cost — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ariely|Dan Ariely]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. FREE! is not just another price -- it is an emotional hot button that triggers irrational overreaction (the **[[zero-price-effect]]**)
2. When something is free, we forget the downside and perceive what is offered as immensely more valuable than it is
3. The zero-price effect is linked to **[[loss-aversion]]** -- free items have no visible possibility of loss
4. FREE! can also be used for social good -- making preventive healthcare free could dramatically increase uptake

## Detailed Notes

### The Lindt Truffle vs. Hershey's Kiss Experiment
Ariely, Kristina Shampanier, and Nina Mazar set up a chocolate stand:

**Condition 1:** Lindt truffle at 15 cents, Hershey's Kiss at 1 cent
- 73% chose the truffle, 27% chose the Kiss -- rational cost-benefit analysis

**Condition 2:** Lindt truffle at 14 cents, Hershey's Kiss FREE
- 69% chose the Kiss, 31% chose the truffle -- a massive reversal

Both prices dropped by exactly 1 cent. The relative price difference was unchanged. Standard economics predicts no shift. But the moment the Kiss became FREE, demand exploded.

### Replication and Controls
- Tested at multiple price points (Kiss at 2 cents, 1 cent, 0 cents; truffle at 27, 26, 25 cents). Drops from 2 to 1 cent made no difference. But the drop from 1 cent to FREE was dramatic
- Replicated in MIT cafeteria setting (chocolates added to lunch purchases) -- same overwhelming preference for the FREE option
- Ruled out the "hassle of finding change" explanation by running it in the cafeteria where payment was already happening

### Why FREE Is Special: Loss Aversion
- Most transactions have an upside and a downside. When something is FREE, **we forget the downside**
- FREE gives us an emotional charge: we perceive what is offered as immensely more valuable than it really is
- Humans are intrinsically afraid of loss. With a FREE item, there is **no visible possibility of loss** (it's free). But choosing the non-free item carries risk of a bad decision. So we go for what is free

### The Amazon Free Shipping Story
- Amazon offered free shipping on orders above a certain amount. Sales increased everywhere except **France**
- The French division had charged one franc (~20 cents) for shipping instead of making it free
- When France switched to genuinely FREE shipping, sales matched other countries
- One franc -- practically nothing -- made all the difference. FREE created a stampede; one franc maintained the status quo

### The Halloween Candy Experiment
Ariely tested kids on Halloween with Hershey's Kisses and Snickers bars:
- **Joey's condition (no free option):** Trade 1 Kiss for a small Snickers, or 2 Kisses for a large Snickers. Nearly all kids rationally chose the large Snickers (better value)
- **Zoe's condition (free option):** Get a small Snickers for FREE (no Kisses required), or trade 1 Kiss for a large Snickers. ~70% of kids chose the worse deal (free small Snickers) over the rationally superior trade
- Replicated with MIT students -- same pattern. The gravitational pull of FREE dominates at all ages

### The Gift Certificate Experiment
- Free $10 Amazon gift certificate vs. $20 certificate for $7 (a $13 profit)
- Most people chose the FREE $10 certificate -- forgoing an extra $3 of value
- Demonstrates how FREE blinds us to straightforward value calculations

### FREE and Policy Implications
- FREE can drive social policy: want people to get preventive health screenings? Don't lower the co-pay -- **eliminate it entirely**
- Want people to drive electric cars? Don't just lower registration fees -- make them FREE
- The difference between one cent and zero is not small -- it is the difference between rationality and emotional overreaction

### Appendix: Formal Economic Analysis
Standard cost-benefit analysis predicts the truffle should always dominate the Kiss by the same margin (31 "pleasure units" difference), whether prices are 15/1 or 14/0. The fact that choice patterns flip dramatically when the Kiss becomes free proves that **zero plays a unique role in decisions** beyond standard economic theory.
