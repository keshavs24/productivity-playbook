# Labor Supply of New York City Cab Drivers: One Day at a Time — Summary

**Author(s):** Colin F. Camerer, Linda Babcock, George Loewenstein, and [[thaler|Richard H. Thaler]]
**Type:** Empirical field study
**Published:** *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 112:2, 407-441, 1997. Reprinted as Ch. 20 in *Choices, Values, and Frames* (2000).
**Phase:** 2 — Decision Science
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **NYC cab drivers exhibit negative wage elasticity of labor supply** -- they work fewer hours on high-wage days and more hours on low-wage days, the opposite of what rational income-maximizing theory predicts.
2. **The behavior is explained by daily income targeting combined with loss aversion.** Drivers appear to set a daily income reference point and stop driving once they reach it. On busy days (high effective wage), they reach their target quickly and go home early; on slow days, they persist to try to reach the target.
3. **This is a powerful real-world demonstration of [[prospect-theory]] principles:** [[reference-dependence]] (the daily target as reference point), [[loss-aversion]] (working longer to avoid falling short of the target feels like avoiding a loss), and narrow bracketing (evaluating income day-by-day rather than over longer periods).
4. **The findings challenge the standard neoclassical labor supply model** which predicts positive wage elasticity (work more when wages are high, substitute leisure when wages are low -- intertemporal substitution).
5. **Experience does not eliminate the bias.** The negative elasticity was present across drivers of varying experience, though there was some evidence that more experienced drivers showed less extreme targeting behavior.

## Detailed Notes

### Study Design

The authors analyzed trip sheet data from NYC taxi drivers. Cab drivers are ideal subjects because:
- They can choose how many hours to work each day
- Daily wages vary substantially and unpredictably (due to weather, conventions, subway breakdowns, etc.)
- The effective hourly wage is easily measured from trip sheets

The data came from three sources: TRIP (trip sheets from a fleet of cabs), TLC1 and TLC2 (Taxi and Limousine Commission data). Total sample: over 1,000 driver-days across multiple datasets.

### Key Findings

**Negative wage elasticity:**
The correlation between daily hours worked and average hourly wage was strongly negative across all three datasets. When hourly wages were high (busy days), drivers worked fewer hours. When wages were low, they worked longer.

Elasticity estimates ranged from approximately -0.5 to -1.0, meaning a 10% increase in wages was associated with a 5-10% decrease in hours worked. This is the opposite of the standard prediction.

**Income targeting:**
The distribution of daily income showed a clustering around round numbers, consistent with daily income targeting. Drivers appeared to set targets and stop working upon reaching them.

**Ruling out alternative explanations:**
- **Fatigue:** Cannot explain the pattern -- drivers would be more fatigued on long (slow) days, yet they persist. On short (busy) days they could continue but don't.
- **Liquidity constraints:** Drivers don't need to meet a daily target for cash-flow reasons; most could borrow against future earnings
- **Selection effects:** The pattern held within individual drivers across days, not just between different drivers

### Theoretical Interpretation

The behavior is consistent with a model where:
1. Drivers set a **daily income target** as their [[reference-dependence|reference point]]
2. Income below the target is coded as a **loss** from the reference point
3. Due to [[loss-aversion]], the marginal utility of income is much higher below the target than above it
4. This creates a kink in the labor supply curve at the target income level -- the driver strongly prefers to keep working below the target and has little motivation to continue above it

This is a textbook case of **narrow bracketing** ([[mental-accounting]]): drivers evaluate their labor supply decision one day at a time rather than optimizing income over longer periods (weeks, months). If they bracketed broadly, they would recognize that working more on high-wage days and less on low-wage days maximizes total income for any given total hours worked.

### Implications

**The income-targeting model implies drivers leave money on the table.** A simple reallocation of hours (work more on busy days, less on slow days) would increase total earnings substantially for the same total hours worked. The authors estimate the income loss from targeting at roughly 5-8%.

**For behavioral economics:** This study became one of the most cited demonstrations of prospect theory "in the wild" -- showing that loss aversion and narrow bracketing affect real economic decisions with real financial consequences.

**For organizations:** Workers with flexible hours may behave like cab drivers, setting informal daily or weekly targets that lead to suboptimal labor allocation.

## New Concepts Introduced

- Daily income targeting — Setting a daily income reference point and stopping work upon reaching it
- Narrow bracketing of labor supply — Evaluating work decisions day-by-day rather than over longer horizons
- Negative wage elasticity as a consequence of loss aversion

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **With neoclassical labor supply theory:** Standard theory predicts positive wage elasticity (intertemporal substitution -- work more when wages are high)
- **Subsequent debates:** Farber (2005, 2008) challenged aspects of these findings using different data and methods, arguing the evidence for income targeting is weaker than initially claimed. However, the broad finding of negative or near-zero wage elasticity for cab drivers has been replicated.
- **Complements [[summary-2-07-cvf-ch14-mental-accounting-matters]]:** Thaler previews the cab driver findings in his mental accounting paper as an example of narrow bracketing

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "Drivers who have been working for a few hours but are well short of their target continue driving; drivers who reached their target early tend to quit."

> "A rational driver should drive more on high-wage days and less on low-wage days... The actual pattern is precisely the opposite."

> "The labor supply findings illustrate an important implication of prospect theory: the combination of loss aversion and narrow bracketing leads to behavior that is inconsistent with income maximization."
