# Made to Stick: Introduction (What Sticks?) — Summary

**Author(s):** Chip Heath and Dan Heath
**Type:** Book introduction
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Sticky ideas share common traits.** The Kidney Heist urban legend persists with zero resources — it is understood, remembered, and changes behavior. Meanwhile, well-funded organizational messages routinely fail. The difference is not the inherent interest of the topic but the *design* of the idea. Stickiness (being understood, remembered, and having lasting impact) can be engineered.
2. **Six principles form the SUCCESs framework.** By studying hundreds of naturally sticky ideas — urban legends, proverbs, conspiracy theories, and effective real-world communications — the [[heath-brothers]] identified six recurring traits: [[commanders-intent|Simple]], [[schema-breaking|Unexpected]], [[concreteness-principle|Concrete]], Credible, Emotional, and Stories. The acronym is [[success-framework|SUCCESs]].
3. **The Curse of Knowledge is the villain.** The [[curse-of-knowledge]] is a natural psychological tendency: once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it. This makes it systematically difficult to communicate ideas clearly. The tapper/listener experiment (Elizabeth Newton, Stanford 1990) demonstrated this vividly — tappers predicted listeners would guess songs 50% of the time; the actual success rate was 2.5%.
4. **Creativity starts with templates, not genius.** An Israeli research team found that 89% of award-winning ads could be classified into just six basic templates, while only 2% of unsuccessful ads fit those templates. A group of novices trained on the templates for two hours produced ads rated 50% more creative than those by untrained novices. Creativity in communication is learnable and systematic.
5. **The book is a "nurture" book for ideas.** The nature-vs-nurture debate applies to ideas: are some ideas inherently interesting? The Heaths argue that while some topics have natural appeal (organ thieves vs. nonprofit financial strategy), the *design* of any idea can be improved using the six principles. The CSPI movie-popcorn campaign proved that even mundane topics (saturated fat content) can be made to stick.

## Detailed Notes

### A. The Kidney Heist as Exemplar (pp. 8-10)

- The Kidney Heist is one of the most successful urban legends of the past fifteen years — a man is drugged by an attractive woman in a bar, wakes in an ice-filled bathtub missing a kidney
- It persists because it embodies several SUCCESs traits: it is *unexpected* (the shocking twist), *concrete* (ice-filled bathtub, tube protruding from lower back), and *emotional* (fear, disgust, suspicion)
- Core elements are preserved across hundreds of versions: (1) the drugged drink, (2) the ice-filled bathtub, (3) the kidney-theft punch line
- Contrast with a nonprofit mission statement full of jargon: "Comprehensive community building naturally lends itself to a return-on-investment rationale..." — utterly unmemorable

### B. The Movie Popcorn Story (pp. 11-13)

- Art Silverman at the Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI) needed to communicate that a typical bag of movie popcorn contained 37 grams of saturated fat — nearly twice the USDA's daily recommended limit of 20 grams
- The abstract statistic "37 grams of saturated fat" had zero appeal. A scientific bar graph would have been too rational
- Silverman's solution: "A medium-sized 'butter' popcorn at a typical neighborhood movie theater contains more artery-clogging fat than a bacon-and-eggs breakfast, a Big Mac and fries for lunch, and a steak dinner with all the trimmings — combined!"
- CSPI displayed a full table of greasy food for television cameras — the visual was visceral and concrete
- The story went viral: CBS, NBC, ABC, CNN, front pages of major newspapers. Theater chains abandoned coconut oil. The idea stuck because it was unexpected, concrete, and emotional

### C. On Stickiness (pp. 13-15)

- "Sticky" = an idea that is understood, remembered, and has a lasting impact — it changes the audience's opinions or behavior
- Most daily communication does not require stickiness ("pass the gravy")
- People report needing to make an idea stick between 12 and 52 times per year — for managers (new strategies), teachers (core themes), columnists (policy opinions), religious leaders (spiritual wisdom)
- Conventional communication advice focuses on delivery (eye contact, posture) or structure (tell 'em what you're going to tell 'em) — but these do not address the *design* of the message itself

### D. What Led to Made to Stick (pp. 16-18)

- Dan Heath co-founded Thinkwell (textbook publishing with video); discovered that the best teachers used almost identical instructional methodologies despite having very different personal styles
- Chip Heath (Stanford professor) studied why bad ideas win in the marketplace of ideas — urban legends, conspiracy theories, wartime rumors, proverbs; conducted 40+ experiments with 1,700+ participants
- In 2004, the brothers realized they had been approaching the same problem from different angles: Chip as researcher, Dan as practitioner
- Complement to Malcolm Gladwell's *The Tipping Point* (2000): Gladwell focused on *social epidemics* (getting the right people, the right context); the Heaths focus on the *traits* of the ideas themselves

### E. Who Spoiled Halloween? (pp. 19-20)

- The Halloween candy-tampering scare changed millions of parents' behavior and even prompted legislation in California and New Jersey
- Sociologists Joel Best and Gerald Horiuchi studied every reported Halloween incident since 1958: zero cases of strangers causing life-threatening harm through candy tampering
- The two actual child deaths on Halloween were caused by family members, not strangers
- The story stuck because it was unexpected (danger in a family holiday), concrete (razor blades in apples), and emotional (fear for children) — the same traits as the Kidney Heist

### F. Six Principles of Sticky Ideas (pp. 21-25)

1. **Simplicity** — Find the essential core of the idea. Strip to the most important message. Not "dumbing down" — a one-sentence statement that is both simple *and* profound. "The Golden Rule is the ultimate model of simplicity."
2. **Unexpectedness** — Violate people's expectations to grab attention. Use surprise (an emotion that increases alertness) and then generate *interest* and *curiosity* to sustain attention by systematically "opening gaps" in knowledge. See [[schema-breaking]], [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]].
3. **Concreteness** — Explain ideas in terms of human actions and sensory information. Mission statements and strategies are abstract to the point of meaninglessness. Naturally sticky ideas are full of concrete images (ice-filled bathtubs, razors in apples). "Our brains are wired to remember concrete data." See [[concreteness-principle]], [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]].
4. **Credibility** — Ideas must carry their own credentials. In most situations we lack the authority of a surgeon general. We need ways to help people test our ideas for themselves — Reagan's "Are you better off than you were four years ago?" is a testable credential.
5. **Emotions** — Make people *feel* something. "37 grams" doesn't elicit emotion. People are more likely to give to a single needy individual than to an impoverished region. We feel for people, not abstractions. See [[pathos]].
6. **Stories** — Tell stories to get people to act. Stories function as mental flight simulators — hearing stories prepares people to respond more quickly and effectively. Firefighters swap stories to multiply their experience.

The acronym: Simple Unexpected Concrete Credentialed Emotional Story = **SUCCESs**. See [[success-framework]].

### G. The Curse of Knowledge (pp. 25-27)

- The [[curse-of-knowledge]] is the single biggest obstacle to applying the six principles
- Once you know something, you find it hard to imagine what it was like not to know it — your knowledge has "cursed" you
- **The Tapper Experiment** (Elizabeth Newton, Stanford, 1990): Tappers were assigned to tap the rhythm of well-known songs for listeners. 120 songs were tapped. Listeners guessed 2.5% (3 of 120). But tappers predicted 50% accuracy — they *heard the song in their heads* while tapping and couldn't imagine what it sounded like without the melody
- The tapper/listener dynamic plays out everywhere: CEOs and frontline employees, teachers and students, marketers and customers — when a CEO discusses "unlocking shareholder value," there's a tune playing in her head that employees can't hear
- Only two ways to beat the Curse of Knowledge: (1) don't learn anything, or (2) transform your ideas using the six principles
- **JFK vs. corporate CEO:** Kennedy's "put a man on the moon and return him safely by the end of the decade" hits all six principles. Had he been a CEO, it would have been: "Our mission is to become the international leader in the space industry through maximum team-centered innovation and strategically targeted aerospace initiatives."

### H. Systematic Creativity (pp. 29-32)

- Israeli researchers found that 89% of 200 award-winning ads fit six basic creative templates; only 2% of 200 less successful ads from the same publications fit those templates
- "Highly creative ads are more predictable than uncreative ones" — Tolstoy applied to advertising
- Three groups of novices tested: (1) no training — ads rated "annoying"; (2) free-association brainstorming training — less annoying but not more creative; (3) template training — ads rated 50% more creative and 55% more positive toward the product
- Implication: if you want your ideas to spread, work within the proven templates, not against them. "You want to invent new ideas, not new rules."

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The six principles overlap heavily with Aristotle's [[three-modes-of-persuasion]]: Credibility maps to [[ethos]], Emotional maps to [[pathos]], and Stories + Simple + Concrete together serve [[logos]] (making the argument understandable and compelling). The SUCCESs framework is more granular and practice-oriented but less philosophically grounded.
- The Curse of Knowledge has deep connections to [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — once anchored to our own knowledge state, we insufficiently adjust toward the listener's perspective. See also [[availability]] — our own knowledge is highly available to us, making it hard to appreciate what the audience lacks.

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "If you have to tell someone the same thing ten times, the idea probably wasn't very well designed. No urban legend has to be repeated ten times." — Heath & Heath on why delivery advice falls short
