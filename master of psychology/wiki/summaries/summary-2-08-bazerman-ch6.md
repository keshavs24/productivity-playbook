# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.6 — What's It Worth to You?

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, pp.91-102)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. People evaluate transactions using both acquisition utility (value of the good) and transactional utility (perceived deal quality), making decisions dependent on irrelevant context like the vendor's prestige
2. The endowment effect causes sellers to demand roughly 2x what buyers will pay for identical goods -- ownership itself creates value through loss-aversion framing
3. Mental accounting leads to systematically different treatment of objectively fungible money depending on which "account" it belongs to
4. "Rebate" vs. "bonus" framing of identical payments produces dramatically different spending behavior -- rebate participants spent $2.43 vs. bonus participants' $11.16 in a lab store
5. Preferences reverse between joint and separate evaluation due to differences in attribute evaluability and the want/should distinction

## Detailed Notes

### Transactional Utility (What's It Worth to You?)
Thaler (1985) beach beer scenario: Participants willing to pay significantly more for an identical beer from a "fancy resort hotel" ($2.65 median) than from a "small, rundown grocery store" ($1.50 median). Same product, same consumption context (beach), yet price expectations differ.

Thaler explains this through two components:
- **Acquisition utility:** the value of the commodity itself
- **Transactional utility:** the quality of the deal, evaluated against "what the item should cost"
- Overpaying at a grocery store creates greater negative transactional utility than overpaying at a resort

Problems 9 & 10 (mouse vs. laptop): Identical 20-minute drive to save money. Most people demand a greater absolute discount on the laptop ($2,000) than on the mouse ($50) to make the trip -- but the rational comparison is always "savings vs. value of 20 minutes of time."

### The Endowment Effect
Thaler (1980) coined the term. Once you own something, giving it up is framed as a loss, and [[loss-aversion]] makes that loss loom larger than the equivalent gain from acquiring it.

**Kahneman, Knetsch, & Thaler (1990) mug experiment:** Three groups -- sellers, buyers, choosers.
- Sellers (owned the mug): median asking price = **$7.12**
- Buyers (could buy): median offer = **$2.87**
- Choosers (pick mug or money): $3.12
- Buyers and choosers valued the mug similarly. Ownership alone inflated seller valuations by ~2.5x.
- Ownership-to-valuation ratios of approximately 2:1 are common across endowment experiments.

**Thaler's Michael Jordan ticket problem:** Students willing to pay $330 for a ticket (Problem 11) but demanded $1,920 to sell the same ticket (Problem 12) -- a 5.8:1 ratio.

Implications: Home sellers overprice, eBay items go unsold (fully one-third fail because reserve prices exceed all bids), and companies hold onto underperforming divisions.

### Mental Accounting
Thaler (1999): People maintain separate "mental accounts" for different financial activities (vacation, renovation, monthly budget). They apply strikingly different decision rules to different accounts.

**Swiss trip example:** Thaler found travel expenses less bothersome when they came from the same account as a speaking fee (Swiss trip account) than when costs came from a separate account (New York talk then separate Swiss trip).

**$10 grocery coupon:** Milkman, Beshears, Rogers, and Bazerman (2008): After receiving a "$10 off" certificate, people spent more at the grocery store. Specifically, $2 of the $10 went to increased purchases. Rationally, a $10 windfall should not change spending patterns.

**Wine problem (Problem 13):** Shafir and Thaler (2006): A $20 bottle now worth $75 -- what does it "cost" to drink it? Responses: $0 (30%), $20 (18%), $20 plus interest (7%), $75 (20%), -$55 "profit" (25%). Most economists chose $75 (opportunity cost). Most people treat costs as either expensed away or as the original purchase price.

**IRS problem:** Two $100 losses from separate accounts (federal + state) feel worse than one $200 loss, despite identical total. This follows from diminishing sensitivity: the first dollars of loss hurt most, so two separate $100 losses create more total pain than one $200 loss. Conversely, spreading gains across multiple accounts maximizes perceived joy.

**Practical hedonic rules from mental accounting:**
- Aggregate losses (one big loss < many small ones)
- Segregate gains (many small gains > one big gain)
- Annoying-losses fund: set aside money for parking tickets and petty fines to reduce irritation

### Rebate/Bonus Framing
In September 2001, the U.S. government sent $38 billion in tax payments to citizens. Framed as "rebates."

**Epley, Mak, & Idson (2006) three studies:**
1. **Study 1 (recall):** "Rebate" participants remembered spending 25% and saving 75%. "Bonus" participants remembered spending 87% and saving 13%.
2. **Study 2 (Harvard $50):** "Rebate" participants reported spending $10/saving $40. "Bonus" participants reported spending $22/saving $28 -- bonus participants spent 2x as much.
3. **Study 3 (lab store, $25):** Rebate participants spent $2.43. Bonus participants spent $11.16 -- **more than 4x as much**.

Mechanism: "Bonus" creates the mental image of surplus cash (new money). "Rebate" conveys returning to the appropriate status quo (restoring what was withheld). The U.S. government could have stimulated the economy far more with a "bonus" campaign.

### Joint-versus-Separate Preference Reversals
People place a higher value on Option A when evaluating it alone, but prefer Option B when comparing both. This violates basic logical consistency.

**Hsee (1996) salary packages:** Package A ($27K, $26K, $25K, $24K -- declining) vs. Package B ($23K, $24K, $25K, $26K -- increasing). Separately, B was rated more acceptable (people dislike declining pay). Jointly, A was clearly better ($102K vs. $98K total).

**Hsee (1998) music dictionaries:** Large dictionary (20,000 words, torn cover) vs. Intact dictionary (10,000 words, intact cover). Separately, Intact valued higher ($24 vs. $20). Jointly, Large valued higher ($27 vs. $19). The torn cover was easy to evaluate in isolation; word count was hard to evaluate without comparison.

**Kahneman & Ritov (1994):** Affectively arousing environmental causes (spotted owls, coral reefs) preferred in separate evaluation; practically relevant human causes (skin cancer, farm workers) preferred in joint evaluation.

**Lowenthal (1996) political candidates:** Candidate who'd create 10,000 jobs but evaded taxes vs. candidate with 5,000 jobs and no scandals. Separately, the clean candidate received better approval. Jointly, the jobs candidate won by 2:1.

Two explanations:
1. **Want/should distinction** (Bazerman, Tenbrunsel, & Wade-Benzoni, 1998): Separate evaluation triggers System 1 ("want" self); joint evaluation triggers System 2 ("should" self)
2. **Evaluability hypothesis** (Bazerman et al., 1992; Hsee, 1996): Hard-to-evaluate attributes (word count) have less impact in separate evaluation but become evaluable in joint evaluation. Easy-to-evaluate attributes (torn cover) dominate in separate evaluation.
