# Conflict Resolution: A Cognitive Perspective — Summary

## Key Takeaways

- Three cognitive phenomena — **optimistic overconfidence**, the **certainty effect**, and **[[loss-aversion]]** — create systematic barriers to successful negotiation and conflict resolution
- These biases are departures from rational judgment; if people behaved according to the standard rational model, these barriers would be reduced or eliminated
- **Concession aversion** (a novel concept introduced here) means each side evaluates its own concessions as losses and the opponent's concessions as gains, shrinking the perceived zone of possible agreement
- The **certainty effect** causes negotiators to underweight uncertain benefits of agreement (e.g., future goodwill) relative to certain or contingently certain outcomes, reducing the perceived value of deals
- **Fairness judgments** are reference-dependent and self-serving — disputants often agree on fairness principles but disagree on the reference point, creating pseudo-fairness deadlocks
- Understanding these biases operates on two levels: (1) recognizing the opponent's irrationality, and (2) recognizing one's own susceptibility to the same biases

## Detailed Notes

### I. Optimistic Overconfidence

Kahneman and Tversky identify two interacting phenomena that fuel overconfidence in conflict:

**Overconfidence in judgment.** People assign probabilities that are more extreme than their knowledge warrants. Confidence intervals are systematically too narrow. When given one-sided evidence, subjects are *more* confident in their predictions than those given both sides, despite being less accurate (Brenner, Koehler, and Tversky 1992).

- In conflict, each side knows its own evidence well and the opponent's poorly
- The difficulty of making proper allowance for missing information means each side overestimates its chances of prevailing
- Negotiators overestimated (by >15%) the chance their offer would be chosen in final-offer arbitration (Neale and Bazerman 1983)

**Inside view vs. outside view.** The *inside view* bases forecasts on the specifics of the current case — plans, intentions, extrapolations of progress. The *outside view* draws from base rates of similar past cases. People spontaneously adopt the inside view, producing anchoring on best-case scenarios and systematic neglect of the many ways plans can fail.

- Cooper, Woo, and Dunkelberg (1988): >80% of entrepreneurs rated their success probability at 70%+; 33% said success was certain; actual 5-year survival rate was ~33%
- This is related to the **winner's curse**: those who enter conflicts are self-selected for the most optimistic assessments

**Fallacy of initiative.** Participants in conflict attribute less initiative and imagination to the opponent than to themselves (Tversky and Kahneman 1973), making them underestimate the opponent's ability to respond.

**Adaptive vs. maladaptive optimism.** Overconfidence is not uniformly harmful:
- During *goal-setting and planning*, unrealistic optimism favors excessive risk-taking
- During *execution*, optimism increases effort, persistence, and commitment
- In competitive sports, confidence is an asset because abandoning the contest is not an option
- In situations where *leaving the field is viable*, the line between justified perseverance and irrational [[escalation-of-commitment]] is blurred
- Overconfident optimism and risk aversion can offset each other — people accept risks because they deny them

### II. Certainty and Pseudocertainty

The **[[certainty-effect]]** (Allais paradox): people overweight outcomes that are certain relative to those that are merely probable. The difference between probabilities of 0.99 and 1.00 looms much larger than between 0.10 and 0.11.

**Three types of agreement outcomes:**
1. **Assured/certain outcomes** — exchanges executed immediately; enforceable, unconditional promises
2. **Contingently certain outcomes** — enforceable undertakings conditional on objectively observed events
3. **Uncertain outcomes** — consequences (e.g., goodwill, reduced tension) that are more likely with agreement but not guaranteed

The certainty effect predicts that uncertain outcomes (type 3) will be systematically underweighted relative to certain and contingently certain outcomes. This reduces the *perceived* value of agreements for both parties.

**Contingently certain outcomes in negotiation:**
- Penalty and insurance provisions in contracts are contingently certain — they loom large because they are fully enforceable
- Strategic assets (e.g., the Golan Heights, the Mitla Pass) provide contingently certain benefits in case of war; giving them up yields only an *uncertain* reduction in the probability of war
- This creates a **pseudo-dominance effect**: territorial concession appears dominated by holding on, because the certain benefit of the asset in wartime offsets the uncertain peace dividend

**Pseudocertainty effect.** When an uncertain event is decomposed into sequential stages, people treat reaching an intermediate stage as if it were certain. In negotiations, contingently certain provisions feel disproportionately valuable.

### III. Loss Aversion

**[[loss-aversion]]** refers to the asymmetry in the evaluation of gains and losses: losses generally loom about twice as large as corresponding gains. The value function is steeper in the negative domain.

**Status quo bias in negotiation.** Because the disadvantages of any alternative to the status quo are weighted more heavily than its advantages, there is a systematic bias toward retaining the status quo (Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988). In international conflict, "states defending the status quo should have a big bargaining advantage" (Jervis 1992).

**[[reference-dependence]] and evaluation of differences.** The location of the reference point affects how differences between options are perceived:
- The difference between salary offers of $40,000 and $45,000 is experienced as a difference between two gains (if current income is $35,000) or two losses (if current income is $50,000)
- The psychological difference is greater in the loss domain due to the steeper slope of the value function

**[[endowment-effect]] demonstration.** The mug experiments (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler 1990):
- Sellers (owners) median price: $7.12
- Choosers median equivalent: $3.12
- Buyers median WTP: $2.88
- Loss aversion can be induced instantaneously — it does not require progressive attachment
- Market consequence: far fewer transactions occur than economic theory predicts (~1/4 of expected volume)

#### Concession Aversion

A central contribution of this chapter. **Concession aversion** is the systematically different evaluation of concessions made versus concessions received:
- Own concessions are coded as losses (steep part of value function)
- Opponent's concessions are coded as gains (shallow part of value function)
- Result: each side requires its opponent to concede more than it concedes — e.g., in missile negotiations, each side demands the other eliminate twice as many missiles as it eliminates

**Concession aversion vs. [[reactive-devaluation]]:**
- Reactive devaluation = a change in the evaluation of a proposal *in response to* an opponent's offer
- Concession aversion = the asymmetric valuation of gains and losses *regardless* of who proposes

**Use goods vs. exchange goods.** Loss aversion primarily applies to goods held for use, not goods held for exchange. Three categories of exchange goods (money for spending, goods for sale, bargaining chips) are less affected. Labor negotiations — where items like job security, benefits, and grievance procedures are "use goods" — are the paradigm case of concession aversion.

**Prescriptive implication:** The most effective concessions you can make are those that reduce or eliminate the opponent's losses; the least effective are those that improve an attribute where the opponent is already "in the gains." This aligns with Pruitt's (1983) **cost-cutting strategy**: seek ways to reduce the cost of a concession to the other side rather than offering offsetting gains.

#### Gains, Losses, and Fairness

**Principle of dual entitlement** (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler 1986): the firm is entitled to its reference profit; transactors are entitled to their reference price/wage/rent. In case of conflict between these entitlements, the firm may protect itself from a loss by transferring it to transactors, but may not impose a loss merely to increase its own gain.

- Unfair: raising snow shovel prices after a blizzard; cutting wages because cheaper labor is available
- Fair: raising prices to offset increased costs; reducing wages for replacement hires at market rate
- The previous history of transactions defines an entitlement that does not extend to replacements

**[[self-serving-bias-in-negotiation]]:** The rules of fairness are often ambiguous, and the ambiguity typically involves the selection of the specific reference standard. Disputants may agree on general principles (prices should be fair) but disagree on the reference point for the case at hand. Multiple legitimate reference points enable self-serving interpretations.

**Enhanced loss aversion.** Losses compounded by outrage (perceived rights violations) are much less acceptable than losses caused by misfortune or legitimate actions. Whether a price increase triggers outrage depends on the perceiver's coding: does maintaining the old price represent a *loss* to the supplier (excusable) or merely a *foregone illegitimate gain* (not excusable)?

### Concluding Remarks

The three cognitive phenomena — overconfidence, the certainty effect, and loss aversion — hinder conflict resolution in distinct ways:
- **Overconfidence** makes opponents believe they can prevail, reducing willingness to concede
- The **certainty effect** leads disputants to undervalue uncertain outcomes of agreement (e.g., goodwill)
- **Loss aversion** shrinks the range of acceptable agreements by inflating the perceived cost of one's own concessions

These biases are not readily eliminated by knowledge or warning. Nevertheless, knowing the opponent's biases *and one's own* may help negotiators understand barriers to resolution and develop methods to overcome them.
