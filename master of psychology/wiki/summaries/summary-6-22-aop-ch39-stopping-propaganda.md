# Age of Propaganda Ch.39: What We Can Do to Stop Propaganda in Its Tracks — Summary

**Authors:** Anthony Pratkanis & Elliot Aronson
**Source:** *Age of Propaganda: The Everyday Use and Abuse of Persuasion* (Revised Edition), Chapter 39
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Both defensive and offensive approaches are needed** to counter propaganda. Defensive: learn to detect it, play devil's advocate, debunk appeals. Offensive: prevent propaganda from entering our information environment by changing institutions and practices.
2. **The "gotcha" procedure** (Sagarin et al.) is more effective than mere education about persuasion. Students were first shown they could be fooled by a fake authority ad, then taught about source credibility — the combination of personal vulnerability + knowledge produced lasting resistance. Knowledge alone did not.
3. **Thirteen actionable defenses** are provided, ranging from personal (monitor your emotions, think rationally about proposals, ask "what are the arguments for the other side?") to institutional (support campaign spending reform, promote diverse media ownership, encourage government policies that maintain a large middle class).
4. **Propaganda flourishes in conditions of relative deprivation.** When citizens feel they are getting less than they deserve, they are more susceptible to scapegoating and demagoguery. A stable middle class is one of the strongest institutional defenses against propaganda.

## Detailed Notes

### Defensive Strategies (Personal)

1. **Know the ways of persuasion and recognize your own vulnerability.** Sagarin's research showed that training about source credibility only produced lasting resistance when combined with a "gotcha" — students were first shown they had been fooled by a dishonest ad, weakening their illusion of invulnerability, and then they eagerly applied the lessons.

2. **Monitor your emotions.** When you notice an emotional response to a communication, ask "Why?" Look for manipulative triggers: a false commitment, a "free" gift, a scarce item that induces inferiority, a we-they distinction that elicits the [[granfalloon]], speeches that make you feel fearful or guilty. If emotions are being played, exit the situation and analyze from a distance.

3. **Explore the motivation and credibility of the source.** Ask: "Why is this person telling me this? What does the source have to gain? Does the communicator really have expertise, or is it a manufactured image?"

4. **Think rationally about any proposal.** Ask: "What is the issue? What labels are being used? Are these labels fair? What are the arguments for and against? How cogent are these arguments?"

5. **Attempt to understand the full range of options.** Ask: "Why are these choices being presented in this manner? Are there other options? What would happen if I chose something other than the recommended option?"

6. **"Don't watch your leaders' lips, watch their hands."** (Theodor Plievier, East German writer) Base evaluation on what a person *does*, not what they *say*.

7. **Consider the possibility that any information you receive may be a factoid.** When you hear a rumor, ask: "What is the evidence? Where did you hear it? Would you try to convince an impartial jury?"

8. **If everyone is "doing it" or you hear the same "news" repeatedly, ask "why?"** Use consensus as a cue to question, not to conform (counter to [[social-proof]]).

9. **If the deal looks too good to be true, it probably is.** Seven warning signs: only good for "today"; seller offers "free gifts" for "minimum effort"; a sale item is "suddenly unavailable" but a "better" item is available for more (lowball); seller emphasizes payment size not total price; a "repair person" discovers a dangerous defect; little or no time to read the contract; the seller makes you feel guilty for asking questions.

10. **Always ask: "What are the arguments for the other side?" and "What would it take to prove me wrong?"**

11. **Teach your children about propaganda.** Use [[inoculation-theory]] — watch TV with children and help them develop counterarguments. Ask: "Do you think that toy can really do that? Why do they make it look so good?"

### Offensive Strategies (Institutional)

12. **Regulate and legislate** fair persuasion practices — forbid tactics that exploit children or play on emotions deceptively. Support the FTC and FCC's role as watchdogs.

13. **Support efforts to eliminate misleading labels** and deceptive advertising practices.

14. **Support campaign spending reform.** Why not require federal matching funds to be used for debates, open forums, and detailed position statements rather than thirty-second attack ads?

15. **Avoid dependence on a single source of information.** Media consolidation is the enemy of democratic deliberation. Diverse sources protect against centralized propaganda.

16. **Increase your involvement with important issues.** When personally involved, people process information via the [[elaboration-likelihood-model|central route]] rather than mindlessly accepting propaganda. Create "involvement devices" (like itemized tax receipts showing where each dollar goes) that make abstract issues personally relevant.

17. **Encourage government policies that maintain a large middle class.** History teaches that propaganda flourishes when citizens feel relatively deprived. Before Hitler, Germany's economy was in shambles; in the American South from 1882-1930, cotton prices dropped and lynching rose. A middle class that feels it has a stake in society scrutinizes persuasive communications rather than embracing demagogues.

18. **Promote the institutions of democracy** — decentralized communication, checks and balances, discussion-based agendas, reciprocal influence between leaders and citizens, flexible group boundaries, and protection of minority opinion.
