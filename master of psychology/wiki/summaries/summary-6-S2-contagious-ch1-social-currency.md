# Contagious Ch.1: Social Currency — Summary

**Author:** Jonah Berger
**Source:** *Contagious: Why Things Catch On* (2013), Chapter 1
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **People share things that make them look good.** Word of mouth is a tool for impression management. Just as people use money to buy products, they use [[social-currency]] to achieve desired positive impressions among friends, family, and colleagues.
2. **Three mechanisms mint social currency:** (a) find inner remarkability, (b) leverage game mechanics, and (c) make people feel like insiders.
3. **Monetary incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation** to share. Paying people to refer friends replaces genuine enthusiasm with transactional behavior, often reducing both quality and quantity of word of mouth.
4. **Secrets paradoxically boost sharing.** If something is supposed to be secret, people are *more* likely to talk about it. The allure of insider knowledge makes the sharer feel special.

## Detailed Notes

### Please Don't Tell — Opening Case
Please Don't Tell is a hidden bar in New York City's East Village, accessed through a vintage phone booth inside Crif Dogs, a hot dog restaurant. It has never advertised. Yet since opening in 2007, it has been one of the most sought-after reservations in NYC, with all spots booking within 30 minutes of the 3:00 p.m. reservation line opening. The bar works because it makes people feel like insiders — knowing about it and sharing the secret provides massive social currency.

Jim Meehan, the proprietor, designed the experience with a specific goal: "The most powerful marketing is personal recommendation." The bar leverages scarcity (45 seats, first-come-first-served), exclusivity (hidden entrance), and remarkability (a phone booth that opens to a secret bar).

### Minting Social Currency
People talk about themselves roughly 40% of the time in conversation. Around half of tweets are "me" focused. Harvard neuroscientists Jason Mitchell and Diana Tamir found that **disclosing information about the self activates the same brain reward circuits that respond to food and money.** People were even willing to forgo money to share their opinions — taking a 25% pay cut for the chance to self-disclose.

What we talk about affects how others see us. Choices signal identity: a woman driving a minivan is assumed to be in her thirties/forties with kids who play sports; a man with a Mohawk is assumed to be young, edgy, and into punk rock. Sharing remarkable things makes the sharer seem remarkable.

### Inner Remarkability
Remarkable things are unusual, extraordinary, or worthy of notice. They are *worthy of remark* — noteworthy. Sharing remarkable content provides [[social-currency]] because it makes the sharer seem more interesting.

**Snapple Real Facts:** Marke Rubenstein, Snapple's ad agency VP, decided in 2002 to put trivia facts under Snapple caps. Facts like "A ball of glass will bounce higher than a ball of rubber" and "The average person spends two weeks waiting for traffic lights" are so surprising that people can't resist sharing them. The facts became embedded in popular culture — hundreds of websites chronicle them, comedians reference them.

**Research evidence:** [[berger]] and Raghu Iyengar analyzed word of mouth for 6,500 products and brands. More remarkable products and brands were talked about almost twice as often as less remarkable ones. More interesting or surprising articles are also more likely to make the *New York Times* Most E-Mailed list.

**Remarkability evolves through retelling.** A study at the University of Illinois had students discover cockroaches in their food and then tell the story to others. When telling it to a peer (vs. an experimenter), they exaggerated the details — the cockroaches became larger, more numerous, more disgusting. Stories become more remarkable through social transmission, like a game of telephone, because tellers embellish to make themselves look good.

**Generating remarkability:** One method is breaking patterns people have come to expect. JetBlue broke the low-cost carrier pattern with large comfortable seats, quality snacks, and seatback TVs. Barclay Prime broke the cheesesteak pattern with Kobe beef and champagne. *The Blair Witch Project* broke the horror film pattern with "found footage." Even mundane products can find inner remarkability — Berger made black toilet paper a conversation piece at a party simply because no one had seen it before.

### Leverage Game Mechanics
Game mechanics — rules, feedback loops, levels, and leaderboards — make activities fun and motivate engagement. They work because:
1. **We enjoy achieving things.** Tangible evidence of progress (points, levels, badges) feels good.
2. **Discrete markers motivate.** Buy-ten-get-one-free coffee punch cards increase purchasing frequency as people approach the reward.
3. **Game mechanics motivate on an interpersonal level** by encouraging social comparison. In a classic Harvard study, students preferred earning $50K when others earned $25K over earning $100K when others earned $200K — they chose to be *relatively* better off even at an absolute cost.

**Status is inherently relational.** Apes engage in status displays, dogs vie for pack leadership, and humans are no different. Being the leader requires a pack, and doing better requires others doing worse.

**Airlines and Foursquare:** Frequent flier programs gamified an activity (flying) that always existed but never had status markers. Foursquare's check-in badges and "mayor" status turned visiting locations into a game. Both systems give people visible symbols of achievement that they share, generating word of mouth for the brands along the way.

**Building a good game requires quantifying performance.** Metrics need to be (a) created or recorded, (b) intuitively understandable, and (c) clear about relative standing. British supermarkets use red/yellow/green circles for sugar, fat, and salt — a system anyone can immediately understand. By contrast, credit card tiers (gold, platinum, sapphire, diamond) create confusion about relative standing.

### Make People Feel Like Insiders
**Rue La La:** Ben Fischman transformed SmartBargains.com (a struggling discount site) into Rue La La (a thriving flash-sale site) by making access invitation-only and deals available for only 24 hours. It sold the exact same products but sales were dramatically different. The site hit the ground running because it leveraged scarcity and exclusivity — by 11:03 a.m. everything would be sold out. Members became the best ambassadors because sharing access to Rue La La made *them* look good. In 2009, Fischman sold both sites for $350 million.

**Scarcity and exclusivity** help products catch on by making them seem more desirable. If something is difficult to obtain, people assume it must be worth the effort. If something is unavailable or sold out, people infer that others must like it ([[social-proof]]). Disney uses this with its "Disney Vault" — pulling animated features off the market and reissuing them creates urgency.

**McDonald's McRib:** After initial nationwide launch flopped, McDonald's made the McRib scarce — offering it at certain locations for limited times. The scarcity strategy generated massive excitement: Facebook groups demanded its return, people created a McRib Locator website. All for what is mostly a mix of tripe, heart, and stomach meat.

### A Brief Note on Motivation
**Monetary incentives can backfire.** People are happy to talk about products they like for free. But paying them to refer friends crowds out intrinsic motivation — the quality and quantity of buzz becomes proportional to the payment rather than genuine enthusiasm. [[social-currency]], like social incentives, is more effective in the long term because it harnesses people's desire to look good to others, which they do naturally for free.
