# Cognitive Psychology and Its Implications Ch.9 -- Expertise

**Author(s):** John R. Anderson
**Type:** Book chapter (9th Edition)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Skill acquisition passes through three phases: the **cognitive phase** (declarative encoding), the **associative phase** (error correction, procedural strengthening), and the **autonomous phase** (automatic, rapid execution) (Anderson, 1983; Fitts & Posner, 1967)
2. Performance improves as a **power function of practice** -- the power law of learning -- which means benefits of further practice rapidly diminish but never fully stop; this holds across domains from cigar manufacturing to geometry proofs (Crossman, 1959; Neves & Anderson, 1981)
3. **[[Deliberate-practice]]** -- effortful practice with feedback and monitoring of discrepancies -- is necessary for expertise, but explains only ~30-34% of variation in achievement in chess and music; genetic factors also contribute significantly (Hambrick et al., 2014; Ericsson, Krampe, & Tesch-Romer, 1993)
4. Experts develop **[[chunking]]** -- the ability to perceive and remember larger meaningful patterns. Chess masters recall 20+ pieces from a 5-second exposure (vs. 4-5 for novices) but show no advantage with random positions (Chase & Simon, 1973; de Groot, 1965)
5. Expert problem solving differs qualitatively from novice problem solving: experts **reason forward** from givens to unknowns, classify problems by **deep principles** rather than surface features, and use **[[proceduralization]]** to bypass declarative retrieval (Larkin, 1981; Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser, 1981)
6. **Transfer of skill is narrow** -- expertise in one domain provides little benefit in unrelated domains, consistent with Thorndike's theory of identical elements; transfer occurs only when domains share the same abstract knowledge elements (Singley & Anderson, 1989)

## Detailed Notes

### Skill Acquisition and Brain Activity
As people become proficient at a task, they use less of their brains (Qin et al., 2003). College students learning an artificial algebra system showed dramatically reduced brain activation from Day 1 to Day 5. Regions of activity dropped out or shrank, indicating less metabolic expenditure. No one reaches genius-level performance without at least 10 years of practice (Hayes, 1985). The difference between novices and experts increases with task difficulty.

### Three Phases of Skill Acquisition
1. **Cognitive phase** (Fitts & Posner, 1967): Learners develop a declarative encoding of the skill -- memorizing facts, rules, and procedures. Performance is slow because knowledge is still in declarative form requiring explicit retrieval. Example: memorizing gear locations when learning to drive a manual transmission.
2. **Associative phase:** Errors are detected and eliminated; connections between elements are strengthened. The learner transitions from retrieving declarative rules to directly performing the skill. Declarative and procedural knowledge may coexist (e.g., speaking a foreign language fluently while still knowing grammar rules).
3. **Autonomous phase:** The procedure becomes automatic and rapid, requiring fewer processing resources. Central cognition drops out of task performance, as in driving a car while holding a conversation.

### Power Law of Learning
Performance improves as a power function of practice. On logarithmic coordinates, this appears as a straight line. Examples:
- Cigar making: Time per cigar decreased as a power function of number produced until workers hit machine speed limits after ~2 years (Crossman, 1959)
- Geometry proofs: Time to generate proofs followed a power function of problems practiced (Neves & Anderson, 1981)
- Reading inverted text: From 16 min/page to 1.6 min/page after 200 pages; a year later, participants started at 3 min/page (forgetting some skill) but reached original proficiency after only 50 pages (Kolers, 1979)

Skills generally show very high levels of retention. Coming back to a skill after years of absence often requires just a short warm-up period.

### Brain Correlates of Proceduralization
Brain imaging reveals the transition from cognitive to associative phase. Poldrack and Gabrieli (2001) found increased basal ganglia activity and decreased hippocampal activation as learning progressed -- consistent with the shift from declarative to procedural knowledge. After 5 days of practice on mathematical tasks, the largest activation decrease is in the prefrontal region (declarative retrieval), while motor and ACC (cognitive control) activation remains stable (Anderson, 2007).

### Proceduralization
**[[Proceduralization]]** is the transition from explicit use of declarative knowledge to direct application of procedural knowledge. Anderson (1982) documented a student learning geometry proofs: initially, the student laboriously retrieved and rehearsed postulate definitions from memory (with many long pauses and working-memory failures); by the sixth problem, the student instantly recognized which postulate applied without any verbal rehearsal. Pattern recognition replaces deliberate rule retrieval.

### Tactical Learning
**Tactical learning** is learning specific procedures for solving specific problems. Logan (1988) argued that a general mechanism of skill acquisition is transitioning from computation to memory retrieval. In alpha-arithmetic (e.g., F + 3 = I), after 12 practice sessions, participants solved problems with addend 5 as quickly as addend 2 because they had memorized the answers rather than counting (Logan & Klapp, 1991).

### Strategic Learning
**Strategic learning** is learning how to organize problem solving to capitalize on the general structure of a class of problems. In physics, novices reason backward from unknowns (means-ends analysis) while experts reason forward from givens (Larkin, 1981). In programming, both novices and experts work top-down, but experts develop breadth-first while novices code depth-first (Anderson, Farrell, & Sauers, 1984). The optimal strategy varies by domain -- what matters is that experts adopt whatever strategy works best for their domain.

### Problem Perception
Experts perceive problems differently. Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser (1981) found that physics novices classified problems by surface features ("inclined plane problems," "friction problems") while experts classified by deep principles ("conservation of energy," "work-energy theorem"). In radiology, medical students interpret lung X-ray shadows as tumors (the most common association) while experts see the size and shape indicate a collapsed lobe (Lesgold et al., 1988).

### Pattern Learning and Memory (Chunking)
Expert memory advantage is a central finding in expertise research:
- **De Groot (1965, 1966):** Chess masters recalled 20+ piece positions from 5-second exposures; duffers recalled 4-5. No difference with random positions.
- **Chase and Simon (1973):** Masters recalled more chunks (7.7 vs. 5.3 per board) and larger chunks (3.8 vs. 2.4 pieces per chunk). They estimated masters know ~50,000 chess **[[chunking|chunks]]**.
- **Chase and Ericsson (1982):** Participant SF increased digit span from 7 to 81 by chunking digits into running times (e.g., 3492 -> "3 minutes 49.2 seconds, near world-record mile time") and developing retrieval structures. The skill did NOT transfer to letters.
- The fusiform visual area (normally engaged in face recognition) shows enhanced activation in chess experts viewing board positions (Bilalic, Langner, Ulrich, & Grodd, 2011).

### Long-Term Memory and Expertise
Expert memory advantage extends to long-term memory. Class A chess players showed no recall loss after a 30-second interfering delay, unlike weaker players (Charness, 1976). Expert chess players showed the same poor memory for three-letter trigrams as ordinary participants -- enhanced memory is domain-specific.

### The Role of Deliberate Practice
**[[Deliberate-practice]]** (Ericsson, Krampe, & Tesch-Romer, 1993) is practice where learners are motivated to learn (not just perform), receive feedback, and monitor discrepancies between their performance and correct performance. The best violinists at a Berlin academy had practiced 7,000+ hours; very good ones had practiced 5,000 hours. Ericsson et al. claimed practice accounts for almost all expertise, but Hambrick et al. (2014) showed it explains only 34% of variation in chess ratings and 30% in music performance. Twin studies confirm that both genetic factors and practice contribute (Hambrick & Tucker-Drob, 2015; Ullen, Mosing, & Madison, 2015). Deliberate practice can drive neural growth: violinists show increased cortical development for left-hand finger regions (Elbert et al., 1995); London taxi drivers have more hippocampal gray matter (Maguire et al., 2000).

### Talent versus Deliberate Practice
Gladwell (2008) popularized the "10,000-hour rule" based on Ericsson's research. Dan McLaughlin tested this by attempting to become a professional golfer through 10,000 hours of deliberate practice; after 6,003 hours he reached a handicap of 2.6 (top 6% of golfers) but was sidelined by injury and was still not at professional level (Phillips, 2017). This illustrates both the power and the limits of practice alone.

### Transfer of Skill
Expertise is often remarkably narrow:
- SF's digit span skill did not transfer to letters (Chase & Ericsson, 1982)
- Brazilian street children who correctly solved 98% of real-world math problems solved only 37% of equivalent written problems in a laboratory (Carraher, Carraher, & Schliemann, 1985)
- Chess grandmasters do not appear to be better thinkers outside chess
- Working-memory training (dual n-back) initially appeared to raise general intelligence (Jaeggi et al., 2008), but better-controlled studies failed to replicate (Chooi & Thompson, 2012; Redick et al., 2013)
- Thorndike's **theory of identical elements**: transfer occurs only when activities share common situation-response elements. Overly narrow in original formulation (transfer is based on abstract structure, not surface elements), but the core insight holds -- transfer requires shared knowledge elements (Singley & Anderson, 1989)
- **Negative transfer** is rare in skill acquisition, unlike the interference common in memory for facts (Polson, Muncher, & Kieras, 1987). The [[einstellung-effect]] is one exception -- transferring a solution method to contexts where it is suboptimal.

### Educational Implications
- **Componential analyses** break complex skills into individual elements for instruction, leading to higher achievement (Anderson, 2000)
- **Mastery learning** tracks student performance on each component and ensures all are mastered before advancing; strong evidence supports its effectiveness (Guskey & Gates, 1986; Kulik, Kulik, & Bangert-Downs, 1986)
- **Intelligent tutoring systems** (cognitive tutors) use computer models of student problem solving to select appropriate problems and provide feedback; they are as effective as human tutors and better than classroom instruction (Kulik & Fletcher, 2016; Ma, Adesope, Nesbit, & Liu, 2014; VanLehn, 2011)

## Contradictions or Tensions
- **Practice vs. talent:** Ericsson et al. (1993) claim practice accounts for almost all expertise. Hambrick et al. (2014) show it explains only ~30%. Anderson presents both views without fully resolving -- genetic factors and practice interact.
- **Transfer:** The doctrine of formal discipline (training general faculties) vs. Thorndike's identical elements (narrow transfer). Modern evidence supports a middle ground: transfer is based on shared abstract structure, not surface elements, but remains limited to domains with common knowledge elements.
