# Never Split the Difference Ch.10: Find the Black Swan — Summary

**Authors:** [[chris-voss]], [[tahl-raz]]
**Source:** *[[5-20 - Never Split the Difference - Voss, Raz]]*, Chapter 10
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Black Swans are unknown unknowns** — hidden pieces of information whose unearthing has game-changing effects on a negotiation dynamic. Every side has at least three. Voss named his company "The Black Swan Group" after this concept.
2. **Three types of leverage** — Positive (ability to give what they want), Negative (ability to inflict loss, rooted in [[loss-aversion]]), and Normative (using their own norms and standards against them). Black Swans are leverage multipliers.
3. **Know their "religion"** — understand your counterpart's worldview, their reason for being. By positioning demands within their worldview, you show respect and gain attention. This is deeper than normative leverage — it's a holistic understanding of their life.
4. **The Similarity Principle** — people trust those who are in their in-group. Triggering a sense of shared identity ("we see the world the same way") immediately gains influence.
5. **When someone seems irrational or crazy, they most likely aren't** — search for three explanations: they are ill-informed, they are constrained (by hidden forces), or they have other interests you don't yet see. Your job is to uncover the Black Swan.
6. **Get face time** — Black Swans are incredibly hard to uncover if you're not literally at the table. Pay special attention to unguarded moments at the beginning and end of meetings, and during interruptions.

## Detailed Notes

### William Griffin — The Case That Changed Everything

On June 17, 1981, in Rochester, New York, William Griffin murdered his mother and a handyman, wounded his stepfather, shot a workman and two bystanders, then took nine bank employees hostage at the Security Trust Company.

- FBI agent Clint Van Zandt and Rochester police handled the negotiation
- Griffin set a 3 p.m. deadline to have a shoot-out in the parking lot or start killing hostages
- **The "known known"** at the time: No hostage-taker in US history had ever killed on deadline. Deadlines were to focus the mind; bad guys wanted money, respect, and a helicopter.
- **Black Swan #1**: Midway through, police reported a double homicide blocks away — no one connected it to Griffin
- **Black Swan #2**: Griffin already had no monetary demands — only a rambling diatribe about his life. This was outside all templates.
- **Black Swan #3**: Hidden in Griffin's long note was the line "...after the police take my life..." — Griffin wanted to die and wanted police to do it for him (suicide by cop)
- At exactly 3 p.m., Griffin had a hostage walk to the glass doors and shot her with a shotgun, killing her. Then he pressed himself against a window in view of a sniper, who shot him dead.
- None of these Black Swans were uncovered in time because the FBI tried to fit the situation into known templates.

### Uncovering Unknown Unknowns — [[black-swan-theory-negotiation]]

**Three kinds of information in every negotiation:**

1. **Known knowns** — the counterpart's name, their offer, your experience from past negotiations
2. **Known unknowns** — things you're certain exist but don't know who has them (like poker wild cards)
3. **Unknown unknowns** — things you don't know that you don't know, pieces of information you've never imagined but that would be game-changing if uncovered. These are Black Swans.

**Nassim Nicholas Taleb** popularized the term in *Fooled by Randomness* (2001) and *The Black Swan* (2007). The term dates to the seventeenth century — people assumed all swans were white until the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh saw a black swan in Australia in 1697.

**Voss's hypothesis**: In every negotiation, each side possesses at least three Black Swans — three pieces of information that, were they to be discovered by the other side, would change everything. His experience has proven this to be true.

### The Three Types of Leverage

Black Swans are leverage multipliers. Understanding leverage taxonomy:

#### Positive Leverage
- Your ability to provide or withhold what your counterpart wants
- When they say "I want..." you have positive leverage
- Experienced negotiators delay making offers to avoid giving up positive leverage
- You can use their desire to create competition or bidding wars

#### Negative Leverage
- Your ability to make your counterpart suffer — based on threats
- Gets attention because of [[loss-aversion]]: potential losses loom larger than equivalent gains
- **Use with extreme care** — shoving negative leverage down their throat will be perceived as taking away autonomy; they'll shut down the negotiation irrationally
- **Label your negative leverage** instead of wielding it directly: "It seems like you strongly value the fact that you've always paid on time" is far more effective than a direct threat
- **The paradox of power**: The harder we push, the more likely we are to be met with resistance

#### Normative Leverage
- Using the other party's own norms and standards to advance your position
- If they let slip that they pay a certain multiple of cash flow for businesses, frame your price accordingly
- "No one likes to look like a hypocrite" — show inconsistencies between their beliefs and actions
- Discovering normative Black Swans is as easy as asking what they believe and listening openly

### Know Their Religion

- In March 2003, Dwight Watson, a North Carolina tobacco farmer, drove a tractor to Washington D.C., parked in a pond between the Washington Monument and the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, and threatened to blow it up with "organophosphate" bombs
- Watson told the *Washington Post* God had instructed him to stage the protest
- The capital went into lockdown for 48 hours

**How the Black Swan was found:**
- Watson was a veteran — his rules of military honor provided normative leverage. He told them he could "withdraw with honor" after three days (the rule from his 82nd Airborne days about being trapped behind enemy lines)
- FBI agent Winnie Miller had been listening intently to subtle Christian references Watson kept making
- Miller realized Watson was a devout Christian. She said: "Tell him tomorrow is the Dawn of the Third Day. That's the day Christians believe Jesus left his tomb and ascended to Heaven. If Christ came out on the Dawn of the Third Day, why not Watson?"
- The next conversation mentioned the Dawn of the Third Day. Watson coughed and said "I'll come out."
- He did. No explosives were found.

**Two tips for reading "religion":**
1. **Review everything you hear** — you won't hear it all the first time. Compare notes with team members.
2. **Use backup listeners** whose only job is to listen between the lines.

### The Similarity Principle

- Research confirms: we trust people more when we view them as similar or familiar (in-group bias)
- **Belonging is a primal instinct** — trigger "Oh, we see the world the same way" and you immediately gain influence
- In many cultures, negotiators spend large amounts of time building rapport before discussing offers — the information gleaned is vital for leverage-building

**Ohio CEO case study**: Voss's counterpart kept referencing born-again Christian themes. Voss found the word "stewardship" (administering God's resources with honesty and accountability). "This is really stewardship for you, isn't it?" — "Yes! You're the only one who understands." Deal closed.

### The Power of Hopes and Dreams

- When you know your counterpart's "religion" and unattained goals, employ those aspirations to lead them
- Show passion for their goals and their ability to achieve them
- **Ted Leonsis** (Wizards/Capitals owner) talks about creating immortal moments in sports — who doesn't want to partner with someone making you immortal?

### Religion as a Reason

- **Ellen Langer (1970s Harvard study)**: People responded favorably to requests with a "because" reason — even when the reason was idiotic ("May I cut in line because I have to make copies?" — 90% compliance vs 60% without a reason)
- On more complicated issues, reference reasons from your counterpart's "religion" for greater effectiveness

### It's Not Crazy, It's a Clue

When your counterpart seems irrational, it's usually a crucial fork in the road — push forward into what you can't initially process, or declare them crazy and guarantee failure.

**Three common reasons negotiators mistakenly call counterparts crazy** (from Malhotra & Bazerman, *Negotiation Genius*):

#### Mistake #1: They Are Ill-Informed
- People operating with bad information make bad choices — GIGO (Garbage In, Garbage Out)
- Malhotra's example: An executive owed $130,000 in commissions to an ex-employee, but the accounts were a mess. Clean accounting showed the company owed $25,000. The employee accepted the audit and dropped his lawsuit.
- **Your job**: Discover what they don't know and supply that information

#### Mistake #2: They Are Constrained
- Hidden constraints make the sanest counterpart seem irrational — legal advice, prior promises, avoiding setting a precedent, or simply lacking the power to close
- **Coca-Cola case study**: A contact suddenly stopped responding. The "always works" nonresponder email ("Have you given up on finalizing this deal this year?") didn't work. Investigation revealed the contact's division was in chaos; he had lost all influence. That's why he was avoiding — he had major constraints.

#### Mistake #3: They Have Other Interests
- Griffin didn't want money — he wanted to die. The hidden interest changed everything.
- A client may delay buying to close a calendar year first (increasing their chance for a promotion)
- An employee may quit before bonus season because they learned colleagues make more — [[fairness-as-constraint]] is as much an interest as money

### Techniques for Uncovering Black Swans

1. **Get face time** — some information only surfaces in direct, face-to-face interaction. Email gives too much time to avoid revealing things, and you can't read nonverbal signals (remember [[seven-thirty-eight-fifty-five-rule|7-38-55]])
2. **Observe unguarded moments** — the first few minutes and last few moments of meetings reveal more than anything in between. Pay attention during interruptions when facades crack.
3. **Use labels to trigger correction** — deliberately mislabel the situation to get your counterpart to correct you and reveal information (the Charleston real estate case)

### Charleston Real Estate — Complete Black Swan Case Study

An MBA student interning at a private equity real estate firm investigated a mixed-use property in Charleston, SC:

- Asking price: $4.3 million (deemed ~$450,000 too high)
- Used calibrated "How" and "What" questions to judge the broker's knowledge
- Used labels to extract information: "It seems like this building functions more as a glorified dormitory"
- **The Black Swan question**: Why would someone sell a 100% occupied cash cow next to a growing campus in an affluent city? The student used a mislabel: "If he or she is selling such a cash cow, it seems like the seller must have doubts about future market fundamentals"
- **The Black Swan revealed**: "The seller has some tougher properties in Atlanta and Savannah, so he has to get out of this property to pay back the other mortgages"
- The seller was suffering constraints previously unknown
- Student got the green light for a lowball offer ($3.4M extreme anchor)
- Broker came back at $3.7M, student pushed to $3.55M saying "No" without the word
- Broker: "I am only authorized to go down to $3.6 million"
- **Result**: $3.6M deal — $700,000 below asking, achieved through labels, calibrated questions, and a Black Swan about the seller's constraint

### Overcoming Fear and Learning to Get What You Want Out of Life

The chapter (and book) closes with a call to embrace conflict:
- People fear conflict because they fear the tone will escalate into personal attacks
- In close relationships, people avoid making their interests known to avoid being perceived as greedy
- The adversary is the situation, not the person — your counterpart is actually your partner
- Genuine, honest conflict energizes the problem-solving process collaboratively
- "Pushing hard for what you believe is not selfish. It is not bullying. It is not just helping you."
- With the style of negotiation taught in the book — information-obsessed, empathic search for the best possible deal — you are trying to uncover value, not to strong-arm or humiliate
