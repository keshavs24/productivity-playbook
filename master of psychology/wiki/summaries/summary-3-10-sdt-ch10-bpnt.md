# SDT Ch.10: Basic Psychological Needs Theory — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ryan-deci|Richard M. Ryan & Edward L. Deci]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. **[[basic-psychological-needs-theory]]** (BPNT) is formalized as the fourth of SDT's six mini-theories, formalizing the three [[basic-psychological-needs]] (autonomy, competence, relatedness) as universal psychological nutriments whose satisfaction predicts wellness and whose frustration predicts psychopathology
2. Wellness in SDT is defined as **full functioning** and **thriving** (eudaimonia), not merely hedonic happiness -- it involves vitality, awareness, psychological flexibility, and integration rather than just positive affect
3. **[[need-satisfaction-vs-frustration|Need satisfaction and need frustration]] are not opposite ends of a single continuum** -- need frustration (active thwarting) produces pathology, not just the absence of wellness; need satisfaction produces growth, not just the absence of illness
4. **[[autonomy-support]]** facilitates satisfaction of all three needs, not just autonomy -- it is the critical contextual factor enabling people to seek out and find need satisfactions
5. **Needs operate universally** across cultures, developmental epochs, and individual values -- their effects are functional, not contingent on subjective desire or cultural endorsement
6. **Subjective vitality** -- energy available to the self -- is a key indicator of need satisfaction that goes beyond hedonic measures; it is depleted by controlling regulation and enhanced by autonomous activity
7. **[[mindfulness]]** facilitates autonomy and need satisfaction by enabling open, receptive awareness that supports self-regulation and integrated functioning
8. **Nature exposure** enhances subjective vitality, mediated in part by basic psychological need satisfaction

## Detailed Notes

### On Wellness: Hedonic vs. Eudaimonic Perspectives

SDT distinguishes between **hedonic** well-being (subjective well-being = positive affect + life satisfaction - negative affect; Kahneman et al., 2006; Diener, 2000) and **eudaimonic** well-being (full functioning, thriving, vitality). While both matter, SDT privileges the eudaimonic view:

- **Aristotle** (1869) considered hedonic happiness a "vulgar" ideal. True happiness is found in the expression of human excellence and virtue -- in the *doing well of what is worth doing* (Ryan, Curren, & Deci, 2013)
- **Eudaimonia** captures Aristotle's idea that actualizing human potentials is both pleasant and truly worthy; the word *flourishing* is a common translation (Curren, 2013; Huppert & So, 2013)
- **Fromm** (1976) distinguished between subjectively felt desires (which may be harmful) and objectively valid needs (which promote growth) -- explicitly embracing a eudaimonic rather than hedonic view
- SDT sees happiness as a *symptom* of wellness (Ryan & Huta, 2009), not its definition. A person can be happy but not well (manic episode, drug high, corporate elite inflating ego) or unhappy but well (grieving appropriately, witnessing injustice with empathy)
- **Full functioning** involves vitality, awareness, access to human capacities, true self-regulation, openness, nondefensiveness, spontaneity, and coherence (Perls, Hefferline, & Goodman, 1951, called this *creative adjustment*)
- SDT's focus is on the health of the **self** -- the integrated set of processes, structures, and representations that are the basis of autonomous functioning

### BPNT Proposition Ia: Three Basic Needs Are Essential

> *BPNT Proposition Ia:* There are three basic psychological needs, the satisfaction of which is essential to optimal development, integrity, and well-being. These are the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Failure to satisfy any of these needs will be manifested in diminished growth, integrity, and wellness. In addition, need frustration, typically due to the thwarting of these basic needs, is associated with greater ill-being and more impoverished functioning.

- Psychological need satisfaction is posited as a **necessary condition** for thriving; need frustration is **injurious** to well-being
- Needs are **functional constructs** -- they identify psychological factors upon which full functioning depends
- Two types of research link needs to wellness: **between-person** studies (individual differences in general need satisfaction predict overall well-being) and **within-person** studies (daily/situational fluctuations in need satisfaction predict corresponding fluctuations in wellness)

### Between-Person Studies of Need Satisfaction

- **Cross-cultural research:** Robust relations between basic need satisfaction and wellness outcomes (SWB, lower psychopathology symptoms) identified across diverse cultures (Chen et al., 2015; Church, Katigbak, Locke, et al., 2013; Sheldon, Abad & Omoile, 2009; Sheldon, Elliot, et al., 2004)
- **Work settings:**
  - Ilardi, Leone, Kasser, & Ryan (1993): Shoe factory workers -- competence, relatedness, and autonomy satisfaction predicted both work-related and personal wellness outcomes despite low pay
  - Deci, Ryan, Gagne, Leone, Usunov, & Kornazheva (2001): Bulgarian and U.S. employees -- greater need satisfaction yielded greater engagement and well-being on the job
  - Chen, Van Assche, Vansteenkiste, Soenens, & Beyers (2015): Multicountry research -- need satisfaction promoted well-being even in relatively unsafe situations
  - Baard, Deci, & Ryan (2004): Investment banking firms -- higher autonomy, competence, and relatedness satisfaction in workplace predicted enhanced vitality and lower anxiety/somatization
- **Education:** Jang, Reeve, Ryan, & Kim (2009); Sheldon, Abad, & Omoile (2009) -- positive impact of need satisfaction on wellness in educational settings
- **Sports training:** Hodge, Lonsdale, & Jackson (2009)
- **Key finding:** The more important a life domain is to the individual, the more important need satisfaction within that domain is to overall wellness

### Need Satisfaction vs. Need Frustration: A Critical Distinction

- **Bartholomew, Ntoumanis, Ryan, Bosch, & Thogersen-Ntoumani (2011):** Two cross-sectional studies with athletes using structural latent factor models showed that need satisfaction was predicted by perceived autonomy support, while need frustration was predicted by controlling coach behaviors. Need satisfaction predicted positive outcomes (vitality, positive affect); need thwarting more consistently predicted maladaptive outcomes (disordered eating, burnout, depression, negative affect, physical symptoms). Athletes' pre-training need frustrations predicted elevated secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA), a biomarker of stress.
- **Cordeiro, Paixao, Lens, Lacante, & Sheldon (2016):** Portuguese high school students -- need satisfaction contributed to vitality and life satisfaction; need frustration predicted symptoms of anxiety, depression, and somatization
- This dual-process model ([[need-satisfaction-vs-frustration]]) shows that satisfaction and frustration have **partially independent effects** on qualitatively different outcomes

### BPNT Proposition Ib: Within-Person Variation

> *BPNT Proposition Ib:* Psychological need satisfactions and frustrations vary within persons over time, contexts, and social interactions. Any factor or event that produces variations in need satisfaction or need frustration will also produce variations in wellness, and this principle extends from highly aggregated levels of analysis down to moment-to-moment or situation-to-situation variations in functioning.

- **Sheldon, Ryan, & Reis (1996):** Daily diary study with university students -- daily fluctuations in autonomy and competence satisfaction predicted within-person fluctuations in daily well-being (positive affect, vitality) beyond between-person trait effects
- **Reis, Sheldon, Gable, Roscoe, & Ryan (2000):** All three needs examined -- daily satisfaction of each predicted unique variance in daily well-being at both between-person and within-person levels
- **Ryan, Bernstein, & Brown (2010):** Extended to adult workers, sampled three times per day. Weekends and nonworking times were associated with enhanced well-being, mediated by greater autonomy and relatedness satisfaction. Weekend "work effect" and "weekend effect" documented.
- **La Guardia, Ryan, Couchman, & Deci (2000):** Within-person variations in need satisfaction across different relationships (mother, father, partner, friends) predicted relationship-specific outcomes
- **Milyavskaya, Philippe, & Koestner (2013):** Both bottom-up and top-down effects between situational, domain-level, and general need satisfaction
- **Philippe, Koestner, Beaulieu-Pelletier, Lecours, & Lekes (2012):** Four studies showed that episodic memories characterized by need satisfaction/frustration predicted wellness, and priming such memories could affect present well-being

### Autonomy Support and Need Satisfaction

> *BPNT Proposition II:* Satisfaction of each of the three psychological needs is facilitated by autonomy support, whereas controlling contexts and events can disrupt not only autonomy satisfactions, but relatedness and competence need fulfillments as well.

- Autonomy support facilitates satisfaction of **all three** needs, not just autonomy. It plays a critical role in allowing individuals to gravitate toward, make relevant choices in relation to, and employ optimizing strategies for satisfying each basic need.
- **Gagne (2003):** Volunteer workers at an animal shelter -- autonomy support predicted stronger engagement and lower turnover, mediated by basic psychological need satisfaction
- **Sheldon & Krieger (2007):** 3-year study at two law schools -- students at both schools decreased in need satisfaction and well-being, but those perceiving greater autonomy support from faculty showed less serious declines, which in turn predicted better grades and bar exam results. Institution-level analyses showed one school was perceived as significantly more controlling.
- **V. Kasser & Ryan (1999):** Elderly care facility residents -- autonomy and relational support from friends, relatives, and nursing home staff predicted vitality and perceived health in daily lives

### Needs and Values: Not Always Congruent

> *BPNT Proposition III:* Because basic psychological need satisfactions are functional requirements for full functioning and wellness, the effects of satisfaction versus frustration of these needs will be evidenced regardless of whether or not people explicitly desire or value the needs, and regardless of their sociocultural context.

- This distinguishes BPNT from **expectancy-value theories** (Bandura, 1996; Vroom, 1964). Some valued outcomes are consistent with need satisfaction and some are not; only those consistent with need satisfaction yield functional outcomes of vitality and wellness.
- **Chen et al. (2015):** Examined need satisfaction and frustration across Belgium, China, United States, and Peru. Across all four cultures, satisfaction of each need predicted unique variance in well-being outcomes, and each predicted unique variance in ill-being outcomes. Magnitudes were fairly equivalent across cultures. Neither self-reported need strengths nor desires for need satisfaction moderated the effects.

### Need Satisfactions: Typically Interrelated and Often Balanced

> *BPNT Proposition IV:* Basic need satisfactions of autonomy, competence, and relatedness will tend to positively relate to one another, especially at an aggregated level of analysis, across domains, situations, or time.

- Some cultural relativists (Markus, Kitayama, & Heiman, 1996; Iyengar & DeVoe, 2003) and early feminist perspectives (Gilligan, 1982; Jordan, 1991) have claimed autonomy and relatedness are in opposition. SDT research clearly distinguishes independence/separateness from autonomy and shows positive correlations.
- Factor analysis of basic needs scales often identifies a higher-order total need satisfaction factor (Chen et al., 2015)
- **Sheldon & Niemiec (2006):** Four studies -- **balance** in satisfaction of the three needs is also important. Individuals with more balanced need satisfaction reported higher well-being than those with the same summary score but greater variability. This was independent of neuroticism and held across concurrent, prospective, within-person diary, and observer-report methods. Controlled for curvilinear effects.
- **Milyavskaya et al. (2009):** Balance across life domains also matters. Adolescents from four countries -- imbalanced need satisfaction across important life domains had additional negative effects beyond cumulative satisfaction levels.
- The **three-legged stool metaphor:** Pull out any one support and the stool falls. Autonomy often plays a particular facilitative role -- it allows persons to pursue what they deem most valuable.

### Are There Other Basic Psychological Needs?

SDT applies six stringent criteria for a candidate need:

1. **Functional specificity:** Must specify content -- particular experiences and behaviors that will lead to well-being (e.g., autonomy makes clear that people need to endorse their actions; self-actualization is too open/vague)
2. **Explanatory necessity:** Must be essential to explain empirical phenomena, with clear empirically supported costs and benefits
3. **Additional variance:** Any new need must serve as a significant additional mediator of relations, beyond existing three
4. **Growth need, not deficit need:** Must facilitate healthy development on an ongoing basis (not just operate when organism is threatened). Security, for instance, becomes salient primarily under threat.
5. **Logically appropriate variable type:** Must be something that, when satisfied, leads to positive outcomes and when thwarted leads to negative outcomes. Vitality is an outcome, not a need.
6. **Universality:** Must operate for all people at all ages in all cultures, with a reasonable evolutionary basis

**Candidate needs rejected:**
- **Meaning:** SDT views meaning as an *outcome* of basic need satisfaction, not a basic need itself. Weinstein, Ryan, & Deci (2012) argued need satisfaction links to meaning; intrinsic aspirations relate to *having* meaning in life, while extrinsic aspirations relate only to *wanting* and *searching for* meaning (Deci, Weinstein, & Ryan, 2006). Meaning lacks content specificity.
- **Self-esteem:** SDT views true self-esteem as an *outcome* of need satisfaction (Deci & Ryan, 1995; Moller, Friedman, & Deci, 2006; Ryan & Deci, 2004a, 2004b). *Contingent* self-esteem is a result of conditional regard (Roth, Assor, Niemiec, Ryan, & Deci, 2009) and indicates introjection, not health. The healthier one is, the less self-esteem is salient.
- **Security/safety:** SDT views these as **deficit needs** that become salient under threat or thwarting of growth needs. Satisfying deficit needs staves off ill-being but does not promote flourishing (Carroll, Arkin, Seidel & Morris, 2009; Rasskazova et al., 2016; Welzel, 2013).

> *BPNT Proposition V:* Deficit needs (such as needs for security and self-esteem) become salient under circumstances of threat, distress, or thwarting of growth needs such as autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Satisfaction of deficit needs can stave off aspects of ill-being but do not typically contribute to enhanced wellness or flourishing.

### The Universality of the Basic Psychological Needs

- Because needs are defined as inner human conditions necessary for optimal development, they apply to **all individuals**
- Even individuals who deny needing autonomy will suffer ill effects when it is thwarted
- SDT researchers have conducted numerous cross-cultural studies (see extensively in Chapter 22)

### Vitality, Basic Needs, and Well-Being

- **Subjective vitality** = energy that is available to the self, energy that can be used in volitional activity (Ryan & Frederick, 1997)
- Vitality is distinct from arousal, hedonic pleasure, or mere physical energy. It was specifically related to basic need satisfactions and *negatively* related to investment in extrinsic goals (Kasser & Ryan, 1996)
- Vitality is depleted by controlling regulation and self-control but enhanced by autonomous activity and need satisfaction

> *BPNT Proposition VI:* Subjective vitality is based on more than physical nutrients; it also reflects satisfaction versus thwarting of basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Therefore, both externally controlling and self-controlling states are expected to deplete vitality, whereas basic psychological needs satisfactions are expected to enhance it.

### SDT's Vitality Model vs. Ego Depletion (Strength Model of Self-Control)

- Baumeister et al. (1998) proposed the **ego-depletion model** (strength model): self-regulation is a limited resource depleted by use, like a muscle
- SDT differentiates self-control (controlled regulation, introjection) from true self-regulation (autonomous regulation). Self-control depletes vitality; autonomous self-regulation may maintain or enhance it.
- **Moller, Deci, & Ryan (2006):** Three conditions -- "true choice" (genuinely autonomous), "compelled choice" (told they had choice but pressured to specific option, replicating Baumeister et al., 1998), and "no choice." Compelled-choice participants showed ego depletion. True-choice participants did not -- they were significantly more energized than compelled-choice participants.
- **Muraven, Gagne, & Rosman (2008):** Autonomy-supportive experimenter context reduced depletion on subsequent self-control tasks; mediated by subjective vitality
- **Muraven (2008):** Autonomous reasons for exerting self-control predicted less depletion
- **Kazen et al. (2015):** Applied both SDT and personality systems interaction (PSI) theory. In external control conditions, participants showed the classic depletion effect. In autonomy-supportive conditions, participants showed a *rise* in blood glucose and better performance.

### Enhancing Vitality and Energy through Need-Satisfying Activity

- SDT view of energy goes beyond limited-resource models: energy derives not just from physical need supports but also from psychological need supports
- **Vlachopoulos & Karavani (2009):** Greek exercise participants -- autonomy support predicted vitality, partially mediated by psychological need satisfaction, especially competence satisfactions
- **Weinstein & Ryan (2010):** Helping others for autonomous rather than controlled reasons enhanced vitality, mediated by all three basic needs including relatedness
- **Deci et al. (2006):** Giving autonomy support to best friends enhanced vitality for the giver as well as the receiver
- **Ryan, Bernstein, & Brown (2010):** Daily vitality among workers was higher when psychological needs were satisfied; especially high on weekends, mediated by relatedness and autonomy enhancements

### Nature and Vitality

- Human nature evolved within the world of living nature. Nature exposure has robust effects on vitality.
- **Ryan, Weinstein, Bernstein, Brown, Mistretta, & Gagne (2010):** Five studies (survey, experimental, diary) all showed being outdoors or around natural elements was associated with greater subjective vitality, beyond effects of physical activity and social interaction. Mediated by the presence of natural elements.
- **Shalev (2016):** Two studies -- landscapes with water and living plants were more vitalizing than arid landscapes. Arid landscapes were devitalizing and associated with stress.
- **Smolders, de Kort, & van den Berg (2013):** Being in daylight significantly predicted vitality on an hour-by-hour basis, independent of sleep patterns and mood
- **Weinstein, Przybylski, & Ryan (2009):** Four studies -- nature exposure increased valuing of intrinsic goals and decreased valuing of extrinsic goals. More natural settings promoted prosocial behavior (sharing money). Mediated by feelings of autonomy and relatedness to nature.
- **Weinstein, Balmford, DeHaan, Gladwell, Bradbury, & Amano (2015):** Perceived quality of access to nature predicted community cohesion, which predicted lower crime and greater well-being

> *BPNT Proposition VII:* Other factors aside, meaningful exposure to living nature has a positive effect on subjective vitality relative to exposure to non-natural, built environments without living elements, and this relation is mediated in part by basic psychological needs.

### Awareness as a Foundation of Autonomous Motivation

- **Awareness** is a foundational element in SDT for proactively engaging with one's inner and outer worlds
- Receptive awareness facilitates insight, self-regulation, congruence with one's needs, feelings, interests, and values (Deci, Ryan, Schultz, & Niemiec, 2015; Ryan, Huta, & Deci, 2008)

#### Mindfulness

- **Brown & Ryan (2003)** described mindfulness as an open receptive awareness of what is happening internally and externally in the present moment -- a quality of consciousness rooted in Buddhist tradition
- SDT defines mindfulness simply as receptive attention and awareness, distinct from Langer's (1989) active cognitive operations, specific philosophies, or character traits like compassion

> *BPNT Proposition VIII:* Mindfulness, defined as the open and receptive awareness of what is occurring both within people and within their context, facilitates greater autonomy and more integrated self-regulation, as well as greater basic psychological need satisfaction, which contributes to greater well-being.

#### Self-Regulation, Autonomy, and Mindfulness
- **Brown & Ryan (2003):** Developed and validated the **Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS)**. Experience-sampling study: higher dispositional and state mindfulness predicted more autonomous activity in daily life and greater need satisfaction (relatedness, competence, and autonomy)
- **Brown & Ryan (2003):** Higher trait mindfulness associated with greater congruence between implicit and explicit emotional states (Implicit Association Test)
- SDT differentiates *automatized* behaviors (volitional, well-integrated, can be done without consciousness) from *automatic* behaviors (controlled by forces outside awareness). Mindfulness protects against the latter.
- **Levesque & Brown (2007):** Mindfulness moderated the relation between implicit regulation and daily behavior -- low-mindfulness individuals were more controlled by nonconscious processes

#### Mindfulness and Terror Management
- **Niemiec, Brown, et al. (2010):** Seven experiments -- high trait mindfulness mitigated defensive responses to mortality salience (MS). High-mindfulness individuals showed less immediate suppression of death thoughts, more full processing, and less accessibility after delay. Most importantly, they did not show the classic TMT defensive responses -- no prejudicial behavior toward outgroup members.

#### Mindfulness, Coping, and Stress
- **Weinstein, Brown, & Ryan (2009):** Four studies -- high mindfulness individuals were less reactive to stress challenges, showed more positive coping, with effects mediated by more adaptive stress feelings and responses
- **Schultz, Ryan, Niemiec, Legate, & Williams (2015):** Mindfulness and autonomy support were directly related to employee wellness; mindfulness buffered effects of controlling management on need frustration

### Well-Being: Living an Authentic, Mindful Life

The chapter concludes by connecting BPNT and [[goal-contents-theory|GCT]]: when people function eudaimonically -- autonomously pursuing and attaining intrinsic aspirations -- this way of living tends to make them happy, even though happiness is not the direct aim (Ryan, Curren, & Deci, 2013). Cultivating mindfulness and interested awareness can help individuals make more informed and integrated choices, contributing to flourishing.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- **SDT vs. ego-depletion model:** SDT challenges the Baumeister et al. (1998) strength model by showing that self-regulation is not uniformly depleting -- only controlled regulation depletes, while autonomous regulation can maintain or enhance energy. The "high-choice" condition in Baumeister's original experiment was actually a compelled-choice manipulation (Moller et al., 2006).
- **SDT vs. expectancy-value theories:** BPNT argues that obtaining valued outcomes does not automatically produce wellness. Only outcomes consistent with basic need satisfaction yield vitality and well-being, regardless of whether the individual values or desires them.
- **SDT vs. cultural relativism:** BPNT insists on universality of needs across cultures, in tension with Markus, Kitayama, & Heiman (1996) who argue autonomy is a Western value. SDT responds that autonomy is not independence; collectivists can be highly autonomous.
