# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.3 -- Common Biases

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Edition)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Twelve specific biases emanate from three general heuristics: availability (2 biases), representativeness (5 biases), and confirmation (5 biases)
2. The availability heuristic produces biases of ease of recall (vividness/recency) and retrievability (memory structure); WHO data shows people overestimate vivid causes of death (war, starvation) and underestimate common ones (respiratory infections, lung disease)
3. The representativeness heuristic produces insensitivity to base rates, insensitivity to sample size, misconceptions of chance, regression to the mean, and the conjunction fallacy
4. The confirmation heuristic produces the confirmation trap (Wason, 1960), anchoring, conjunctive/disjunctive events bias, hindsight bias (Fischhoff, 1975), and the curse of knowledge
5. The chapter uses interactive quiz problems (Table 3.1) to demonstrate each bias experientially before explaining it

## Detailed Notes

### Biases from the Availability Heuristic

**Bias 1 -- Ease of Recall (Vividness/Recency):** People judge events that are more easily recalled as more numerous. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) showed famous names on a list cause people to overestimate that gender's proportion. WHO data reveals causes of death are ranked in reverse order of their actual frequency -- respiratory infections kill far more than war or starvation, but vivid deaths get more press coverage. Managers give more weight to employee performance immediately prior to evaluations than to the previous nine months. Schwarz et al. (1991) showed that people asked to list 12 examples of their assertiveness rated themselves as less assertive than those asked for 6 examples -- because the difficulty of generating 12 was interpreted as evidence of low assertiveness. Risen and Gilovich (2008) found that "tempting fate" actions (not doing assigned reading) make negative outcomes seem more available/likely.

**Bias 2 -- Retrievability:** Memory structures bias frequency estimates. People estimate that more words begin with "a" than have "a" as the third letter because first-letter retrieval is easier -- in fact, "a" as third letter is more common (>9% vs ~6%). Tversky and Kahneman (1983) showed seven-letter words with "n" in sixth position estimated as less common than those ending in "ing" -- impossible since the latter is a subset of the former. Managers relying on social networks to find employees end up with candidates similar to themselves (Petersen, Saporta, & Seidel, 2000), unintentionally reducing diversity (Pager & Shepherd, 2008).

### Biases from the Representativeness Heuristic

**Bias 3 -- Insensitivity to Base Rates:** Lisa's Down syndrome test problem: a positive test with 86% accuracy and 5% false positive rate, given a 1/1000 base rate, yields only a 1.7% chance of actually having Down syndrome -- but most people estimate much higher. Kahneman and Tversky (1972) engineer/lawyer study: participants' probability estimates were impervious to base-rate differences (70/30 vs. 30/70 split) when personal descriptions were provided. People do use base rates when no individuating information is available. Entrepreneurs spend too much time imagining their success and too little considering the base rate of business failure (Moore, Oesch, & Zietsma, 2007).

**Bias 4 -- Insensitivity to Sample Size:** Hospital problem (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974): most people choose "about the same" when asked which hospital (45 vs. 15 births/day) records more days with >60% boys, failing to recognize that extreme outcomes are more likely in smaller samples. "Four out of five dentists" claims are meaningless without knowing total dentists surveyed.

**Bias 5 -- Misconceptions of Chance:** People expect random sequences to "look random" -- H-T-H-T-T-H judged more likely than H-H-H-T-H-H. The gambler's fallacy: expecting chance to self-correct. The "hot hand" in basketball: exhaustive analyses show no such thing exists -- only chance patterns (Gilovich, Vallone, & Tversky, 1985; Reifman, 2011). Even research psychologists fall victim to the [[law-of-small-numbers]].

**Bias 6 -- Regression to the Mean:** People fail to predict that extreme performances will regress toward the mean. Baseball batting averages correlate only ~.41 from one year to the next. Flight instructor example (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973): praise followed by worse performance and criticism followed by improvement is simply regression, not evidence that punishment works better than reward.

**Bias 7 -- The Conjunction Fallacy:** The Linda problem (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983): most people rank "Linda is a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement" as more probable than "Linda is a bank teller" -- violating the conjunction rule that P(A and B) cannot exceed P(A). The conjunction fallacy also extends to judgments about floods and earthquakes.

### Biases from the Confirmation Heuristic

**Bias 8 -- The Confirmation Trap:** Wason's (1960) 2-4-6 task: participants test hypotheses by seeking confirmatory rather than disconfirmatory evidence. Only 6 of 29 participants found the correct rule on first guess. People assume hypotheses are true absent contrary evidence (Gilbert, 1991). Loftus (1975) implanted false memories of a barn by asking leading questions about a film. Lord, Ross, and Lepper (1979) showed that pro- and anti-death-penalty partisans both found the same mixed evidence to support their pre-existing views. Gilovich (1991): when encountering confirming information we ask "May I believe it?" but for disconfirming evidence we ask "Must I believe it?" Fischer, Greitemeyer, and Frey (2008) showed tired individuals prefer viewpoint-consistent articles even more.

**Bias 9 -- Anchoring:** Phone number/Taj Mahal experiment demonstrates anchoring from irrelevant numbers. Two mechanisms (Epley, 2004): externally set anchors trigger biased information search (Mussweiler & Strack, 1999, 2000, 2001); self-generated anchors trigger insufficient adjustment (Epley & Gilovich, 2001). Paying for accuracy does not reduce anchoring. Joyce and Biddle (1981) showed professional auditors anchored to arbitrary numbers when estimating fraud incidence (16.52 vs. 43.11 per 1000 depending on anchor). Even judges' sentencing decisions are influenced by anchors as random as dice rolls (Englich & Mussweiler, 2001; Englich, Mussweiler, & Strack, 2006). In education, teacher expectations anchored by early tracking influence actual student performance (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968). Race serves as an anchor for behavioral expectations (Duncan, 1976).

**Bias 10 -- Conjunctive and Disjunctive Events Bias:** People overestimate conjunctive probabilities (all events occurring) and underestimate disjunctive probabilities (at least one occurring). The correct order for the marble problems is C (52%), A (50%), B (48%) -- the exact opposite of intuition. This explains the planning fallacy (overestimating all steps going right) and pessimism about disjunctive opportunities (underestimating chance of getting on at least one overbooked flight -- actually 73% likely).

**Bias 11 -- Hindsight and the Curse of Knowledge:** Fischhoff (1975) showed participants told an outcome believed they would have predicted it -- hindsight bias. British/Gurkha war study: regardless of which outcome was presented as true, participants overestimated their prior prediction of it. The curse of knowledge (Camerer, Loewenstein, & Weber, 1989): people cannot ignore knowledge they have when assessing others' knowledge. Marketing experts are worse at predicting consumer preferences than non-experts because they assume consumers know as much as they do (Hoch, 1988). Keysar (1994) "David's restaurant" study: knowing David liked/disliked the restaurant biased predictions of how a friend would interpret his ambiguous note. Remedy: adopt a mindset of perceiving differences (Todd, Hanko, Galinsky, & Mussweiler, 2011).

### Integration
Table 3.2 summarizes all 12 biases and their parent heuristics. Reliance on heuristics is not always unwise -- often the time saved outweighs the quality loss. But we use heuristics unconsciously and fail to distinguish beneficial from harmful uses. When stakes are high, engaging effortful System 2 thinking to distinguish appropriate from inappropriate heuristic use is worth the effort.
