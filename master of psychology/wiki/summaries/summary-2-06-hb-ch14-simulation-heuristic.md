# The Simulation Heuristic — Summary

**Author(s):** [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]] and [[tversky|Amos Tversky]]
**Type:** Research paper (Chapter 14 in *Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases*)
**Published:** 1982, in Kahneman, Slovic, & Tversky (Eds.), Cambridge University Press
**Phase:** 2 — Decision Science
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Beyond [[representativeness]] and [[availability]], Kahneman and Tversky identify a third class of mental operation: the **simulation heuristic** -- mentally "running" a model of a situation to judge probability, causality, or closeness to alternative outcomes.
2. People answer questions about events by running something analogous to a Monte Carlo simulation in their heads. The **ease** with which a simulation reaches a particular outcome is used to judge the **propensity** of the real system to produce that outcome.
3. Mental simulation is governed by robust rules -- most notably the **downhill change rule**: people undo events by removing surprising or exceptional elements, restoring normalcy. They almost never introduce unlikely elements ("uphill changes") and never make arbitrary "horizontal" substitutions.
4. The simulation heuristic produces **counterfactual emotions** -- frustration, regret, grief, indignation -- whose intensity depends on how easily an alternative reality can be mentally constructed.
5. Scenario construction is a special application: plausible scenarios bias probability upward, and the inherent downhill bias makes plans appear more likely to succeed than they are.

## Detailed Notes

### The Core Idea: Mental Simulation as Heuristic

The simulation heuristic extends the concept of [[availability]]. Where availability-by-retrieval asks "how easily can I recall instances?", availability-by-construction asks "how easily can I mentally build a scenario?" Kahneman and Tversky propose that people routinely run mental models -- internal simulations -- to answer questions about probability, causality, and counterfactual possibility.

A simulation has adjustable parameters: starting conditions can be left at realistic defaults or modified; outcomes can be left open or targeted. The output is not a single narrative but an assessment of **ease** -- how readily the model produces various outcomes. This ease is then mapped onto judgments of probability or propensity.

### Five Uses of Mental Simulation

The paper identifies five distinct judgmental tasks served by simulation:

1. **Prediction:** Imagining how a situation will unfold (e.g., predicting how two acquaintances who have never met will interact).
2. **Probability assessment:** Assessing likelihood by constructing a path to a specified outcome (e.g., gauging the probability of U.S. military intervention in Saudi Arabia).
3. **Conditional probability:** "If X happens, what follows?" -- requires adjusting the model's initial conditions before running the simulation.
4. **Counterfactual assessment:** "How close did this come to happening differently?" (e.g., "How close did Hitler's scientists come to the atom bomb?"). This is the paper's primary focus.
5. **Causality testing:** Mentally undoing a suspected cause and observing whether the effect still occurs in the simulation (e.g., "They would have quarreled even if she hadn't mentioned his mother").

### The "Mr. Tees and Mr. Crane" Problem

The paper's most famous demonstration: Two men miss their flights by the same amount (30 minutes late). But Mr. Tees learns his flight was delayed and left just 5 minutes ago; Mr. Crane's left on time. 96% of subjects said Mr. Tees would be more upset. The objective situation is identical, yet the ease of mentally simulating "I could have made it" differs dramatically. Mr. Tees can easily imagine being 5 minutes earlier; Mr. Crane cannot easily undo a 30-minute delay. The counterfactual construction functions as a quasi-expectation, driving emotional intensity.

### Studies of Undoing: The "Mr. Jones" Accident Story

In the most elaborate experiment, subjects read about Mr. Jones, killed at an intersection after varying from his routine (different route or different departure time). They completed the stem "If only..."

Key findings:

- **Downhill changes dominate.** Subjects overwhelmingly restored normal/default values of the story -- removing exceptions and returning to routine. If the route was unusual, they undid the route; if the time was unusual, they undid the time.
- **Uphill changes are rare.** Almost no one introduced unlikely new elements.
- **Horizontal changes are nonexistent.** No one substituted one arbitrary value for another equally arbitrary one.
- **The focus rule.** Subjects modified properties of the main character (Mr. Jones), not the peripheral cause. Only 28% of subjects empathizing with Jones's family mentioned the drugged teenage driver. But 68% of subjects empathizing with the *driver's* family modified the driver's behavior.
- **Timing coincidence ignored.** Not a single subject noted that arriving at the intersection 2-3 seconds earlier would have saved Mr. Jones -- the objectively most mutable variable. Events are not undone by arbitrary alterations in continuous variables.

### Asymmetry of Psychological Distance

The paper introduces a powerful metaphor: psychological distance between possible states is **asymmetric**, like cross-country skiing. Moving from an exception to the norm it violates (downhill) is psychologically short. Moving from a norm to an exception (uphill) is psychologically long. Exceptional states are peaks; normal states are valleys. This asymmetry is the fundamental constraint that lends realism to counterfactual fantasy.

### Counterfactual Emotions

The simulation heuristic explains a cluster of emotions the authors call **counterfactual emotions**: frustration, [[regret-avoidance|regret]], grief, indignation, and envy. These share a common structure: the hedonic adaptation level is set higher than current reality because unrealized possibilities are weighted into it -- weighted by the ease with which those possibilities are reached in mental simulation. The closer the alternative outcome, the stronger the emotion.

### Scenarios and Their Biases

The paper closes with an analysis of **scenario construction** as a special case of the simulation heuristic:

- A "good" scenario bridges initial to target state via a series of downhill intermediate events with no significant uphill moves.
- Plausibility depends more on the **weakest link** than on the number of links.
- A scenario can actually **raise** the subjective probability of an event by providing plausible intermediate steps that make it feel reachable.

This produces two characteristic biases:
1. **Dramatic transition bias:** Scenarios favor dramatic causal turning points over slow, incremental change. Events produced by gradual processes are underestimated.
2. **Planning optimism bias:** Plans are inherently chains of plausible links. At each link, the expected outcome is downhill. But the cumulative probability of at least one fatal failure can be overwhelmingly high even when each individual failure probability is negligible. "Plans fail because of surprises" -- and the simulation heuristic, which is biased toward downhill changes, systematically underestimates surprise.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The simulation heuristic is framed as an extension of [[availability]], specifically "availability for construction" (as opposed to retrieval). This raises the question of whether it is a truly independent heuristic or a subtype. The authors treat it as deserving "the label of a distinctive simulation heuristic" due to the specific rules governing construction (downhill bias, focus rule, asymmetric distance).
- The paper's analysis of counterfactual emotions anticipates and grounds [[regret-avoidance]] as discussed by Bazerman & Moore ([[summary-2-08-bazerman-ch7]]). The Mr. Tees/Mr. Crane problem is essentially the same as Bazerman's missed-flight scenario, confirming that counterfactual closeness drives regret intensity.

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "We suggest that mental simulation yields a measure of the propensity of one's model of the situation to generate various outcomes, much as the propensities of a statistical model can be assessed by Monte Carlo techniques." — Kahneman & Tversky, p. 201

> "The preference for downhill changes is perhaps the major rule that mental simulations obey; it embodies the essential constraints that lend realism to counterfactual fantasies." — Kahneman & Tversky, p. 207

> "Plans fail because of surprises, occasions on which the unexpected uphill change occurs. The simulation heuristic, which is biased in favor of downhill changes, is therefore associated with a risk of large and systematic errors." — Kahneman & Tversky, p. 210
