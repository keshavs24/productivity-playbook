# Never Split the Difference Ch.5: Trigger the Two Words That Immediately Transform Any Negotiation — Summary

**Author(s):** Chris Voss, Tahl Raz
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 5
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **"That's right" is the breakthrough moment in any negotiation.** When your counterpart says "That's right," they feel understood, positively affirmed, and ready to change. It signals that they have assessed what you said and pronounced it correct of their own free will. It breaks down barriers, creates realization points, and dissolves deadlocks -- without the feeling of having given in.
2. **"You're right" is a disaster.** When someone says "you're right," they are dismissing you. They haven't internalized anything -- they just want you to shut up and go away. "You're right" produces zero behavior change. It is what people say to end a conversation, not to embrace a conclusion.
3. **Trigger "that's right" with a summary.** A good summary = paraphrasing (repeating the meaning of what was said in your own words) + labeling (acknowledging the emotions underlying that meaning). When you accurately and completely summarize your counterpart's world, the only possible response is "that's right."
4. **The Behavioral Change Stairway Model (BCSM)** is the FBI's framework: active listening → empathy → rapport → influence → behavioral change. The breakthrough moment comes when unconditional positive regard (Carl Rogers) is established -- the moment of "that's right."
5. **Unconditional positive regard opens the door to changing thoughts and behaviors.** Most people experience conditional positive regard -- love/approval dependent on doing the "right" things. This creates habits of hiding and calibrating words to gain approval. Real behavior change requires the counterpart to feel truly understood and accepted as they are.
6. **The six active listening tools for triggering "that's right":** (1) Effective pauses/silence, (2) Minimal encouragers ("Yes," "OK," "Uh-huh"), (3) [[mirroring-technique|Mirroring]], (4) [[labeling-emotions|Labeling]], (5) Paraphrasing, (6) Summarizing (paraphrasing + labeling).

## Detailed Notes

### I. The Jeffrey Schilling Kidnapping (Philippines, 2000)

Abu Sayyaf (militant Islamic group in the southern Philippines) kidnaps Jeffrey Schilling, a 24-year-old American, on Jolo Island. $10 million ransom demanded. Voss is assigned as Supervisory Special Agent to the FBI's Crisis Negotiation Unit (CNU) at Quantico and flies to Manila to run the negotiation strategy for the U.S. government. This case makes him the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator.

**The adversary:** Abu Sabaya, the rebel leader -- a veteran terrorist-sociopath-killer with a history of rape, murder, and beheadings. He loved media attention, wore sunglasses and a bandana, had Philippine reporters on speed dial, and answered in English because "they should make a movie about me."

**Sabaya's price calculation:** U.S. was offering $5M for 1993 WTC bombing fugitives; a rival Abu Sayyaf faction got $20M (from Libya's Gaddafi) for six Western European captives. Sabaya figures Schilling is worth $10M. Problem: Schilling's family is working-class; the U.S. won't pay ransom (but would allow a payment as a "sting" operation).

**The negotiation proxy:** Benjie, a Filipino military officer (leader of the Philippine National Police's Special Action Force), negotiates directly with Sabaya in Tagalog. Voss reviews translated transcripts and advises Benjie.

### II. The First "That's Right" Breakthrough (with Benjie)

Benjie wanted a hard-line approach -- direct, no-nonsense, no rapport with the enemy. He found Voss's rapport-building strategy distasteful.

Voss recognized he needed to negotiate with Benjie first. He led with a label: "You hate Sabaya, don't you?" Benjie unloaded: "I tell you I *do*! He has murdered and raped. He came on our radio when we were lobbing mortars on his position and said 'these mortars are music to my ears.' I heard his voice come on our radio one day and celebrate that he was standing over the body of one of my men."

This outburst was Benjie's "that's right" -- acknowledging his rage and hearing it reflected. He calmed down, became a superstar negotiator from that point forward.

### III. Trigger a "That's Right!" with a Summary

After four months, Sabaya still refused to budge on the $10 million "war damages" demand. Voss decided to reposition Sabaya with his own words.

**The six-tool active listening arsenal:**

1. **Effective Pauses:** Silence is powerful. Use it for emphasis, to encourage Sabaya to keep talking until the emotions drain.
2. **Minimal Encouragers:** Simple phrases -- "Yes," "OK," "Uh-huh," "I see" -- to convey full attention.
3. **[[mirroring-technique|Mirroring]]:** Rather than argue, Benjie would listen and repeat back what Sabaya said.
4. **[[labeling-emotions|Labeling]]:** Give Sabaya's feelings a name -- "It all seems so tragically unfair, I can now see why you sound so angry."
5. **Paraphrasing:** Repeat what Sabaya is saying in Benjie's own words to powerfully show understanding beyond parroting.
6. **Summarizing:** The combination of rearticulating the meaning of what is said *plus* acknowledging the emotions underlying that meaning. Paraphrasing + labeling = summary. Benjie needed to fully and completely summarize "the world according to Abu Sabaya" -- all the war damages, fishing rights, five hundred years of oppression.

**The breakthrough call:** Two days later, Sabaya phones. Benjie listens, follows the script -- mirroring, encouraging, labeling. Then Benjie repeats in Sabaya's own words his version of history and the emotions that came with it.

Sabaya was silent for nearly a minute.
"That's right," he said.

The "war damages" demand disappeared. From that point forward, Sabaya never mentioned money again. He became so weary of the case that he let down his guard. Schilling escaped, Philippine commandoes rescued him. He returned safely to California.

Two weeks later, Sabaya called Benjie: "Have you been promoted yet? If not, you should have been. I was going to hurt Jeffrey. I don't know what you did to keep me from doing that, but whatever it was, it worked." (Sabaya was killed in a shoot-out with Philippine military in June 2002.)

### IV. "That's Right" Is Great, but "You're Right" Changes Nothing

**The Brandon story:** Voss's son Brandon, a 6'2", 250-lb football player, was moved from lineman to linebacker. He was supposed to dodge blockers and get to the ball, but he kept hitting blockers head-on. Both his coach and Voss tried explaining why he needed to change. Every time: "You're right." And every time, he went right back to hitting.

"You're right" is what people say to make you shut up and leave them alone. It creates a happy smile for about 24 hours. But there is no internalization, no behavior change, no ownership.

Voss took Brandon aside before a crucial game and used a label: "You seem to think it's unmanly to dodge a block. You think it's cowardly to get out of someone's way that's trying to hit you." Brandon stared. "That's right." He embraced the reality of what was holding him back, understood why, and changed course. He became an exceptionally fine linebacker.

### V. Using "That's Right" to Make the Sale

A pharmaceutical sales rep was being blocked by a doctor who dismissed her product. She stopped pitching and started summarizing his world: "The last time I was in, we spoke about your patients with this condition, and I remember thinking that you seemed very passionate about treating them, and how you worked hard to tailor the specific treatment to each and every patient."

"That's right," the doctor said, looking at her as if seeing her for the first time. The walls came down. She let him describe his treatment and procedures, then paraphrased the challenges of his practice back to him.

"You seem to tailor specific treatments and medications for each patient." "That's right." This was the breakthrough -- he dropped his guard, she learned how her medication would fit, and eventually he agreed to try it. She made the sale.

### VI. Using "That's Right" for Career Success

A Korean MBA student needed his ex-boss's approval to transfer from the semiconductor division to consumer electronics. The ex-boss refused. The student asked calibrated questions:

"Is there any reason you want me to go to the semiconductor headquarters?"
"It's the best position for you."
"It sounds like there's no regulation that I have to remain with the semiconductor division."
"Hmm, I don't think there is any."
"So it sounds like you could approve my new position no matter which division, as long as I was in headquarters and could help you communicate better with the top managers."
**"That's right."** The ex-boss admitted he needed an ally in headquarters and was up for VP promotion.

"I would help you in any way. But I could help with the networking and also talk you up to the CEO even if I were at the consumer products division, right?"
**"That's right."** "If you get an offer from the consumer products unit, I will approve it."

By asking questions that led to "that's right," the student achieved his goal *and* uncovered two Black Swans: the boss needed a networking ally and was seeking a VP promotion.

### VII. Key Lessons

- **Creating unconditional positive regard** opens the door to changing thoughts and behaviors. The more a person feels understood and positively affirmed, the more likely constructive behavior will take hold.
- **"That's right" is better than "yes."** Reaching "that's right" in a negotiation creates breakthroughs.
- **Use a summary to trigger "that's right."** The building blocks of a good summary are a label combined with paraphrasing. Identify, rearticulate, and emotionally affirm "the world according to..."

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Builds on Carl Rogers' unconditional positive regard:** Voss explicitly traces the Behavioral Change Stairway Model back to Rogers. The insight that conditional regard produces people who hide their true selves and calibrate words for approval connects to [[self-determination-theory]]'s concept of contingent self-esteem and introjected regulation.
- **Complements [[three-conversations-framework]]:** The summary technique (paraphrasing + labeling) is very close to what Stone/Patton/Heen describe as "the third story" -- articulating the situation from a perspective that encompasses both sides' views. The key difference is that Voss's summary is explicitly designed to articulate *their* side so completely that they say "that's right," rather than seeking a neutral third perspective.
