# Predictably Irrational Ch.11: The Power of Price — Summary

**Author(s):** Dan Ariely
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Price acts as a placebo — expensive treatments are perceived as more effective AND actually produce better objective outcomes than cheap ones
2. Placebo surgery is as effective as real surgery for some conditions (mammary artery ligation, knee arthroscopy)
3. Two mechanisms drive placebos: belief/confidence and conditioning (Pavlovian expectancy)
4. Discounted products are unconsciously assumed to be inferior, and this assumption becomes self-fulfilling — but deliberate reflection can break the effect

## Detailed Notes

### Placebo Surgery: Internal Mammary Artery Ligation
**Year:** 1955. **Researcher:** Leonard Cobb, cardiologist in Seattle.

**Procedure:** Half the patients received real surgery (opening chest, tying up the internal mammary artery). The other half received placebo surgery (merely cutting into the flesh with a scalpel, making two incisions, nothing else).

**Result:** Both groups reported immediate relief from chest pain. Relief lasted about three months in both groups, then returned. Electrocardiograms showed no difference. Neither procedure provided significant long-term relief — the traditional procedure was no better than the placebo.

### Placebo Knee Surgery (Moseley, 2002)
**Setup:** 180 patients with osteoarthritis. Three groups: (1) full surgery (scopes, cartilage removal, saline wash), (2) partial surgery (scopes, saline, no cartilage removal), (3) simulated surgery (anesthesia, incisions, same time, but no instruments inserted).

**Result:** For two years, all three groups showed equal improvement in pain and walking. The placebo group got the same relief as the real surgery groups. Published as lead article in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (July 2002).

**Quote from Dr. Nelda Wray:** "The fact that the effectiveness of arthroscopic lavage and debridement in patients with osteoarthritis of the knee is no greater than that of placebo surgery makes us question whether the $1 billion spent on these procedures might be put to better use."

### History of Placebos
- Latin origin: "I shall please" — 14th century term for sham mourners hired to wail at funerals
- 1785: Appeared in *New Medical Dictionary* for marginal medical practices
- 1794: Italian physician Gerbi treated toothaches with worm secretions — 68% reported pain relief for a year
- Historical placebos: eye of the toad, wing of the bat, dried fox lungs, mercury, mineral water, cocaine, electric current
- Egyptian mummy powder — remedy for epilepsy, abscesses, rashes, fractures, paralysis, migraine. Orderable from E. Merck catalog as late as 1908
- The "royal touch" — kings healing by touch, continued until the 1820s. Charles II reportedly touched 100,000 people.
- Modern examples: 75% of antidepressant effect duplicated by placebo. Sham brain surgery for Parkinson's had same outcome as real surgery.

### Two Mechanisms of Placebo
1. **Belief/confidence:** Faith in the drug, procedure, or caregiver triggers internal healing processes. A doctor's enthusiasm for a treatment can play a real role in its efficacy.
2. **Conditioning (Pavlovian):** The body builds up expectancy after repeated experiences and releases chemicals (endorphins, opiates) in anticipation. Ariely recalls lying in the burn ward — the sight of the nurse approaching with a painkiller needle triggered pain relief even before the needle broke his skin.

### The Veladone-Rx Experiment (Waber, Shiv, Carmon, and Ariely)
**Setup:** ~100 adult Bostonians at MIT Media Lab. Elaborate staging: Taya Leary in a business suit from "Vel Pharmaceuticals," medical office with brochures, Time and Newsweek magazines. Participants read about "Veladone-Rx," a new opioid painkiller ($2.50 per dose). Then electrical shocks were administered before and after taking the pill.

**Result:** Almost all participants reported less pain after taking Veladone-Rx. The pill was a capsule of vitamin C.

**Price manipulation:** When the brochure showed the price discounted from $2.50 to just 10 cents, only half of participants experienced pain relief (vs. nearly all at $2.50). The price-placebo relationship was especially pronounced for people with more experience with pain medications.

### University of Iowa Cold Medicine Study
Students tracked whether they used full-price or discount cold medicines. 13 paid list price, 16 bought discount drugs. The 13 who paid list price reported significantly better outcomes.

### SoBe Adrenaline Rush Experiments
**Experiment 1 — Subjective fatigue:** Students at the gym drank SoBe at full price or one-third price. Both felt less fatigued, but full-price drinkers reported LESS fatigue.

**Experiment 2 — Objective anagram performance:** Students solved 15-word anagram puzzles. Baseline (no drink): 9 correct. Full-price SoBe: ~9 correct. Discounted SoBe: 6.5 correct — a 28% performance gap.

**Experiment 3 — With priming message:** When a message stated "Drinks such as SoBe have been shown to improve mental functioning" (plus fictional reference to 50 studies), discount drinkers improved by 0.6 questions, but full-price drinkers improved by 3.3 additional questions. The message on the bottle was arguably more powerful than the beverage itself.

### Breaking the Price-Placebo Effect
**Key question:** Are we doomed to get lower benefits from discounts?

**Answer:** If we stop and rationally consider the product versus the price, we can break free. Experiments showed consumers who reflected on the price-quality relationship were far less likely to assume a discounted drink was less effective, and consequently didn't perform as poorly on word puzzles. The discount effect is largely an unconscious reaction.

### Ariely's Personal Placebo Story — The Jobst Suit
After his burn injury, Ariely was given a Jobst compression suit (custom-made synthetic garment from Ireland) that was supposed to help his scars heal. Shula the physiotherapist built up his expectations enormously. The suit turned out to be intensely painful — itching, heat, tearing of delicate new skin. After months of suffering, he learned the suit had no real benefits. The areas covered looked no different from uncovered areas. This was a placebo treatment that caused real suffering because it had never been properly tested against alternatives.

### Reflections: Airborne
Ariely recounts becoming a devoted user of Airborne (fizzy vitamin tablet marketed to prevent colds). The bubbles, the ritual, the placebo effect all made him feel better. Then the company was forced to pay $23.3 million for false advertising. Knowing it was "just some dumb vitamin with neat Alka-Seltzer special effects" destroyed his ability to enjoy the placebo benefit.
