# Shortcomings in the Attribution Process — Summary

**Author(s):** Lee Ross and Craig A. Anderson
**Type:** Book chapter (Ch. 9 in *Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases*, 1982)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **The fundamental attribution error** -- the tendency to overweight dispositional factors and underweight situational factors -- is pervasive and constitutes the single most important non-motivational bias in attribution, first identified by Heider (1958) and named by Ross (1977).
2. **Belief perseverance** is remarkably robust: beliefs survive logically devastating challenges, including complete evidential discrediting, and over 50% of the initial effect of totally fictitious "case history" information persists even after thorough debriefing.
3. **The false consensus effect** -- people systematically overestimate the commonness of their own behavioral choices and see alternative responses as more revealing of others' dispositions -- results from both selective exposure to like-minded others and the cognitive availability of one's own behavioral tendencies.
4. **Biased assimilation** of new evidence means that exposure to the same mixed evidence actually *polarizes* opposing factions further apart, rather than bringing them toward a common position (Lord, Ross & Lepper, 1979 capital punishment study).
5. Three mechanisms underlie belief perseverance: (a) biased search, recollection, and assimilation of information; (b) the formation of causal explanations that become self-sustaining; and (c) behavioral confirmation / self-fulfilling prophecies.

## Detailed Notes

### Introduction to Attribution Theory and Attributional Errors

Ross and Anderson frame attribution theory as the study of "intuitive psychology" -- the attempts of ordinary people to understand causes and implications of events. The intuitive scientist, like the professional one, is guided by implicit assumptions, relies on data, and employs heuristic strategies. Three core attribution tasks are identified:

1. **Causal judgment** -- identifying causes for effects
2. **Social inference** -- forming inferences about actors' attributes/dispositions
3. **Prediction/estimation** -- forming expectations about future outcomes and behavior

The chapter focuses on non-motivational biases rather than self-serving motivational distortions.

### Logical Attributional Principles vs. Self-Serving Biases

**The covariance and discounting principles:**
- H.H. Kelley distinguished two cases: (a) *multiple observation* cases where the attributer applies the "covariance principle" (behavior covaries with causes), and (b) *single observation* cases where the attributer applies the "discounting principle" (each cause is discounted to the extent other plausible causes exist).
- The covariance principle requires statistical/logical skill; the discounting principle requires psychological insight about human nature and situational forces.
- Evidence from questionnaire studies (Jones, Davis, & Gergen, 1961; Jones & Harris, 1967; Strickland, 1958; Thibaut & Riecken, 1955) shows attributers make some use of these principles, but the *degree* of accuracy and error direction remain ambiguous.

**Self-serving motivational biases:**
- Theorists initially postulated "ego-defensive" biases: attributing successes to self, failures to external factors (Davis & Davis, 1972; Feather, 1969; Fitch, 1970).
- Counterevidence: Ross, Bierbrauer, and Polly (1974) found *counterdefensive* tendencies -- instructors rated their own performance as more important for failures, and their learner's abilities as more critical for successes. Professional teachers showed this pattern even more strongly.
- The motivational vs. non-motivational debate has become irresolvable in practice (cf. Bradley, 1978). Ross and Anderson advocate temporarily setting aside motivational constructs to focus on informational, perceptual, and cognitive factors.

### Non-Motivational Attribution Biases

#### The Fundamental Attribution Error

The [[fundamental-attribution-error]] is defined as "the tendency for attributers to underestimate the impact of situational factors and to overestimate the role of dispositional factors in controlling behavior" (first identified by Heider, 1958).

**General evidence:**
- **Jones & Harris (1967):** Listeners assumed correspondence between communicators' pro-Castro remarks and their private opinions even when listeners *knew* the communicators were obeying the experimenter's explicit request under no-choice conditions.
- **Bierbrauer (1973):** Participants witnessed a verbatim reenactment of one subject's "obedience" in the Milgram (1963) situation, delivering maximum shock to the supposed victim. Regardless of type and amount of delay before judging, regardless of whether they actually played the role or merely observed, participants consistently and dramatically underestimated the degree to which subjects in general would yield to the situational forces. They attributed the particular subject's obedience to his personal dispositions.

**The special case of role-conferred advantages in self-presentation:**
- **Ross, Amabile, & Steinmetz (1977) quiz game study:** Subjects randomly assigned the role of "questioner" in a general-knowledge quiz composed challenging questions from their own esoteric knowledge domains. Contestants had to answer. Despite the *blatant* arbitrariness of role assignment, both contestants and uninvolved observers rated questioners as far superior in general knowledge. Questioners themselves were not misled -- having additional information about the limitations of their knowledge, they rated themselves and contestants as "average." The observers concluded questioners were truly outstanding.
- Real-world implications: formal and informal social roles constrain interpersonal encounters and bias impressions, justifying the prerogatives and limitations of advantaged and disadvantaged roles.

#### Salience or Availability Biases

- Whenever an aspect of the environment is disproportionately salient or "available" to the perceiver, that aspect is given more weight in causal attribution (cf. Tversky & Kahneman, 1973, [[availability]]).
- **Taylor et al. (1976):** An actor made visually salient within a group (by unique racial or sexual status, striking appearance/dress) is assigned disproportionate responsibility for outcomes.
- **McArthur & Post (1977); McArthur & Soloman (1978):** Being salient due to appearance or dress increases attributed causal responsibility.
- **Regan & Totten (1975):** Instructional set can shift attention and thus attribution.
- **Storms (1973); Taylor & Fiske (1975):** Seating arrangements and visual perspective determine attribution -- the person you can see is judged more causally responsible.
- The [[fundamental-attribution-error]] may importantly reflect the fact that actors are simply more salient than situational features.
- **Jones & Nisbett (1971):** Actors attend to their environment; observers attend to actors. This actor-observer asymmetry is a special case of the attention/attribution generalization. Manipulating focus of attention (Storms, 1973; Taylor & Fiske, 1975) correspondingly manipulates situational vs. dispositional attributions.
- **Non-occurrences** (Sherlock Holmes's "dog that didn't bark") are informationally valuable but cognitively unavailable, and therefore systematically underweighted in attribution (Ross, 1977).

#### The False Consensus or Egocentric Attribution Bias

The [[false-consensus-effect]] is defined as people's tendency to see their own behavioral choices and judgments as relatively common and appropriate while viewing alternative responses as uncommon, deviant, and revealing of the respondent's dispositions.

**Ross, Greene, & House (1977) studies:**
- *Study 1 (hypothetical questionnaire):* Subjects read descriptions of hypothetical conflict situations, estimated the commonness of two response alternatives, indicated their own choice, and assessed the traits of typical people who would follow each alternative. Results: subjects estimated the alternative they chose as more common; they also made stronger trait inferences about people who chose the *opposite* alternative.
- *Study 2 ("Eat at Joe's" sandwich-board study):* A real behavioral conflict -- subjects asked to walk around campus for 30 minutes wearing a large sandwich-board sign saying "eat at joe's." Those who agreed estimated 62% of their peers would do the same; those who refused estimated only 33% would agree. Both groups made stronger trait inferences about the person who chose differently from themselves.

**Mechanisms underlying false consensus:**
1. **Selective exposure:** People associate with those who share their values and behavioral tendencies, creating a biased sample
2. **Availability:** Behaviors we have chosen or would choose are more cognitively "available" (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973) -- more easily recalled, visualized, or imagined
3. **Ambiguity resolution:** People resolve ambiguous situational features in accord with their own behavioral tendencies, then assume others resolve them similarly

**Implications:** Attributional analyses are distorted not only by errors in data analysis but by earlier biases in sampling and estimating social data. Lay estimates of deviance and normalcy are systematically and egocentrically biased.

### Belief Perseverance in the Face of Empirical Challenges

#### Belief Perseverance and Polarization in the Face of New Data

**Lord, Lepper, & Ross (1979) capital punishment study:**
- Selected subjects who strongly supported or opposed capital punishment and believed it was or was not an effective deterrent.
- Presented both groups with the *same* two purportedly authentic empirical studies -- one seemingly supporting and one seemingly opposing the deterrence position.
- Results: Both groups consistently rated the study supporting their position as "more convincing" and "better conducted." The net effect of reading both studies was to *polarize* opponents and proponents further apart -- exactly contrary to any normative strategy.
- Mechanism: Upon reading a brief result statement supporting their view, subjects' beliefs became considerably more extreme; these changes were maintained when procedural details were read. Upon reading an opposing result statement, beliefs became only slightly less extreme; and upon reading the details, subjects tended to *revert* to the beliefs held before learning of the study.

#### Belief Perseverance After Evidential Discrediting

**The debriefing paradigm (Ross, Lepper, & Hubbard, 1975):**
- Subjects received continuous false feedback as they performed a novel discrimination task (distinguishing authentic from fictitious suicide notes).
- *Experiment 1:* False feedback manipulated subjects' perceptions of their own performance and ability. After total debriefing (revealing the feedback was predetermined and unrelated to actual performance), subjects still showed significant "residual" effects on every measure: estimated initial performance, predicted future performance, and rated ability.
- *Experiment 2:* Introduced observers who formed social impressions as they witnessed the false feedback. After total debriefing, observers' assessments also persevered.
- Table 2 data: "Success" subjects estimated 18.33 correct (vs. 12.83 for "failure"), predicted 18.33 future correct (vs. 14.25), and rated their ability at 5.00 (vs. 3.83) -- all *after* complete debriefing. All differences statistically significant (p < .05 to p < .001).

**Follow-up replications:**
- **Jennings, Lepper, & Ross (1980):** Subjects' impressions of their ability at interpersonal persuasion (having them succeed or fail at convincing a confederate to donate blood) persisted after learning the outcome was totally inauthentic.
- **Lepper, Ross, & Lau (1979):** Students' erroneous impressions of their "logical problem solving abilities" persevered even two months later, affecting academic course choices.

**Anderson, Lepper, & Ross (1980) -- extending to broader theories:**
- Studies first manipulated then attempted to undermine subjects' theories about the functional relationship between firefighters' professional performances and their prior scores on a risk-taking preference test.
- Formative evidence consisted of *only one pair* of specific cases (one successful and one unsuccessful firefighter with appropriately discrepant risk-taking scores).
- Interestingly, such minimal data sufficed to produce strong theories about the relationship.
- More importantly, these theories survived the revelation that the cases were totally fictitious and different subjects had received opposite pairings.
- Over 50% of the initial effect of the "case history" information remained after debriefing.

### Possible Mechanisms Underlying Belief Perseverance

#### Biased Search, Recollection, and Assimilation of Information

- [[confirmation-heuristic|Confirmation biases]] (Einhorn & Hogarth, 1978; Hamilton, 1979; Hastie & Kumar, 1979; Wason & Johnson-Laird, 1972) have long been noted by philosophers of science (Bacon, 1620/1960).
- **Lord et al. (1979):** Potentially confirmatory evidence is taken at face value; potentially disconfirmatory evidence is subjected to highly critical, skeptical scrutiny.
- Two consequences: (1) any pattern of evidence, even essentially random, tends to *bolster* the initial belief; (2) once evidence is processed this way, the belief gains capacity to *sustain* itself against new disconfirmation or attacks on its original evidential basis.
- Critical assumption: people do not constantly update or reevaluate evidence relevant to their beliefs when the basis is undermined. They do not "go back and reassess all of the evidence."

#### The Formation of Causal Explanations

- People do more than note evidence -- they engage in causal analysis and explanation (Heider, 1958). They try to *account for* the characteristics of self or others, or for functional relationships they have come to believe exist.
- A subject induced to believe a positive or negative relationship between firefighting and risk preference will readily postulate a logical basis for either relationship.
- **Anderson et al. (1980):** Explicitly requiring subjects to formulate causal explanations *before* debriefing greatly enhanced the perseverance effect. Subjects who explained the relationship before debriefing were only trivially less certain than subjects who received no debriefing at all.
- **Ross, Lepper, Strack, & Steinmetz (1977):** Subjects induced to explain outcomes in the lives of clinical patients continued to regard those outcomes as relatively likely even after learning the events were inauthentic and contrived.

#### Behavioral Confirmation or "Self-Fulfilling" Hypotheses

- Actions based on beliefs create new data that may confirm the beliefs (Festinger, 1957; Ashmore & Collins, 1968; Collins & Hoyt, 1972; Hovland, Campbell, & Brock, 1957).
- **Rosenthal & Jacobson (1968) "Pygmalion" studies:** Teachers' expectations about students' abilities influenced students' actual performance.
- **Snyder and colleagues (Snyder & Swann, 1978a, 1978b; Snyder, Tanke, & Berscheid, 1977):** Subjects' expectations or hypotheses they are testing can generate "objective support" through the manner in which they interact, thereby confirming even initially erroneous beliefs.

### Concluding Remarks: Beliefs Do Change!

Ross and Anderson conclude that beliefs *do* change, even if logical and empirical challenges have less impact than normative standards would warrant. Change may come through:
- Brute force of persistent disconfirming evidence
- Formal hypothesis testing methods that protect against informal reasoning dangers
- **Vivid, concrete, first-hand experience** (see Nisbett & Ross, 1980) -- the same type of information that the [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]] principle predicts will be most impactful
- The effectiveness of groups and leaders that accomplish dramatic political or religious conversions

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The motivational vs. non-motivational account of attributional biases remains unresolved. Ross and Anderson deliberately set aside motivational biases, but acknowledge they cannot be definitively ruled out.
- The chapter argues that ego-defensive biases may actually be *counterdefensive* in some contexts (Ross, Bierbrauer, & Polly, 1974), challenging the common assumption.
