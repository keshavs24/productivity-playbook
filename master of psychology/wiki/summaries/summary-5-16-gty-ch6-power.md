# Getting to Yes Ch.6: What If They Are More Powerful? — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 6
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **No method can guarantee success if all the leverage lies on the other side.** In response to power, the most any method of negotiation can do is meet two objectives: protect you against making an agreement you should reject, and help you make the most of the assets you have.
2. **The cost of using a bottom line.** Negotiators commonly try to protect themselves by establishing a bottom line — the worst acceptable outcome. But a bottom line is rigid, inhibits imagination, and is almost certain to be set too high or too low. It provides no measure of what you should accept.
3. **Know your BATNA — your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement.** This is the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured. It is the only standard that can protect you both from accepting terms that are too unfavorable and from rejecting terms it would be in your interest to accept.
4. **The better your BATNA, the greater your power.** The relative negotiating power of two parties depends primarily upon how attractive to each is the option of not reaching agreement.
5. **Develop your BATNA.** Attractive alternatives are not just sitting there waiting for you. Generate them through three operations: (1) invent a list of actions you might conceivably take if no agreement is reached, (2) improve some of the more promising ideas and convert them into practical alternatives, (3) select the one that seems best.

## Detailed Notes

### Protecting Yourself

**The cost of using a bottom line.** A bottom line is the highest price you would pay (if buying) or the lowest you would accept (if selling). Having one limits your ability to benefit from what you learn during negotiation. By definition, a bottom line is rigid. It also inhibits imagination — rather than inventing a tailor-made solution which would reconcile differing interests in a way more advantageous for both, you lock in.

Moreover, a bottom line is almost certain to be set wrong. It may be too high (selling the house at $200,000 when $180,000 is realistic) or too low (selling a figure when you should have held for better). It provides no measure of what you *should* accept.

### Know Your BATNA

**The key question:** When a family is deciding on the minimum price for their house, the right question is not what they "ought" to be able to get, but what they will do if by a certain time they have not sold the house. Will they keep it on the market indefinitely? Rent it? Tear it down? Let someone else live in it rent-free? Which of those alternatives is most attractive, all things considered?

**Your BATNA is the standard.** The reason you negotiate is to produce something better than the results you can obtain without negotiating. Your [[batna|BATNA]] — your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — is the standard against which any proposed agreement should be measured. It is the *only* standard that can protect you both from accepting too-unfavorable terms and from rejecting terms you should accept.

**The insecurity of an unknown BATNA.** If you have not thought carefully about what you will do if you fail to reach an agreement, you are negotiating with your eyes closed. You may be too optimistic (assuming you have many options) or too pessimistic (underappreciating the full agony of not reaching agreement).

**Formulate a trip wire.** Although your BATNA is the true measure by which you should judge any proposed agreement, you may want another test as well — a trip wire that gives you early warning that the content of a possible agreement is beginning to run the risk of being too unattractive. Like a bottom line, a trip wire can limit the authority of an agent. But unlike a bottom line, it provides some margin for maneuver.

### Making the Most of Your Assets

**The better your BATNA, the greater your power.** People think of negotiating power as being determined by resources like wealth, political connections, physical strength, friends, and military might. In fact, the relative negotiating power of two parties depends primarily upon how attractive to each is the option of not reaching agreement.

**The wealthy tourist and the Bombay vendor.** A wealthy tourist who wants to buy a brass pot from a Bombay railroad station vendor may be wealthy and "powerful," but in this negotiation he will be weak indeed unless he knows approximately how much it would cost and how difficult it would be to find a comparable pot elsewhere. The tourist's wealth in no way strengthens his negotiating power.

**Develop your BATNA.** Vigorous exploration of what you will do if you do not reach agreement can greatly strengthen your hand. Three operations:
1. **Invent** a list of actions you might conceivably take if no agreement is reached
2. **Improve** some of the more promising ideas and convert them into practical alternatives
3. **Select** the one that seems best

**Consider the other side's BATNA.** You should also think about the alternatives to a negotiated agreement available to the other side. They may be unduly optimistic about what they can do if no agreement is reached. The more you can learn of their options, the better prepared you are for negotiation.

**The small town vs. the corporation.** A small town negotiated a "goodwill" payment of $300,000 per year to $2,300,000 per year. The corporation had committed itself to keeping the factory; it had developed no alternative to reaching agreement. Having an attractive BATNA, the small town had more ability to affect the outcome than did one of the world's largest corporations.

### When the Other Side Is Powerful

Having a good BATNA can help you negotiate on the merits. You can convert resources you have into effective negotiating power by developing and improving your BATNA. The more easily and happily you can walk away from a negotiation, the greater your capacity to affect its outcome.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The BATNA concept directly challenges the common assumption that negotiation power comes from resources — connecting to [[framing-effects]] (power is a matter of perception and alternatives, not raw assets)
- Bottom lines connect to [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — a self-imposed anchor that constrains rather than helps
- The emphasis on developing alternatives before negotiating connects to preparation research in [[cognitive-barriers-to-resolution]]
