# On Rhetoric: Book 1 — Summary

**Author(s):** Aristotle (translated by George A. Kennedy)
**Type:** Book (pp. 27-110)
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Rhetoric is the counterpart (antistrophos) of dialectic.** Both deal with probabilities and commonly held opinions (*endoxa*), not scientific certainties. Both are universal methods, not confined to any specific subject matter. The difference: dialectic proceeds by question and answer, rhetoric by continuous public address.
2. **Rhetoric is defined as "an ability, in each particular case, to see the available means of persuasion."** It is not the act of persuading, but the faculty of identifying what is persuasive — a theoretical and practical capacity.
3. **The three pisteis (means of persuasion) are [[ethos]], [[pathos]], and [[logos]]** — character, emotion, and argument. These are the "artistic" proofs the speaker invents, as opposed to "non-artistic" (atechnic) proofs like laws, witnesses, contracts, and oaths.
4. **The [[enthymeme]] (rhetorical syllogism) and paradigm (rhetorical induction) are the two forms of logical proof.** Enthymemes are drawn from fewer premises than full syllogisms and deal with probabilities. Paradigms use historical examples or fables.
5. **The three species of rhetoric each have a distinct audience, time orientation, and end goal** — deliberative (assembly, future, the advantageous), judicial (jury, past, justice), epideictic (observer, present, the honorable).

## Detailed Notes

### Chapters 1-3: Introduction and Definitions

#### Chapter 1: Rhetoric and Dialectic
- Rhetoric is the *antistrophos* (counterpart/coordinate) of dialectic — both deal with things knowable by ordinary people, not specialized sciences
- Previous handbook writers focused wrongly on emotional appeals and judicial rhetoric, neglecting logical argument and deliberative rhetoric
- Rhetoric is useful because: (a) truth and justice are naturally stronger than their opposites; (b) audiences cannot follow exact scientific demonstration; (c) one should be able to argue both sides; (d) verbal self-defense is more characteristically human than physical self-defense
- The [[enthymeme]] is called the "body" (*soma*) of persuasion — the primary tool of rhetorical proof
- Rhetoric is not a matter of ability but of deliberate choice (*proairesis*); the difference between a *rhetor* (speaker) and a *sophist* is moral intent, not technique

#### Chapter 2: Definition of Rhetoric and the Three Pisteis
- **Definition:** "Let rhetoric be [defined as] an ability, in each particular case, to see the available means of persuasion" (1.2.1)
- **Non-artistic (atechnic) proofs:** preexisting evidence — laws, witnesses, contracts, torture testimony, oaths
- **Artistic (entechnic) proofs** constructed by the speaker, of three species:
  1. **[[Ethos]]** — character projected through the speech making the speaker seem trustworthy; "almost the most authoritative form of persuasion" (1.2.4)
  2. **[[Pathos]]** — leading hearers to feel emotion by the speech, since "we do not give the same judgment when grieved and rejoicing or when being friendly and hostile" (1.2.5)
  3. **[[Logos]]** — showing truth or apparent truth through the persuasive arguments in each case (1.2.6)
- **Two forms of logical proof:**
  - **[[Enthymeme]]** (rhetorical syllogism) — deductive, drawn from stated or implied premises; the more common and powerful form
  - **Paradigm** (rhetorical example/induction) — drawing particular conclusions from parallel cases; less logically powerful but more audience-friendly
- Enthymemes are derived from *eikota* (probabilities) and *semeia* (signs); signs can be necessary (*tekmeria*) or refutable
- **[[Topoi]]** (topics) are distinguished into *koina* (common topics, applicable to all subjects) and *idia* (specific topics, belonging to particular disciplines like politics or ethics)

#### Chapter 3: The Three Species of Rhetoric
- A speech has three elements: speaker, subject, audience. The audience is either an observer (*theoros*) or a judge (*krites*) of past or future events.
- The **[[three-genres-of-rhetoric]]**:
  - **Deliberative** (*symbouleutikon*): advising/dissuading about future action; end = the advantageous (*sympheron*)
  - **Judicial** (*dikanikon*): accusing/defending past actions; end = the just (*dikaion*)
  - **Epideictic** (*epideiktikon*): praising/blaming present qualities; end = the honorable (*kalon*)
- Four common propositions (*koina*) applicable to all species: the possible/impossible, past fact, future fact, and degree of magnitude

### Chapters 4-8: Topics for Deliberative Rhetoric

#### Chapter 4: Political Topics
- Five main subjects of deliberation: finances, war and peace, national defense, imports/exports, and legislation
- The deliberative speaker must know the forms of constitution (democracy, oligarchy, aristocracy, monarchy) and the "end" of each — for democracy it is freedom, for oligarchy wealth, for aristocracy education and law, for tyranny self-preservation

#### Chapter 5: Ethical Topics — Happiness
- The goal of all human action is happiness (*eudaimonia*)
- Happiness defined as "success combined with virtue, or self-sufficiency in life, or the pleasantest life accompanied with security" (1.5.3)
- Parts of happiness: good birth, good children, wealth, good reputation, honor, health, beauty, strength, stature, athletic ability, good old age, many friendships, good luck, virtue

#### Chapter 6: The Good
- Good defined as "whatever is chosen for itself" and what intelligence or practical wisdom would choose (1.6.2)
- Extensive catalog of goods: happiness, virtues, pleasure, health, wealth, friends, honor, speech ability, natural talent, knowledge, life, justice
- Key argumentative principle: "the opposite of what enemies want or rejoice at is good" (1.6.19-20)

#### Chapter 7: The Greater Good (Degree of Magnitude)
- 40+ strategies for arguing that one thing is greater/better/more important than another
- Examples: the scarcer is greater than the abundant; what is chosen for itself is greater than what is chosen for something else; what the wise would choose is greater; the cause of greater goods is greater
- This *koinon* (common topic) of degree applies to all three species of rhetoric

#### Chapter 8: Constitutions
- The four forms of constitution and what is persuasive in each
- Character (*ethos*) appropriate to each type of constitution is distinct — a speaker must adapt to the audience's political values

### Chapter 9: Epideictic Rhetoric — The Honorable

- Virtue (*arete*) is defined as "an ability for doing good" and as "productive and preservative of goods" (1.9.4)
- The parts of virtue: justice, courage, self-control, magnanimity, magnificence, liberality, prudence, wisdom
- The greatest virtues are those most useful to others — hence courage and justice are most honored
- **Amplification** (*auxesis*) is the most characteristic device of epideictic rhetoric — showing the subject is first, only, or exceptional
- Key technique: **reframing** — describe qualities "close to actual ones" favorably ("a cautious person is cold and designing; a simple person is amiable"; an irascible person is "straightforward") (1.9.28-29)
- Praise is based on actions showing deliberate purpose (*proairesis*); one should make coincidences seem deliberate
- Praise and deliberation share a common species: advice can become praise by changing the form of expression (1.9.35-36)

### Chapters 10-15: Judicial Rhetoric

#### Chapter 10: Wrongdoing
- Wrongdoing defined as "doing harm willingly in contravention of the law" (1.10.3)
- Law is either specific (written law of a polis) or common (universal unwritten principles)
- Seven causes of human action: chance, nature, compulsion, habit, reason, anger, longing (1.10.8-9)

#### Chapter 11: Pleasure
- Pleasure defined as "a certain movement of the soul" (*kinesis tes psyches*) — a collective sensual perception reaching into fundamental nature (1.11.1)
- Extensive catalog of pleasures: movement into natural state, habit, hope, memory, anger (revenge), winning, honor, imagination, learning, change, things related to oneself
- Particularly relevant to judicial rhetoric: understanding what people find pleasurable reveals motives for wrongdoing

#### Chapter 12: Wrongdoers and the Wronged
- Analysis of who wrongs whom and why — the psychology of criminal motivation
- People wrong when they think: (a) the act is possible, (b) they can get away with it, (c) even if caught, the penalty is less than the gain
- Those wronged tend to be: the trusting, the careless, the shy, those who have been wronged before and not prosecuted

#### Chapter 13: Justice and Injustice
- Two kinds of law: specific (written) and common (universal/natural)
- Key concept: **fairness** (*epieikes*) — justice that goes beyond the written law to capture the legislator's intent (1.13.13-19)
- Fairness considers the action over the letter of the law, the whole life over the present moment, the deliberate purpose over the mere deed

#### Chapter 14: Degree of Wrongdoing
- Techniques for arguing that one wrong is greater than another
- Greater wrongs: those from vice (not just ignorance), those that are more premeditated, those that violate unwritten codes of justice, those committed where wrongdoing is punished

#### Chapter 15: Non-artistic Proofs
- Five types of non-artistic (*atechnic*) proof in judicial rhetoric: **laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures, oaths**
- For each, Aristotle provides arguments both for and against — showing how to use them when they support your case and how to discredit them when they support the opponent
- Topics for/against witnesses include credibility, consistency, character, and motive
- On torture: Aristotle notes testimony under torture is unreliable — "many slow-witted and thick-skinned persons hold out nobly" while "the cowardly and cautious will denounce someone before seeing the instruments" (1.15.26)

## Contradictions or Tensions

- Aristotle's definition of [[ethos]] as derived solely from the speech (not prior reputation) contradicts the [[authority-principle]] and the common modern understanding of credibility as partly extrinsic
- The ethical topics in Chapters 5-7 present a surprisingly conventional and popular account of "the good" compared to Aristotle's more rigorous treatment in *Nicomachean Ethics* — Kennedy notes this reflects the practical, audience-adapted nature of rhetoric
