# Predictably Irrational Ch.10: The Effect of Expectations — Summary

**Author(s):** Dan Ariely
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Expectations fundamentally shape our actual experience — they are not merely cognitive overlays but alter sensory perception itself
2. Prior knowledge delivered BEFORE an experience changes the experience differently than the same knowledge delivered AFTER — expectations reshape perception in real time
3. Stereotypes are a form of expectation that influence both how we judge others and how we perform ourselves (stereotype threat)
4. Brand associations activate higher-order brain mechanisms (DLPFC) that genuinely alter the hedonic experience, as demonstrated by Coke vs. Pepsi fMRI studies

## Detailed Notes

### The Football Fan Parable
Two honest friends watching the same Eagles vs. Giants game interpret a contested catch completely differently based on their team loyalties. Their expectations literally alter what they see. This extends to politics, religion, and international conflicts (IRA assassination example — British and Irish saw the same event as proof of opposite conclusions).

### The MIT Brew Experiment (Lee, Frederick, and Ariely)
**Location:** The Muddy Charles pub, MIT

**Setup:** Participants tasted two beers — Budweiser and "MIT Brew" (Budweiser plus two drops of balsamic vinegar per ounce).

**Condition 1 — Blind tasting (no information):** Most participants preferred the MIT Brew. Jeffrey, a PhD student in computer science, chose beer B (the vinegar beer) after careful sampling.

**Condition 2 — Told about vinegar BEFORE tasting:** Mina and others wrinkled their noses and chose the standard beer. Prior negative expectations completely reversed their preference.

**Key finding:** Without foreknowledge about the vinegar, most people chose the vinegar-laced MIT Brew. But when told in advance, they rejected it — not because their experience told them so, but because their expectations told them so.

### The Timing-of-Knowledge Experiment
**Critical question:** Does knowledge merely inform (same effect before or after) or does it reshape sensory perception (different effect depending on timing)?

**Condition — Told about vinegar AFTER tasting:** Students who learned about the vinegar after drinking liked the beer just as much as those who never learned about it at all. This proves expectations reshape the actual sensory experience in real time — once the taste was established, late-arriving knowledge could not retroactively change it.

**Extreme version:** When given ingredients to make their own MIT Brew, telling participants about vinegar AFTER tasting doubled the number who added vinegar to their own beer. The "after" group had experienced it as good, so they were willing to try again.

### The Coffee Condiments Experiment (Ofek, Bertini, and Ariely)
**Location:** MIT Sloan School impromptu coffee shop

**Setup:** Free coffee with unusual condiments (cloves, nutmeg, orange peel, anise, sweet paprika, cardamom) displayed either in beautiful glass-and-metal containers on brushed metal trays or in crude Styrofoam cups with hand-cut jagged edges and handwritten labels.

**Result:** The fancy containers did not persuade anyone to USE the odd condiments. But when condiments were in fancy containers, coffee drinkers rated the coffee higher, were willing to pay more, and recommended it for the cafeteria. The upscale presentation changed the experience of the coffee itself.

### Coke vs. Pepsi fMRI Study (McClure, Li, Tomlin, Cypert, Montague, and Montague)
**Setup:** Blind and non-blind taste tests with fMRI brain scanning.

**Blind condition:** Basic hedonic brain response (VMPFC) was similar for both drinks.

**Non-blind condition (brand revealed):** The frontal area of the brain — specifically the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), involved in working memory, associations, and higher-order cognitions — was also activated. This was stronger for Coke, especially in people with stronger Coke preferences.

**Conclusion:** Coke's advantage was not in its chemical properties but in its brand — the associations activated higher-order brain mechanisms that genuinely enhanced the hedonic experience. The bright red can and decades of messaging were as much responsible for the love of Coke as the drink itself.

### Stereotype Priming Studies
**Asian-American women math experiment (Shih, Pittinsky, and Ambady):** Asian-American women performed worse on math when primed with their gender identity and better when primed with their Asian identity — matching stereotypes for both groups.

**Elderly priming study (Bargh, Chen, and Burrows):** Participants who unscrambled sentences with words like *Florida*, *bingo*, and *ancient* walked more slowly down the hallway afterward — even though they were young undergraduates at NYU. The polite/rude version: those primed with polite words waited 9.3 minutes to interrupt; rude-primed participants waited only 5.5 minutes.

### Joshua Bell Metro Station Experiment
**Setup:** Washington Post journalist Gene Weingarten had Joshua Bell, one of the world's best violinists, play at a DC Metro station during rush hour.

**Result:** 98% of 1,097 people hurried by. Only 27 put money in his case. Only 7 stopped for more than a minute. He made about $32. No one expected a world-class musician in a Metro station, so accordingly, they didn't hear one.

**Bell's own insight:** "The expectations would triumph over the experience." He could think of people who were not great violinists but received wild applause because they were in the right environment.

### Reflections on Expectations: Music and Food
- A truck stop serving foie gras would be enjoyed less than the identical dish in Manhattan — ambience and expectations change the experience
- Descriptive food language ("succulent organic breast of chicken roasted to perfection") increases enjoyment of the same dish
- Adding exotic-sounding ingredients to cooking changes expectations and therefore taste
- Presentation matters: artful plating, nice dishes, proper wineglasses all enhance experience (even though controlled studies show glass shape makes no difference in blind tests)
