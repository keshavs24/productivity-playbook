# Age of Propaganda Ch.5: The Four Stratagems of Influence — Summary

**Authors:** Anthony Pratkanis & Elliot Aronson
**Source:** *Age of Propaganda: The Everyday Use and Abuse of Persuasion* (Revised Edition), Chapter 5
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Maximum influence occurs when four stratagems are accomplished:** (1) [[pre-persuasion-tactics]] — take control of the situation and establish a favorable climate; (2) establish [[source-credibility]] — appear likable, authoritative, or trustworthy; (3) construct and deliver a focused **message** that channels the target's thoughts; (4) control the target's **emotions** — arouse an emotion and offer a way to respond to it that serves the persuader's goal.
2. **These four stratagems map to classical rhetoric.** Aristotle identified three facets of persuasion: [[ethos]] (source), [[logos]] (message), and [[pathos]] (emotion). Cicero added the concept of *statis* (status of the issue) — what Pratkanis and Aronson call pre-persuasion.
3. **Lincoln's Gettysburg Address exemplifies all four stratagems working together:** Pre-persuasion (redefining the birth of the nation as 1776, not 1789); source credibility (redirecting the source to the nation's founders and fallen soldiers); message (packaging around birth-dedication-consecration); emotions (pride, fear, obligation, hypocrisy).
4. **The distinction between propaganda and persuasion** rests on two questions: Does the communication induce thought or truncate it? Does the communicator use emotions to motivate exploration or to exploit insecurities?

## Detailed Notes

### The Four Stratagems Defined

**1. Pre-persuasion:** Refers to how the issue is structured and how the decision is framed. If fully successful, pre-persuasion establishes "what everyone knows" and "what everyone takes for granted." By cleverly defining how an issue is discussed, a communicator can influence cognitive responses and obtain consent without appearing to be persuading at all. Cicero's concept of *statis* (status of the issue) is the classical precursor.

**2. Source credibility:** The communicator must appear likable, authoritative, trustworthy, or otherwise credible. Aristotle recommended presenting oneself as a good person worthy of trust. This corresponds to [[ethos]].

**3. Message:** Construct and deliver a message that focuses the target's attention and thoughts on exactly what the communicator wants them to think about — by distracting from counterarguments, focusing on vivid images, or inducing targets to persuade themselves. This corresponds to [[logos]].

**4. Emotions:** Arouse an emotion in the target and offer a way to respond to that emotion that serves the desired course of action. The target becomes preoccupied with dealing with the emotion, complying in hopes of escaping a negative emotion or maintaining a positive one. This corresponds to [[pathos]].

### Case Study: Lincoln's Gettysburg Address

**Pre-persuasion:** "Four score and seven years ago" redefined the birth of the nation as 1776 (Declaration of Independence) rather than 1789 (Constitution). This linked the nation's identity to "all men are created equal," justifying both the war and the Emancipation Proclamation without mentioning either directly.

**Source credibility:** Lincoln redirected the source away from himself (rejected by southerners, distrusted by many northerners) to the nation's founders and fallen soldiers, building their credibility and questioning his own worthiness.

**Message:** Packaged around a single theme — conception, birth, dedication, and consecration — that conveyed spiritual significance to a Bible-literate audience.

**Emotions:** Pride in being American ([[granfalloon]] encompassing all sides); fear that the nation could perish; obligation to the dead; and most powerfully, **hypocrisy** — how can a nation "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" exclude some Americans? This use of [[cognitive-dissonance]] gave the audience no choice but to confront their prejudice. Within seven years, the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments were ratified.

### Case Study: Rush Limbaugh

Limbaugh demonstrates the four stratagems in a simpler form:

**Pre-persuasion:** Labeling opponents with dismissive terms ("environmental wackos," "feminazis," "liberal Democrats"); using rumor and innuendo to set context; distorting opponents' positions to make them easy to refute.

**Source credibility:** Presenting himself as a "regular guy just like his audience members" despite earning $20 million per year. Maintaining complete control of information flow (screening callers, cutting audio).

**Message:** Vivid images ("Watermelons are environmentalist. They're green on the outside but red on the inside"); disparaging humor to prevent deep thinking; constant repetition.

**Emotions:** Fear of losing religion, rights, and traditional values; pride in being a "dittohead" (the [[granfalloon]]) contrasted with out-groups; ridicule of opponents to create [[social-proof]]-like conformity.

### Propaganda vs. Persuasion: Two Diagnostic Questions

1. **Does the communication induce thought about the issues at hand, or does it truncate thought and play on prejudices?** Is the message processed via the [[elaboration-likelihood-model|central or peripheral route]]?
2. **How does the communicator use emotions?** Lincoln used pride to explore what it means to be American. A good therapist uses dissatisfaction to motivate growth. Contrast this with cheap jokes at a teenager's expense or rumor-mongering — these truncate thought and exploit insecurity.

The authors note an important irony: the primary purpose of propaganda is to get you to like the communicator and agree with the message. So if you find yourself readily liking and agreeing, this could be a sign of effective propaganda rather than truth.
