# Influence Ch.3: Commitment and Consistency — Summary

**Author:** Robert B. Cialdini
**Source:** *Influence: Science and Practice*, 5th Edition (2009), Chapter 3
**Phase:** 1 — Cognitive Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Once we make a choice or take a stand, we encounter personal and interpersonal pressures to behave consistently with that commitment.** This drive for consistency is one of the most powerful weapons of social influence, often causing us to act against our own best interests.
2. **Commitment is the key** — if a compliance professional can get you to make a commitment (take a stand, go on record), they have set the stage for automatic consistency with that earlier commitment.
3. **Not all commitments are equally binding** — the most effective commitments are those that are *active* (behavioral, not passive), *public* (visible to others), *effortful* (requiring exertion or sacrifice), and *internally motivated* (perceived as uncoerced, freely chosen).
4. **Commitments "grow their own legs"** — once a commitment leads to a self-image change, people generate new reasons to justify it, so the commitment persists even after the original reason is removed. This is the mechanism behind the [[lowball-technique]].
5. **Defense requires attending to two signals**: "stomach signs" (the visceral feeling of being trapped into foolish consistency) and "heart-of-hearts signs" (asking "Knowing what I know now, would I make the same choice again?" and trusting the first flash of feeling).

## Detailed Notes

### Opening: The Racetrack Study
- **Knox & Inkster (1968)**: Canadian psychologists found that racetrack bettors become *much more confident* of their horse's chances of winning immediately *after* placing their bets. Nothing about the horse changes — the commitment shifts the perception.
- The same effect occurs with voters: immediately after casting a ballot, voters believe more strongly that their candidate will win (Regan & Kilduff, 1988).
- Additional confirmation from Fazio, Blascovich, & Driscoll (1992); Brinol, Petty, & Wheeler (2006); Mather, Shafir, & Johnson (2000); Rusbult et al. (2000).

### The Story of Sara and Tim
- Cialdini's neighbor Sara had a live-in boyfriend Tim who wouldn't marry her and drank heavily. Sara eventually broke it off and got engaged to another man. Tim begged her back, offering to marry her and quit drinking. Sara broke the engagement and returned to Tim. Within a month, Tim reneged on both promises. Yet Sara became *more devoted* than ever — she says "being forced to choose taught her that Tim really is number one in her heart."
- This story recurs throughout the chapter as an illustration of consistency trapping someone in a bad decision, and is later analyzed as a [[lowball-technique]] scenario.

### Whirring Along: Why Consistency Is Powerful
- **Prominent theorists**: Leon Festinger (1957), Fritz Heider (1946), Theodore Newcomb (1953) all viewed the desire for consistency as a central motivator of behavior.
- **NYC Beach Theft Experiment (Moriarty, 1975)**: Researchers staged thefts of a radio from a blanket on a New York City beach. Under normal conditions, only **4 out of 20** bystanders challenged the thief. But when the accomplice first asked "please watch my things," **19 out of 20** intervened — running after, restraining, and confronting the thief. A trivial verbal commitment transformed passive observers into "virtual vigilantes."
- **Consistency is culturally valued**: Inconsistency is considered an undesirable trait — associated with confusion, being two-faced, mental illness (Allgeier, Byrne, Brooks, & Revnes, 1979; Asch, 1946). High consistency is associated with personal and intellectual strength, logic, rationality, stability, and honesty.
- **Michael Faraday anecdote**: The great British chemist was asked after a lecture if a rival was always wrong. He glowered: "He's not that consistent." Consistency is valued so much that even *being consistently wrong* earns grudging respect.
- Personal consistency makes life manageable (Sheldon, Ryan, Rawsthorne, & Ilardi, 1997).

### The Quick Fix: Consistency as Cognitive Shortcut
- Consistency functions as a mental shortcut, like [[click-whirr]] responding. Once we make up our minds, we don't need to think about the issue anymore — we just *click* on our consistency tape and *whirr*, we know what to believe, say, or do.
- **Sir Joshua Reynolds** quote: "There is no expedient to which a man will not resort to avoid the real labor of thinking."
- This makes consistency a convenient, effortless, and efficient method for dealing with complexity.

### The Foolish Fortress: Consistency as Shield Against Thought
- A second, more perverse attraction: consistency can shield us from *unwelcome conclusions*. Rather than face disturbing truths, people retreat behind walls of consistency — "a safe hiding place from troubling realizations."
- **Transcendental Meditation lecture story**: Cialdini attended a TM recruitment lecture with a logician friend. The logician utterly demolished the TM argument during Q&A. The discussion leaders admitted his points needed "further study." Yet the *audience rushed to pay $75 down payments*. Why? Three audience members explained: they had real problems (acting career, insomnia, failing college courses). They *wanted* TM to be their answer. When the logician proved TM was unsound, they *panicked* — if they thought about it, they'd lose their only hope. So they bought in *faster* to avoid having to think. The spokesman: "I knew I'd better give them my money now, or I'd go home and start thinking about what he said and *never* sign up."
- This illustrates how people flee into commitment to escape the burden of rational evaluation.

### Seek and Hide: Toy Company Strategy
- **Toy manufacturers' scheme**: Cialdini describes how certain large toy companies exploit the consistency principle to boost post-Christmas sales:
  1. Before Christmas, they heavily advertise a special toy.
  2. Kids extract promises from parents to buy it.
  3. The companies deliberately *undersupply* the toy to stores.
  4. Parents can't find it, so they substitute other toys of equal value (maintaining Christmas spending).
  5. After Christmas, the company re-runs the ads for the original toy.
  6. Kids say "You promised, you promised!"
  7. Parents trudge back to the store to fulfill their commitment — buying *a second expensive toy*.
- **Cialdini's personal experience**: He fell for this scheme *two years in a row* — once with a robot toy, once with a road-race set. A friend in the toy business explained the trick. Even after learning the scheme, Cialdini was trapped: "I'm still trapped — by my own words."
- **Historical examples**: Cabbage Patch Kids in the mid-1980s ($25 dolls, promoted heavily but woefully undersupplied — adults battled in stores, paid up to $700 at auction, government filed false advertising charges); Beanie Babies; Tickle Me Elmo dolls; Furbies (1998 — Hasbro spokeswoman advised parents: "I'll try, but if I can't get it for you now, I'll get it for you later" — exactly the promise that fuels the scheme).

### Commitment Is the Key
- The mechanism: **If I can get you to make a commitment (take a stand, go on record), I have set the stage for automatic consistency with that earlier commitment.** Even preliminary leanings can bias subsequent choices (Brownstein, 2003; Brownstein, Read, & Simon, 2004; Russo, Carlson, & Meloy, 2006).
- **Jack Stanko**, used-car sales manager, at a National Auto Dealers Association convention: "Put 'em on paper. Get the customer's OK on paper. Get the money up front. Control 'em. Control the deal. Ask 'em if they would buy the car right now if the price is right. Pin 'em down" (Rubinstein, 1985).
- **Steven J. Sherman's prediction study (1980)**: Called Bloomington, Indiana, residents and asked them to *predict* what they would say if asked to spend three hours collecting for the American Cancer Society. Most said they would volunteer (to avoid seeming uncharitable). When the ACS actually called a few days later, there was a **700% increase in volunteers** compared to the baseline.
- Same strategy applied to voting: asking citizens to predict whether they'd vote increased turnout significantly (Greenwald, Carnot, Beach, & Young, 1987; Spangenberg & Greenwald, in press).
- **Jo-Ellen Demitrius** — top jury consultant: screens jurors by asking "If you were the only person who believed in my client's innocence, could you withstand the pressure of the rest of the jury to change your mind?" — extracting a public commitment to independence.
- **"How are you feeling this evening?" technique**: Telephone solicitors open with this question. People respond with "Fine" or "Good" — a public commitment to well-being. Then the solicitor says: "I'm glad to hear that, because I'm calling to ask if you'd be willing to make a donation to help the unfortunate victims of..." It becomes psychologically awkward for someone who just said they're doing fine to appear stingy.
- **Daniel Howard (1990)**: Dallas, Texas, study — standard solicitation alone got 18% agreement for the Hunger Relief Committee. Adding "How are you feeling this evening?" first got **32% agreement** (nearly double). Of the 120 called, most (108) gave a favorable reply. And 89% of those who agreed to a visit actually made a cookie purchase.

### Korean War POW Camps: The Chinese "Lenient Policy"
- The Chinese approach to American POWs was a masterclass in commitment escalation. Unlike the North Koreans who used brutal punishment, the Chinese used a sophisticated psychological program they called "lenient policy."
- **Edgar Schein (1956)**, principal American investigator: "When an escape did occur, the Chinese usually recovered the man easily by offering a bag of rice to anyone turning him in." Nearly all American prisoners collaborated in one way or another (broadly defined to include signing peace petitions, running errands, making radio appeals, informing on fellow prisoners, etc.).
- **Start small and build** — the Chinese answer to extracting collaboration from men trained to give only name, rank, and serial number:
  1. First, request mildly anti-American or pro-Communist statements ("The United States is not perfect." "In a Communist country, unemployment is not a problem.")
  2. Once these trivial requests were complied with, push to more substantive requests
  3. A prisoner who agreed the US wasn't perfect would be asked to *list* specific problems
  4. Then asked to *sign* his name to the list
  5. Then asked to *read* the list in a discussion group ("After all, it's what you really believe, isn't it?")
  6. Then asked to *write an essay* expanding on these problems
  7. His name and essay used in anti-American radio broadcasts beamed to the entire camp and to American forces in South Korea
  8. The man now sees himself as a "collaborator" — and adjusts his self-image accordingly
  9. More extensive collaboration follows naturally from the new self-view
- Schein: "only a few men were able to avoid collaboration altogether... the majority collaborated at one time or another by doing things which seemed to them trivial but which the Chinese were able to turn to their own advantage. This was particularly effective in eliciting confessions, self-criticism, and information during interrogation" (1956).

### Reader's Report 3.1: The Texas Insurance Trainer
- A telemarketing trainer discovered that asking customers "Why have you chosen to purchase your insurance with <our company>?" at the end of the call — committing them to their own *reasons* — generated **19% more sales** from new salespeople and **10% more** when applied across all presentations.
- **Cialdini's author note**: This was masterful because it didn't just commit customers to their choice — it committed them to the *reasons* for their choice.

### The Foot-in-the-Door Technique
- **Freedman & Fraser (1966)** — the foundational study:
  - Researchers posing as volunteer workers went door-to-door in a residential California neighborhood asking homeowners to install a huge, ugly public-service billboard ("DRIVE CAREFULLY") on their front lawns. They showed a photo of a house almost completely obscured by the sign.
  - **Baseline**: Only **17%** agreed.
  - **Foot-in-the-door condition**: A different volunteer had visited two weeks earlier asking them to display a tiny 3-inch-square sign reading "BE A SAFE DRIVER." Nearly everyone agreed to this trivial request. Result: **76%** later agreed to the massive billboard.
  - **Most astounding finding**: Freedman and Fraser tried yet another condition. People were first asked to sign a petition favoring "keeping California beautiful" — an entirely different topic. Nearly everyone signed. Two weeks later, a different volunteer asked them to install the DRIVE CAREFULLY billboard. **Approximately half agreed** — even though their initial commitment was to state beautification, not driver safety.
  - **Explanation**: Signing the beautification petition changed how these people saw *themselves*. They became, in their own eyes, "public-spirited citizens who act on their civic principles." When asked to display the DRIVE CAREFULLY sign, they complied to be consistent with their new self-image.
  - Freedman & Fraser: "What may occur is a change in the person's feelings about getting involved or taking action. Once he has agreed to a request, his attitude may change, he may become, in his own eyes, the kind of person who does this sort of thing" (p. 201).
- **Implications**: Even trivial commitments can alter self-concept and increase compliance with much larger, even unrelated requests (Burger & Caldwell, 2003).
- **Petition signing**: Groups that collect petition signatures may not even use the signatures — the principal purpose is to commit the signers to the group's position.

### Hearts and Minds: Self-Image Change
- The foot-in-the-door experts' excitement centers on self-image manipulation: small commitments can turn "citizens" into "public servants," "prospects" into "customers," "prisoners" into "collaborators."
- **Four conditions for commitments that affect self-image**: They should be **active**, **public**, **effortful**, and **freely chosen** (internally motivated).
- **Dr. Henry Segal (1954)**, chief neuropsychiatric evaluator of returning Korean War POWs: war-related beliefs had been substantially shifted. "Many expressed antipathy toward the Chinese Communists but at the same time praised them for 'the fine job they had done in China.' Others stated that 'although communism won't work in America, I think it's a good thing for Asia.'" (p. 360)
- The real Chinese goal was not intelligence extraction but *indoctrination* — changing attitudes, perceptions of self, and beliefs about their country, the war, and communism.

### The Magic Act: Active Commitments
- **Bem (1972); Vallacher & Wegner (1985)**: People use their own behavior as evidence of their beliefs and attitudes. We look at what we *do* to decide what we *think*.
- **Active vs. Passive commitments**: Allison & Messick (1988); Fazio, Sherman, & Herr (1982). College students volunteered for an AIDS education project — half actively (filling out a form saying they *wanted* to participate), half passively (by *not* filling out a form saying they *didn't*). When asked to show up 3-4 days later, **74%** of active volunteers appeared vs. far fewer passive ones. Active volunteers also explained their decisions more in terms of personal values, preferences, and traits (Cioffi & Garner, 1996).
- **Written declarations**: Writing was the commitment action the Chinese pushed hardest. Schein (1956): "A further technique was to have the man write out the question and then the [pro-Communist] answer. If he refused to write it voluntarily, he was asked to copy it from the notebooks, which must have seemed like a harmless enough concession" (p. 161).
- **Why writing is powerful**: (1) It provides physical evidence that the act occurred — harder to deny or forget than verbal statements. (2) A written statement can be shown to others, persuading them the author genuinely believes what was written.
- **Jones & Harris (1967)**: Showed people a pro-Castro essay and asked them to guess the author's true feelings. Even when told the author had been *assigned* to write the pro-Castro essay (no free choice), readers still guessed the writer actually liked Castro. Written statements produce a [[click-whirr]] response — observers assume the writer means it (Gawronski, 2003; Allison, Mackie, Muller, & Worth, 1993).
- **Double-barreled effect on prisoners**: A pro-Chinese written statement (1) reminded the writer of his action, and (2) persuaded others it reflected his real beliefs.
- **Kraut (1973)**: Homemakers in New Haven, Connecticut, who heard that they were considered "charitable people" gave much more money a week later to a canvasser from the Multiple Sclerosis Association. Labels change self-image, which drives consistent action.
- **Anwar Sadat**: Before negotiations, he would assure opponents they and their citizens were widely known for cooperativeness and fairness — giving them a reputation to uphold. Henry Kissinger (1982): Sadat was successful because "he got others to act in his interests by giving them a reputation to uphold."
- **Chinese letter-writing tactic**: Prisoners wanting to reach their families knew mail was censored. To get letters through, some began including peace appeals, claims of kind treatment, and pro-Communist statements — reasoning the Chinese would release such letters. The Chinese were delighted — it provided propaganda *and* got men on record supporting communism.
- **Political essay contests**: The Chinese held essay contests with small prizes (a few cigarettes, a bit of fruit). Crucially, they used *small* prizes deliberately — large rewards would let writers rationalize ("I only wrote that for the prize"). Small prizes meant the writer had to *own* what they wrote. Occasionally, the winning essay generally supported the United States but included one or two pro-Chinese concessions — exactly what the Chinese wanted: small voluntary commitments to nurture into larger ones.
- **After Tiananmen Square (1989)**: Nine state-run newspapers and TV stations in Beijing sponsored essay competitions on "the quelling of the counterrevolutionary rebellion." Consistent with long-standing practice, prize sizes were left unspecified.

### Written Commitments in Business
- **Amway Corporation**: Sales staff set personal goals and *write them down*: "Set a goal and write it down. Whatever the goal, the important thing is that you set it, so you've got something for which to aim — and that you write it down. There is something magical about writing things down."
- **Door-to-door sales**: Companies use written commitments to combat "cooling-off" laws that allow cancellation within a few days. Solution: have the *customer* (not the salesperson) fill out the sales agreement. "That personal commitment alone has proved to be 'a very important psychological aid in preventing customers from backing out of their contracts.'"
- **"25-words-or-less" testimonial contests**: Procter & Gamble, General Foods, etc. run these not for the entries but to get contestants to write praise for products. No purchase required — the goal is written endorsement, which creates consistency pressure.

### Reader's Report 3.2: Fred DeLucca (Subway)
- DeLucca put "10,000 stores by 2001" on napkins in every Subway restaurant. Explanation: "If I put my goals down in writing and make them known to the world, I'm committed to achieving them." He exceeded the goal — by January 2008, Subway had over 28,000 restaurants in 86 countries.

### The Public Eye: Public Commitments
- **Principle**: Public commitments tend to be lasting commitments, because of the drive to *look* consistent to others (Tedeschi, Schlenker, & Bonoma, 1971; Schlenker et al., 1994).
- The Chinese in Korea constantly arranged to have prisoners' pro-Communist statements seen by others — posted around camp, read in discussion groups, broadcast on camp radio.
- **Deutsch & Gerard (1955)** — classic line-length estimation experiment:
  - Three groups of college students estimated line lengths, then received information their estimates were wrong.
  - Group 1 (public commitment): wrote estimates, signed names, turned in to experimenter. **Most resistant to change.**
  - Group 2 (private commitment): wrote estimates on a Magic Writing Pad, then erased. **Less resistant than public, but still more than no-commitment group.**
  - Group 3 (no commitment): kept estimates in their heads. **Most willing to change.**
  - Public commitments hardened them into "the most stubborn of all."
- **Jury deliberations**: In close cases, hung juries were significantly more frequent when jurors expressed opinions via a *visible show of hands* rather than a *secret ballot*. Public commitment locks in positions (Kerr & MacCoun, 1985).
- **Weight-loss clinics**: Require clients to write down goals and *show* them to friends, relatives, and neighbors. The public commitment makes the decision stronger than a private resolution.
- **San Diego woman's smoking cessation**: Wrote "I promise you that I will never smoke another cigarette" on the back of blank business cards and distributed them to everyone she cared about. Whenever she craved a cigarette, she'd picture how all those people would think less of her. "And that's all it took. I've never taken another puff."
- **Charles DeGaulle**: Announced publicly he would quit smoking, then explained: "DeGaulle cannot go back on his word" (D. Cook, 1984). Works especially well for individuals with high public self-consciousness (Feingstein, Scheier, & Buss, 1975).

### Reader's Report 3.3: Gordon Sinclair's Restaurant
- Restaurant owner Gordon Sinclair of Gordon's restaurant in Chicago solved his no-show problem by changing his receptionist's script from "Please call us if you change your plans" to "**Will you please call us if you change your plans?**" — and waiting for a response. No-show rate dropped immediately from **30% to 10%**. The key was extracting a verbal *commitment* (not just delivering instructions).

### The Effort Extra: Effortful Commitments
- **Principle**: The more effort that goes into a commitment, the greater its ability to influence attitudes and behavior.
- **Concert ticket pricing**: Promoters increasingly hide prices from ads. When fans call or visit an outlet and *invest effort* to learn the price, they're at the end of "an active, public, effortful commitment to the event" — and more likely to buy.
- **Thonga initiation rites (Southern Africa)**: Anthropologists Whiting, Kluckhohn, & Anthony (1958) described a 3-month ordeal for boys ages 10-16: circumcision school with severe hazing, beatings, exposure to cold, thirst, eating unsavory food, punishment, and threats of death.
- **Fraternity Hell Week**: Closely parallels tribal initiation rites. Specific examples:
  - *Beatings*: Michael Kalogris, 14, spent 3 weeks in hospital after his fraternity's "atomic bomb" initiation.
  - *Exposure to cold*: Frederick Bronner died of exposure after being taken 3,000 feet up and 10 miles into a national forest in winter.
  - *Thirst*: Two Ohio State freshmen locked in a storage closet for nearly 2 days with only salty food and plastic cups for drinking.
  - *Eating unsavory foods*: Richard Swanson at USC choked to death trying to swallow a quarter-pound slab of raw liver soaked in oil.
  - *Punishment*: A pledge had bones broken in both feet during a punishment ritual.
  - *Threats of death*: A Zeta Beta Tau pledge was told to dig his own grave; the hole collapsed on him.
- **Why hazing persists**: Initiation ceremonies are phenomenally resistant to elimination. Authorities have tried threats, legal actions, banishments, bribes, and bans — none have been successful (Gordon & Gordon, 1963).
- **Aronson & Mills (1959)**: College women who endured a severely embarrassing initiation to join a sex discussion group rated the group and its (intentionally "worthless and uninteresting") discussions as extremely valuable. Replicated by Gerard & Mathewson (1966) using electric shock.
- **Young (1965)**: Study of 54 tribal cultures — those with the most dramatic and stringent initiation ceremonies had the greatest group solidarity.
- **Military boot camp**: William Styron (1977): "There is no ex-Marine of my acquaintance... who does not view the training as a crucible out of which he emerged in some way more resilient, simply braver and better for the wear" (p. 3).
- **Marine "blood pinning" (1997)**: Despite "zero tolerance" claims, only 1 of 30 marines was recommended for discharge.
- **Why groups refuse to substitute community service for hazing**: Community service would let pledges believe they suffered for charitable reasons. Hazing forces them to own the suffering as proof of their commitment to the group.

### The Inner Choice: Internal Motivation
- **The most important property of effective commitment**: A commitment must be perceived as **internally motivated** (freely chosen, without strong external pressure).
- **Social science principle**: "We accept inner responsibility for a behavior when we think we have chosen to perform it in the absence of strong outside pressure" (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Higgins, Lee, Kwon, & Trope, 1995; Lepper & Greene, 1978).
- Large rewards or strong threats produce compliance but not commitment. The behavior won't persist when the external pressure is removed.
- **Why Chinese and fraternities used small prizes**: No external justification should be available. The participants must *own* what they did.

### Jonathan Freedman's Forbidden Toy Experiment (1965)
- **Setup**: Boys aged 7-9 shown five toys including an extremely attractive battery-controlled robot. Told "It is wrong to play with the robot."
- **Strong threat condition**: "If you play with the robot, I'll be very angry and will have to do something about it." **21 of 22 boys** didn't touch the robot.
- **Mild threat condition**: Simply "It is wrong to play with the robot." Again, **21 of 22 boys** didn't touch it.
- **Six weeks later**: A young woman (no connection to Freedman) took each boy to the same room.
  - **Strong threat group**: **77%** chose to play with the previously forbidden robot.
  - **Mild threat group**: Only **33%** played with the robot. These boys had internalized the choice.
- **Implication for child-rearing**: Use reasons "just sufficient" to produce behavior but not so strong they provide an external explanation. The child takes personal responsibility and continues the behavior long-term.
- Samuel Butler: "He who agrees against his will / Is of the same opinion still."

### Growing Legs to Stand On: The Low-Ball Technique
- **Principle**: Commitments that produce inner change "grow their own legs" — people generate *new* reasons to justify the commitment. Even if the original reason disappears, the new self-generated justifications sustain the decision.
- **Car dealer low-ball tactic**: A very good price ($400+ below competitors) is offered. Commitment activities follow (purchase forms, financing, test drives). Then the original inducement is removed via an "error" in calculations or reduced trade-in. The customer stays because of all the new legs supporting the decision.

### Cialdini's Low-Ball Experiment (Cialdini, Cacioppo, Bassett, & Miller, 1978)
- **Control group**: Told about 7:00 AM start time immediately. Only **24%** agreed.
- **Low-ball group**: First asked if they'd participate (56% said yes). *Then* told about 7:00 AM. **None backed out.** And **95%** actually showed up.
- Replicated: Brownstein & Katzev (1985); Burger & Petty (1981); Joule (1987).

### Sara Revisited as Low-Ball Victim
- Tim offered inducements (marry, quit drinking) to get Sara to commit. After she did, he removed them. Her commitment grew its own legs — she found new positive qualities in Tim.

### Standing Up for the Public Good: Iowa Energy Conservation (Pallak, Cook, & Sullivan, 1980)
- **Phase 1**: Iowa homeowners given conservation tips, asked to save fuel. Result: **no real savings.**
- **Phase 2**: Same treatment plus newspaper publicity as "public-spirited citizens." Saved **422 cubic feet of gas per month.**
- **The rug pull**: Letter saying publicity wasn't possible after all. They **increased savings from 12.2% to 15.5%** for the rest of winter.
- **Explanation**: The commitment had "grown its own legs." When the external prop was removed, homeowners could fully own their conservation identity.
- Confirmed for pro-social purposes: Bachman & Katzev (1982); Katzev & Johnson (1983, 1984); Katzev & Pardini (1988); Pardini & Katzev (1983-84).

### Defense Against Commitment and Consistency Traps

#### Signal 1: Stomach Signs
- A visceral tightening when we realize we're being trapped into compliance.
- **Cialdini's entertainment-club story**: An attractive woman ran a "survey" of entertainment habits, then used his (exaggerated) answers to sell a Clubamerica membership. He felt his stomach tighten but bought anyway.
- **Current approach**: Tell the manipulator exactly what's happening. "No click, whirr for me."

#### Signal 2: Heart-of-Hearts Signs
- **The question**: "Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice?"
- Focus on the *first flash of feeling* before rationalizations flood in (Murphy & Zajonc, 1993; van den Berg et al., 2006).
- **Gas station example**: Cialdini asked himself the key question, got a clear "no," and left.

### Special Vulnerabilities
- **Age**: Preference for consistency increases with age. Beyond age 50, strongest inclination to honor commitments (Brown, Asher, & Cialdini, 2005). Explains why elder fraud relies heavily on consistency tactics. **AARP sting study (Pratkanis & Shadel, 2005)** confirmed this.
- **Individualism**: Individualistic cultures (US, Western Europe) are more susceptible. **Petrova, Cialdini, & Sills (2007)**: American students were **2x as likely** to comply with foot-in-the-door as Asian students (21.6% vs. 9.9%).
- **Jack Nicklaus (2005)**: After his grandson's death, still honored prior commitments because "You make commitments, and you've got to do them." His two vulnerability traits: age 65, American.

- [[active-commitment]] — Behavioral (active) commitments change self-image more than passive ones
