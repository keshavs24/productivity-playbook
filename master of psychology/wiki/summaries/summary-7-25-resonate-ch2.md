# Resonate Ch.2: Lessons from Myths and Movies — Summary

**Author:** Nancy Duarte
**Source:** *Resonate* (2010), Chapter 2
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Presentations sit between reports and stories on a spectrum.** Reports organize facts by topic and are exhaustive; stories organize scenes dramatically and are experiential. Presentations fall in the middle — they are *explanations* that alternate between fact and storytelling. The moment you need to create a presentation and not a report, shift your mindset from transferring information to creating an experience.
2. **Story templates from mythology, literature, and cinema provide the structural backbone.** Duarte draws on Syd Field's Paradigm (three-act structure with plot points), Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey (via Christopher Vogler), and Gustav Freytag's Pyramid. All stories share a universal pattern: a relatable hero encounters roadblocks and emerges transformed.
3. **The Presentation Form (the "contour of communication") is Duarte's adaptation of story structure for presentations.** It has a clear beginning, middle, and end with two turning points. The beginning establishes *what is* (the audience's ordinary world). The first turning point is the *call to adventure* — the gap between what is and [[what-is-what-could-be]]. The middle alternates back and forth between what is and what could be, creating contrast. The second turning point is the *call to action*. The end paints a picture of the *new bliss* — the world after adoption.
4. **The sparkline is the visual analysis tool** (term borrowed from Edward Tufte) that maps the contour of a presentation, showing how content moves between what is and what could be, and how emotion and delivery contrast over time. Each sparkline is unique because each presentation is unique.

## Detailed Notes

### Incorporate Story
All types of writing fall between two extreme poles: reports and stories. Reports organize facts by topic (topical, hierarchical). Stories organize scenes dramatically (exposition, rising action, climax, denouement). Presentations fall in the middle — they are explanatory, persuasive, motivating. Blending report material with story material makes information more digestible. "It's the sugar that helps the medicine go down."

### Drama Is Everything
Great stories introduce a hero to whom you can relate, with an acute desire or goal that is threatened. As Robert McKee explains, "Something must be at stake that convinces the audience that a great deal will be lost if the hero doesn't obtain his goal." If nothing is at risk, it is not interesting. Your communications follow a similar pattern — there will be trials and resistance, but remarkable results when your desire is realized.

### Story Templates Create Structure
Syd Field is considered the father of Hollywood's story template. His book *Screenplay* uses the three-act structure first proposed by [[aristotle]]:
- **Act 1** (Set-Up): introduces characters, relationships, and the hero's unfulfilled desire
- **Act 2** (Confrontation): obstacles prevent the hero from achieving the desire
- **Act 3** (Resolution): did the hero succeed or fail?

A great presentation is similar to a screenplay: clear beginning, middle, and end; identifiable structure; first plot point captures intrigue (turning point); beginning and end are much shorter than the middle.

### The Hero's Journey Structure
Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey (simplified by Christopher Vogler) provides a 12-stage circular model: (1) Ordinary World, (2) Call to Adventure, (3) Refusal of the Call, (4) Meeting with the Mentor, (5) Crossing the Threshold, (6) Tests/Allies/Enemies, (7) Approach the Inmost Cave, (8) Ordeal, (9) Reward, (10) Road Back, (11) Resurrection, (12) Return with the Elixir. The key insight: the journey divides the *ordinary world* from the *special world* with a threshold-crossing moment.

Presentations use these insights: the audience is a likable yet flawed hero; the presentation takes them on a journey into the presenter's special world; they make a conscious decision to cross the threshold (they are not forced); they will resist and point out roadblocks; they need to change on the inside before they change on the outside.

### Crossing the Threshold
There must be some kind of conflict or imbalance perceived by the audience that the presentation resolves. This imbalance is created by juxtaposing *what is* with *what could be*. Drawing attention to that gap forces the audience to contend with it until a new balance is achieved. Connects to [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]].

### The Contour of Communication — The Presentation Form
Drawing from mythological, literary, and cinematic structures, the *presentation form* emerged. Most great presentations unknowingly follow this form:
- **Beginning:** Paint a picture of the audience's current world (*what is*). Should be concise — no more than 10% of total time.
- **Turning Point 1 (Call to Adventure):** Create a gap between what is and what could be, jolting the audience from complacency. Should be explicit, not vague.
- **Middle:** Present contrasting content alternating between what is and what could be. Three types of contrast: content, emotion, and delivery.
- **Turning Point 2 (Call to Action):** Clearly define what you are asking the audience to do. Four types of audience action: doers (assemble, decide, try), suppliers (acquire, fund, provide), influencers (activate, adopt, promote), innovators (create, discover, pioneer).
- **End:** Describe the new bliss — an inspirational, blissful world that has adopted your idea. The principle of recency states that audiences remember the last content most vividly.

### What Is a Sparkline?
The [[sparkline]] is the graphical analysis tool used throughout the book to visualize the presentation form. The line moves up and down between what is and what could be, and also changes colors to signify contrasts in emotion and delivery. No two sparklines are alike. The term was borrowed from Edward Tufte.

### Case Study: Benjamin Zander
Zander's 2008 TED talk on classical music demonstrates the presentation form perfectly. He uses structural contrast (shifting between what is and what could be), delivery contrast (alternating speaking and piano playing, touching audience faces), and emotional contrast (stories that evoke tears alternated with humor). His S.T.A.R. moment: having the audience hear longing in Chopin's music by connecting it to a loved one who is no longer there.
