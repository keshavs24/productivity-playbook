# Ch.11 Collective Efficacy — Summary

**Author(s):** Albert Bandura
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Collective efficacy is defined as a group's shared belief in its conjoint capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to produce given levels of attainments** (p.477). It is an emergent group-level property -- more than the sum of individual members' attributes.
2. **Two measurement approaches** for collective efficacy: (a) aggregating members' appraisals of their personal capabilities for the functions they perform in the group, and (b) aggregating members' appraisals of their group's capability as a whole. The holistic judgment is the better predictor for activities requiring high system interdependence (p.480).
3. **Perceived collective efficacy predicts group performance** across diverse social systems including schools, organizations, and athletic teams (Bandura, 1993; Hodges & Carron, 1992; Little & Madigan, 1995). The stronger the beliefs people hold about their collective capabilities, the more they achieve (p.480).
4. **Political efficacy** involves beliefs that one can influence the political system. It has two aspects: personal political efficacy (belief that one can exert influence through political action) and system responsiveness (belief that the system is amenable to change). These two aspects are neither fixed nor independent -- they interact dynamically (pp.482-485).
5. **Televised modeling in dramatic serials (Sabido method)** is an extraordinarily effective vehicle for societal change -- producing measurable increases in literacy program enrollment, contraceptive use, and birthrate reduction across Mexico, India, Kenya, Tanzania, and Brazil (pp.506-510).
6. **Social diffusion of innovations** follows a four-phase model: (1) selecting an optimal site, (2) creating preconditions for change, (3) implementing a demonstrably effective program, (4) using the power of successful example to disperse the innovation (pp.513-515). The enablement strategy (providing guidance, supervision, and support) achieves adoption rates of 29%, compared to 16% for informational strategy and 1% for purely persuasory approaches (Fairweather et al., 1969).

## Detailed Notes

### 1. Gauging Collective Efficacy (pp.478-482)

- Perceived collective efficacy is an emergent group-level attribute rather than simply the sum of members' perceived personal efficacies
- A host of factors contribute to the interactive effects: mix of knowledge and competencies, how the group is structured and coordinated, how well it is led, the strategies it adopts, and whether members interact in mutually facilitory or undermining ways
- The same participants can achieve different results depending on how well their particular skills and efforts are coordinated and guided
- Two indices of perceived collective efficacy: (a) aggregated individual -- members' appraisals of their personal capabilities for functions they perform, (b) aggregated holistic -- members' appraisals of their group's capability as a whole
- The relative predictiveness depends on degree of interdependent effort required (p.480): under low system interdependence, aggregate of individual efficacies has predictive value; under high system interdependence, holistic judgments of group's efficacy are more relevant (Gibson, 1995)
- Collective efficacy is not a monolithic group attribute -- it may vary across different domains of activities and different positions/functions within the same social system (Bandura, 1993)
- Agreement within groups rather than differences across groups is the major criterion of a shared belief attribute
- Measuring collective efficacy by group discussion has serious limitations: group members are rarely of one mind; pressures for conformity, few influential individuals swaying consensus (Bond & Smith, 1996)
- Prussia & Kinicki (1996): Experimentally demonstrated that sociocognitive determinants operate at the collective level as they do at the individual level. Vicarious experience, positive/negative feedback, group affective evaluations, and group goals all predicted collective efficacy and group performance in brainstorming tasks (Fig. 11.2)
- Academic domain especially suited for studying collective efficacy: educational districts include multiple schools administered centrally, pursuing the same mission, assessed with standardized instruments
- Bandura (1993): 79 elementary schools -- student body characteristics influence school achievement largely by altering staff's beliefs in their collective instructional efficacy (Fig. 11.1). Collective efficacy contributes independently after controlling for student characteristics, teacher experience, and prior achievement

### 2. Political Efficacy (pp.482-497)

**Structure of political efficacy beliefs (pp.482-485):**
- Two aspects of exercise of control especially relevant to social change through political effort: (1) level of personal efficacy (producing results by enlistment of effort and proficient use of capabilities), (2) amenability of the system to change by individual and collective influence
- Perceived political efficacy -- beliefs that one can influence the political system through political action -- should be distinguished from means efficacy (beliefs that a certain political means will be effective) and system responsiveness (beliefs that the system responds to citizens' influence)
- Unfortunately, many survey items purporting to measure perceived political efficacy do not actually assess political efficacy beliefs -- they mix in system responsiveness, political knowledge, and level of participation
- Perceived political efficacy is better explained and predicted by particularized efficacy beliefs that bear on legislative activities and institutional practices of interest than by global measures of general life efficacy or nondescript political influence (Gootnick, 1974; Fox & Schofield, 1989; Wollman & Stouder, 1991)
- Locus of control is nonpredictive of political action; the widely used general measure of perceived political efficacy varies from weakly predictive to nonpredictive

**Participatory dilemmas in collective action (pp.488-490):**
- Mobilization of collective effort poses participatory dilemmas: people can easily persuade themselves that their individual contribution will not really matter in a huge collective effort (connects to [[commons-dilemma]] and free-rider problem)
- Kerr (1996): The larger the collectivity, the more insignificant the individual effort may appear
- Incentives for engagement in collective endeavors are varied and change over the course of social events
- Collective self-interest (not personal freeloading) drives participation: people participate not just because of expected shared benefits but because of continuing costs of inaction -- conditions will worsen if no one acts
- Belief that collective action is achievable by a critical mass of activists rather than requiring universal participation sustains engagement
- Social and self-evaluative incentives (self-respect from challenging unjust practices, self-reproach from acquiescing) serve as powerful motivators alongside material benefits
- Strong sense of collective self-worth can help support tough struggles for social betterment

**Joint impact of efficacy and system trust on political activism (pp.490-491):**
- Gamson (1968): People are moved to social action when they feel politically efficacious but mistrustful of their leaders and government
- Politically efficacious individuals are more apt to take action regardless of whether they hold the political system in high or low regard (Hawkins, Marando, & Taylor, 1971; Fraser, 1970)
- Four types of political actors based on efficacy x system trust: (1) trustful efficacious -- conventional political participation, (2) distrustful efficacious -- confrontational tactics outside traditional channels, (3) trustful inefficacious -- political passivity, (4) distrustful inefficacious -- political alienation
- Wolfsfeld (1986): The politically inactive have a low sense of all modes of political efficacy and view all modes of activism as ineffective. Active conventionalists express high efficacy for conventional but low for protest tactics. Dissidents express strong belief in protest efficacy but view conventional tactics as ineffective. Pragmatists express high approval and efficacy for all forms

**Development of political efficacy and system trust (pp.491-492):**
- Development of beliefs about political influence starts early in life through modeling
- From elementary to high school, children's beliefs in their efficacy for political action increase, but cynicism about government also increases
- African-American youths: lower sense of political efficacy and higher political cynicism than white youths (Lyons, 1970; Rodgers, 1974). Controls for socioeconomic status qualify these differences
- Racial differences disappear for high achievers; the more minority youths learn about the political system academically, the more cynical they become about it

**Electronic campaigning and the political process (pp.492-497):**
- Call-in media readily accessible to people of limited socioeconomic status; the more advantaged electorate is cruising the Internet
- Interactive communication through computer networks brings perceived efficacy even more heavily into the political influence process: fewer social constraints, transcends time/place/national boundaries
- Voter turnout declining: only about 40% of eligible electorate votes. Combined effect of eroding party loyalties and growing cynicism
- Computerized politicking provides vast opportunities for participatory discourse but does not dictate its quality
- Image politics: easier to shape images of candidates by portraying favorable personal characteristics than by addressing important issues substantively
- Tobacco industry lobbying illustrates power dynamics: the more campaign money legislators receive, the more likely they are to vote against tobacco regulation (Fig. 11.3, Public Citizen Health Research Group, 1993)
- Common Cause (Gardner, 1972): Citizens' organization working for structural reforms that make governmental system more responsive to public will

**Collective efficacy and militant social action (pp.497-500):**
- Authoritarian regimes use suppressive practices to block collective organization and stifle collective efficacy
- Challengers must create belief in collective capability to rout oppressors largely through modeled successes of others who toppled autocratic regimes under similar conditions
- Fall of Communist regimes in Eastern Europe: modeling enablement process -- social discontent was widespread, and the same collective strategy was adopted across countries with different structural conditions (Braithwaite, 1994)
- The modeling theory of political enablement provides a better explanation for the timing and form of collective action than does structural theory
- Consistent with efficacy theory, those who instigate collective protest have met with at least some success, are generally better educated, have greater self-pride, and have a stronger belief in their ability to influence events (Caplan, 1970; Crawford & Naditch, 1970)

### 3. Building Community-Wide Efficacy for Social Change (pp.500-504)

- Alinsky (1969, 1971): Community organizing approach founded on converting social apathy to community-wide efficacy. Cardinal principle: "Never do anything for someone that they can do for themselves"
- Role of community organizer: not to solve people's problems for them but to help develop their capabilities to operate as a continuing potent force
- Social power comes in three forms: political power, the power of money, and the power of numbers. The disadvantaged have the power of numbers if well organized
- Alinsky's enablement model: enlists motivational goals in their most effective forms -- explicit, bearing on daily lives, realizable through concerted effort, structured proximally for tangible evidence of progress
- Modern adaptations of the Alinsky model have shed blazoned confrontational tactics in favor of negotiatory mode; shifted from galvanizing around contentious issues to developing people's competencies and building communal ties
- Self-help groups: people organize to overcome intractable problems by creating their own subcommunity, managing own affairs, creating their own businesses (Riessman & Carroll, 1995). Builds on strengths rather than dwelling on deficits
- Collective enablement programs: training in group advocacy skills raises both personal and collective efficacy and increases actions designed to bring about desired changes (Yeich & Levine, 1994)

### 4. Enablement by Media Modes of Influence (pp.504-510)

**Media use for policy initiatives (pp.505):**
- Wallack et al. (1993): Codified principles for using media to influence public policies. Media advocacy involves gaining access to media and dramatizing issues in ways that move the public to action
- Building coalitions with diverse interest groups calls for high level of perceived coordinative efficacy

**Entertainment media in social change -- the Sabido method (pp.505-510):**
- Sabido (1981): Format for promoting society-wide changes through televised modeling in dramatic serials
- Five key features: (1) capitalizes on power of social modeling to alter attitudes, values, and lifestyle patterns, (2) relies on model similarity, (3) designed for attentional engagement (melodramatic embellishments), (4) epilogues with symbolic coding of modeled messages, (5) enlists environmental supports and referrals to agencies and self-help groups
- Mexico literacy project: soap opera depicting self-instruction group. Viewership exceeded standard soap operas. Enrollment in national self-instruction program increased from 99,000 to 840,000 during year of series; another 400,000 enrolled the following year
- Mexico family planning serial: 32% increase in new contraceptive users; 23% increase in national contraceptive sales (vs. 4-7% in preceding years); 34% drop in birthrate over several years
- India: Modeling series raised status of women, promoted family planning; viewers reported they had learned women should have equal opportunities and family size should be limited. A famous Indian film actor reinforced modeled messages in epilogues
- Kenya: The higher the exposure to the series, the stronger the effects on birthrate and reproductive behavior (Westoff & Rodriguez, 1995)
- Tanzania: Controlled study -- radio series significantly increased adoption of contraceptive methods, family planning services, and AIDS prevention practices vs. control region (Vaughan, Rogers, & Swalehe, 1995). Perceived self-efficacy emerged as an important factor in regulating reproductive behavior
- Results across all applications: airing televised serials consistently followed by appreciable drop in birthrates (34% in Mexico, 23% in Brazil, 58% increase in contraceptive usage in Kenya)
- Population Communications International: coordinates global diffusion of televised dramas, works with media personnel and nongovernmental organizations

### 5. Enablement for Sociocultural Change (pp.511-519)

**Obstacles to social change (pp.511-513):**
- Adopters must abandon secure routines and learn new ways, going through intimidating and tedious process of mastering new competencies
- Those with an insecure sense of efficacy are intimidated by new demands and the prospect of failure
- Even greater obstacles: privileged groups that benefit from existing social arrangements use coercive pressure and moral codes to resist change
- Interdependent arrangements that tie people's benefits to common goals help overcome resistance
- Social cognitive theory addresses motivation for change in terms of explicit cognitive factors (efficacy beliefs, outcome expectations, aspirations, perceived impediments) rather than attitudes alone

**Properties of efficacious models of diffusion (pp.513-516):**
- Four-phase model of social diffusion: (1) selecting an optimal site, (2) creating necessary preconditions for change, (3) implementing a demonstrably effective program, (4) using power of successful example to disperse the innovation
- Innovations best introduced initially where people are willing to try them provisionally; successes then serve as demonstration models
- Three-facet approach (modeling of desired competencies, guided enactments, generalized applications) yields most impressive results (Goldstein, 1973; Latham & Saari, 1979)
- Fairweather et al. (1969): Compared three diffusion strategies for psychiatric treatment model. Informational strategy: 1% adoption. Social persuasion strategy: 16% adoption. Enablement strategy (teaching staff to implement, providing supportive supervision): 29% adoption
- Vicarious incentives from seeing benefits gained by initial adopters serve to weaken social impediments to adoption (Gray, 1973; Poel, 1976)

**Social impact of innovations (pp.516-517):**
- Same innovation can create markedly different distribution of benefits under different social structures (Gotsch, 1972): tube wells in Pakistan benefited only large landholders in feudal system but benefited small cooperative farmers in Bangladesh
- Self-efficacy theory approaches equitable distribution by expanding opportunities for the less advantaged through cultivating their talents, strengthening their belief in capability, and providing needed resources

**Communications innovations and social diffusion (pp.517-519):**
- Social cognitive theory analyzes social diffusion of ideas, values, and social practices in terms of three constituent processes: acquisition of knowledge about new ways, adoption of these behavioral practices, and diffusion through social networks
- Symbolic modeling is the principal conveyor of innovations to widely dispersed areas in early phases
- The view that diffusion is exclusively a filter-down process from opinion leaders is disputed -- media can instill ideas directly (Bandura, 1986a)
- Perceived personal efficacy determines how complex things look: activities within bounds of perceived capabilities are viewed as doable, those exceeding capabilities appear complex (Rogers, 1983; Tornatzky & Klein, 1982)
- Both preexisting and induced level of perceived self-efficacy play an influential role in adoption and social diffusion of behavioral practices (Maibach et al., 1991; Slater, 1989)
- Social networks structure: people are enmeshed in networks of relationships (kinship, friendships, occupational colleagues) that create overlapping network clusters. Weak ties paradoxically spread innovations more efficiently than strong ties (Granovetter, 1983)

## New Concepts Introduced
- [[collective-efficacy]] -- group's shared belief in its conjoint capabilities (full treatment)
- [[political-efficacy]] -- beliefs about influencing the political system through political action

## Contradictions or Tensions
- Collective efficacy can be undermined by the free-rider problem ([[commons-dilemma]]): rational self-interest would predict minimal participation in large collective endeavors, but people participate anyway due to continuing costs of inaction and self-evaluative incentives
- The distrustful-but-efficacious type challenges simple models: high political efficacy + low system trust can produce the most confrontational forms of activism, not apathy
- The enablement approach to social change stands in contrast to both the informational/attitude-change approach (which rarely changes behavior) and the purely coercive approach (which breeds regimentation)
