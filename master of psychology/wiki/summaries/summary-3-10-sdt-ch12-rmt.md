# Relationships Motivation Theory (Ch. 12) — Summary

**Author(s):** Richard M. Ryan, Edward L. Deci
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Autonomy and relatedness are complementary, not opposing.** RMT's central thesis is that the needs for [[autonomy-need|autonomy]] and [[relatedness-need|relatedness]] are inherently synergistic -- each need's satisfaction facilitates the other's. Social contexts such as [[conditional-regard]] that pit these needs against each other invariably produce negative psychological outcomes.
2. **Need satisfaction within relationships mediates the link between social support and wellness.** It is not the sheer quantity of social contact but the degree to which relationships satisfy all three [[basic-psychological-needs]] (autonomy, competence, relatedness) that predicts relationship quality, attachment security, and individual well-being.
3. **Autonomy support is the single most important relational behavior.** Receiving [[autonomy-support]] from a close partner predicts emotional reliance, authenticity, transparency, and relationship satisfaction. Remarkably, *giving* autonomy support to others is also independently associated with the giver's own well-being -- sometimes even more strongly than receiving it.
4. **Mutuality of autonomy support amplifies relationship quality.** When both partners in a dyad perceive mutual autonomy support, the benefits to need satisfaction, attachment security, emotional reliance, and dyadic adjustment are greater than when only one partner provides it (RMT Proposition V).
5. **Conditional regard and objectification are the primary mechanisms by which relationships thwart needs.** When relational partners make love or approval contingent on specific behaviors, or treat partners as objects valued for external attributes rather than as autonomous selves, both autonomy and relatedness are frustrated simultaneously.

## Detailed Notes

### The Importance of Relationships and Relatedness

RMT is the sixth and final mini-theory within [[self-determination-theory-framework|SDT]]. It focuses specifically on the qualities of close personal relationships and their consequences for wellness.

Ryan and Deci ground the chapter in Harry Harlow's primate experiments (Harlow, 1958), which showed that deprivation of social contact and warmth caused global deficits in social and motivational development, independent of physiological needs. Object relations theorists (Winnicott, 1965; Fairbairn, 1952; Bowlby, 1969) similarly emphasized an inherent human propensity to relate to others. More recently, Baumeister and Leary (1995) proposed a basic *need for belongingness* -- a need to maintain connection and acceptance by others.

**Not all social interactions convey relatedness.** Mere contact frequency or relationship status does not determine need satisfaction. Exchanges with ticket agents or store clerks that are role-bound and transactional have little interpersonal salience. Even interactions with close friends or romantic partners can be experienced as impersonal, agenda-driven, or superficial. In contrast, encounters with strangers can sometimes deeply satisfy relatedness. What matters is *how* people relate to each other -- specifically whether the interaction involves perceived autonomous and authentic caring between self and other.

RMT proposes that in the highest-quality relationships, there is concomitant satisfaction of both relatedness and autonomy needs. It is experiences that involve *acceptance and support of the self* that people find most truly relational.

### Close Relationships and RMT

Close relationships (friendships, romantic partnerships) are "elective" (within cultural limits) and require personal motivation to maintain. RMT is unique among relationship theories in accounting for the importance of multiple [[basic-psychological-needs]] and volitional motivation in close adult relationships.

SDT distinguishes *ultimate causes* (evolutionary selective advantages) from *proximal intrinsic motivations* (phenomenal reasons). Connecting with others is intrinsically valued -- not instrumentally directed at adaptive benefits but motivated by caring and love. When someone's friendship is experienced as *unconditional*, relatedness is most deeply satisfying.

**RMT Proposition I:** People have a basic psychological need for [[relatedness-need|relatedness]], the satisfaction of which is essential to growth, integrity, and wellness, and the frustration of which can play a causal role in ill-being.

**Supporting evidence for Proposition I:**
- **Reis, Sheldon, Gable, Roscoe, & Ryan (2000):** Diary study over 2 weeks showed that satisfaction of the relatedness need was an independent positive predictor of well-being for both males and females, using multilevel modeling at both between-person and within-person levels.
- **Ryan, Bernstein, & Brown (2010):** Experience sampling with working adults replicated the independent contribution of relatedness to wellness. Relatedness satisfaction fluctuated by day of week, with greater vitality and positive affect on weekends when people were with close others.
- **Gagne, Ryan, & Bargmann (2003):** Female gymnasts' reports of relatedness need satisfaction during workout sessions predicted increases in positive affect, vitality, and self-esteem from before to after each session.
- **Ostracism research (Cyberball paradigm; Williams, 2009):** Virtual exclusion led to negative affect and distress. Legate, DeHaan, Weinstein, and Ryan (2013) showed that the negative affect from exclusion was mediated by lower satisfaction of the need for relatedness.
- **Eisenberger, Lieberman, & Williams (2003):** Argued that ostracism triggers "social pain" involving some of the same neural activation patterns as physical pain.

### Being Autonomously Motivated within Close Personal Relationships

**RMT Proposition II:** High-quality relationships are facilitated not only by having close and enduring social contact but also by experiencing [[autonomous-motivation]] within and for that contact. Autonomous motivation -- the individual's authentic willingness to participate -- contributes to high satisfaction and greater psychological wellness in both parties.

**Key studies:**
- **Blais, Sabourin, Boucher, & Vallerand (1990):** Married or cohabiting heterosexual couples who experienced greater relative autonomy showed greater dyadic adjustment, general relationship satisfaction, and willingness to work through problems rather than viewing them as annoyances. A structural model confirmed: (1) autonomous motivation predicted positive relational behaviors, (2) behaviors predicted adaptive beliefs about the relationship, and (3) beliefs predicted personal happiness.
- **Knee, Lonsbary, Canevello, & Patrick (2005):** Four studies extending the Blais et al. work.
  - *Study 1 (diary study):* Relational autonomy predicted more satisfaction after disagreements.
  - *Study 2:* Relational autonomy predicted greater understanding of one's partner and less defensiveness in conflicts.
  - *Study 3:* Both person's and partner's relational autonomy predicted less defensiveness and more understanding, with the partner's autonomy also contributing significant variance.
  - *Study 4:* Naive raters coded videotaped discussions -- greater relational autonomy was associated with more understanding and open (nondefensive) behavior.
- **Patrick (2007):** Pro-relationship behaviors (making sacrifices for partners) performed for autonomous reasons predicted more relationship satisfaction, closeness, vitality, and self-esteem. In a subsequent diary study, being autonomous in pro-relational behaviors left people feeling greater vitality and higher self-esteem.
- **Gore, Cross, & Kanagawa (2009):** Differentiated *personal autonomy* (for being in the relationship) from *relational autonomy* (being autonomous in supporting the close other's interests and needs). Relational autonomy explained positive outcome variance over and above personal autonomy. This shows that acting "for the other" in a willing, volitional way reflects having integrated the interests of the other -- and is a fully autonomous activity.
- **Gaine & La Guardia (2009):** Developed a *Motivations for Relational Activities Scale* assessing autonomy for specific behaviors within a relationship. Autonomy for specific relational activities explained variance beyond personal autonomy for being in the relationship.
- **Kindt et al. (2015):** Partners of patients suffering from intense pain -- autonomous (vs. controlled) motives for helping related to less exhaustion and more relationship-based need satisfaction for the helper. When helping motivation was more autonomous, there was higher patient-reported relationship quality and lower distress.
- **Hadden, Rodriguez, Knee, & Porter (2015):** Autonomous motivation for a relationship was associated with more availability and responsiveness to the partner and less intrusiveness. In a dyadic study, participants who were more autonomously motivated had partners who felt their basic psychological needs were more supported.

### Autonomous Causality Orientations in Relationships

People with higher [[autonomy-orientation]] (a dispositional trait assessed by the General Causality Orientations Scale, GCOS) tend to have higher quality relationships.

- **Hodgins, Koestner, & Duncan (1996):** In two studies, those higher on autonomy orientation reported more positive and honest relationships, with more self-disclosure and flexible interaction patterns.
- **Knee, Patrick, Vietor, Nanayakkara, & Neighbors (2002):** Partners high in autonomy orientation expressed less negative emotion during conflicts, used more relationship-maintaining coping strategies, and showed more positive relationship-oriented behaviors.

**Priming autonomy in close relationships:**
- **Niemiec (2010, unpublished):** Two strangers primed with autonomy (vs. control or neutral) orientations via scrambled sentence task engaged in a structured self-disclosure activity. Autonomy-primed dyads showed more satisfaction with the new relationship, more positive affect, more relatedness need satisfaction, greater well-being, and behavioral closeness (setting chairs closer together).
- **Weinstein, Hodgins, & Ryan (2010):** Dyads primed with autonomy (vs. control) during creative tasks (remote associates, charades) were rated as more emotionally and cognitively attuned, more empathic, more encouraging, more engaged with tasks, performed tasks more effectively, and reported more closeness.

### Perceiving the Other as Being Autonomous

- **Weinstein, DeHaan, & Ryan (2010):** Three experiments showed that recipients experienced more gratitude toward helpers with autonomous (vs. introjected/controlled) motivations, more positive attitudes toward helpers, and more felt closeness. Attributions of autonomous motivation independently predicted gratitude even controlling for perceived helper empathy, cost to helper, perceived value of help, and perceived similarity.

### Basic Need Satisfactions, High-Quality Relationships, and Well-Being

**RMT Proposition IIIa:** Within relationships the satisfactions of all three basic psychological needs for [[relatedness-need|relatedness]], [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], and [[competence-need|competence]] contribute to, and in fact define, higher quality relationships and facilitate greater relationship satisfaction, attachment security, and well-being.

**RMT Proposition IIIb:** Within relationships the frustration of psychological needs for relatedness, autonomy, or competence contributes to relationship dysfunction and defense and greater relationship dissatisfaction, insecurity, and ill-being.

**Key studies:**
- **Patrick, Knee, Canevello, & Lonsbary (2007):** Satisfaction of each of the three needs contributed significant variance to personal well-being (self-esteem, positive affect, less negative affect, vitality), relationship well-being (commitment, effective conflict management), and especially relationship satisfaction. Relatedness was the strongest predictor, but autonomy and competence contributed significantly beyond relatedness. A subsequent study showed that the *partner's* need satisfaction also contributed to the target individual's relational well-being.

### Need Satisfaction and Attachment Security

RMT proposes that security of attachment varies not only between persons (individual differences per traditional attachment theory) but also *within persons across relationships*, as a function of relationship-specific need support.

- **La Guardia, Ryan, Couchman, & Deci (2000):** Three studies examining attachment security across relationships with mothers, fathers, romantic partners, and best friends. About 35% of attachment security variance was between-person (confirming working model effects). But considerably more than half was within-person, systematically explained by need satisfaction with specific partners. Relatedness satisfaction was the strongest predictor; autonomy need satisfaction was also very significant. Even removing relatedness from analyses, autonomy and competence needs significantly predicted attachment security. Both need satisfaction and attachment security predicted psychological well-being and relationship satisfaction.
- **Ducat & Zimmer-Gembeck (2007):** Found significant relations between individuals' adult attachment styles and both well-being and relationship quality. Satisfaction of basic psychological needs within the relationship mediated these relations.
- **Wei, Shaffer, Young, & Zakalik (2005):** Insecure attachment was related to loneliness, depression, and shame; these relations were mediated by frustration of basic psychological needs.
- **Leak & Cooney (2001):** Individuals high in secure attachment were more autonomously motivated in relationship engagement. Autonomous motivation for relationship engagement mediated the relation between secure attachment and well-being.

### Need Thwarting in Relationships

Need thwarting in close relationships can take many forms: dominance and control (thwarting autonomy), coldness or distancing (thwarting relatedness), criticalness and derogation (thwarting competence). It can also manifest as stigmatization, prejudice, and exclusion.

- **Costa, Ntoumanis, & Bartholomew (2015):** Need frustration within relationships was uniquely associated with rejection sensitivity, whereas need satisfaction better predicted interpersonal competence.

### Autonomy-Supportive Partners: Facilitating Interdependence and Full Functioning

[[Autonomy-support]] in relationships involves taking the partner's *internal frame of reference*, conveying empathy and respect, facilitating self-initiated expressions and actions, providing informational (not controlling) feedback, and facilitating the partner's self-organization and self-regulation.

**RMT Proposition IVa:** Individuals who experience [[autonomy-support]] from their partners within a close relationship will be more willing to emotionally rely on those partners and to turn to them for support.

- **Ryan, La Guardia, Solky-Butzel, Chirkov, & Kim (2005):** Series of studies on emotional reliance across varied ages and cultural backgrounds. Those more willing to turn to others for emotional support had greater wellness. Willingness to rely on a given parent was strongly associated with that parent's degree of autonomy support. At the within-person level, the more an individual experienced a relational partner as providing autonomy support, the greater the willingness to rely on that partner. Replicated across Russia, Turkey, South Korea, and the United States.
- **Lynch, La Guardia, & Ryan (2009):** Replicated the emotional reliance findings in Russia and China, showing that volitional reliance was predicted by perceived autonomy supportiveness across cultures.

**RMT Proposition IVb:** Individuals who experience autonomy support within a close relationship will be more able to "be themselves" -- that is, to be authentic and transparent and to function closer to their own ideals.

**Supporting evidence:**
- **La Guardia (2006):** People who felt greater need satisfaction in relationships reported more contact with their inner emotions, more willingness to examine those emotions, and more self-disclosure of emotional experiences. These factors contributed to a greater sense of intimacy, vitality, and aliveness.
- **Sheldon, Ryan, Rawsthorne, & Ilardi (1997):** Authenticity is associated with greater relationship satisfaction and lower strain in both friendships and romantic relationships.
- **Lewis & Neighbors (2005):** Autonomy is associated with less use of self-presentation strategies.
- **Lynch et al. (2009):** Cross-cultural study (Russia, U.S., China) with 600+ participants. Individuals were closer to their ideal trait profile (assessed via the Big Five) when with autonomy-supportive partners. When people felt less autonomy support, they moved further from their ideals -- becoming less extraverted, open, agreeable, and more neurotic. Relationship satisfaction and vitality were higher in autonomy-supportive relationships. Results held across cultures, regardless of independent vs. interdependent self-construal.

### Concealment

Self-concealment in close relationships thwarts autonomy and relatedness:

- **Uysal, Lin, & Knee (2010):** Self-concealment predicted thwarting of all three basic needs, plus negative outcomes. A diary study over 16 days confirmed daily associations between concealment, need satisfaction, and well-being.
- **Uysal, Lin, Knee, & Bush (2012):** Self-concealment was associated with lower relationship satisfaction and commitment, mediated by low autonomy and relatedness satisfaction. Cross-lagged analyses showed that a partner's concealing on one day predicted lower relationship well-being the following day. The *partner's* concealment also contributed to diminished satisfaction and commitment.

### Mutuality of Autonomy and Autonomy Support in Relationships

**RMT Proposition V:** Autonomy-supportive partners in close relationships tend to experience a sense of mutuality -- when one partner experiences autonomy or autonomy support, the other is more likely to experience it as well. The greater the mutuality, the greater the relationship satisfaction, attachment security, and well-being of both partners.

**Key studies:**
- **Deci, La Guardia, Moller, Scheiner, & Ryan (2006) -- Study 1:** Close friend dyads. Each partner reported on autonomy support received from their best-friend partner. Results at the individual level showed that perceptions of autonomy support received contributed to higher need satisfaction, greater security of attachment, more emotional reliance, and more inclusion of the friend in the self. At the dyad level, there was significant mutuality in perceived autonomy support. The level of mutuality was significantly related to need satisfaction, attachment security, emotional reliance, and inclusion of other in self.
- **Deci, La Guardia, et al. (2006) -- Study 2:** Added measures of autonomy support *given* (not just received), positive and negative emotions in the relationship, and willingness to express those emotions. All Study 1 findings replicated. *Giving* autonomy support had a positive relation to every variable except negative affect. When giving and receiving competed for variance, *giving* autonomy support had the stronger relation to the person's own well-being.
- **Gendered analysis:** Data from the best-friend study were separated into male-male and female-female dyads. For both groups, perceived autonomy support from friends significantly predicted indicators of relationship quality.

### Conditional Regard: Turning Basic Psychological Needs Against Each Other

**RMT Proposition VI:** Although, inherently, satisfactions of the basic psychological needs are complementary and positive, if the social environment turns any two against each other -- for example, if an individual's relational partner requires the individual to relinquish satisfaction of one need (e.g., autonomy) in order to get satisfaction of another (e.g., relatedness) -- the individual will experience a poorer relationship quality and a lower level of wellness.

[[Conditional-regard]] makes love, attention, and affection contingent upon the individual doing what the relational partner demands. This is "quite controlling, because the children must perform a specified behavior in order to get their parents' love -- that is, they must give up autonomy to get relatedness."

**Key studies on conditional regard:**
- **Assor, Roth, & Deci (2004):** College students who reported higher parental [[conditional-regard]] (PCR) also reported higher internal pressure to perform target behaviors, but with substantial well-being costs: fragile self-esteem, short-lived satisfaction after success, shame and guilt after failure, and poorer coping strategies. Parents' conditional regard also damaged the parent-child relationship: children felt more rejected, with more anger and resentment toward parents.
- **Assor (2004):** When parents offer esteem contingent on specific attributes and behaviors, children develop *contingent self-esteem* -- esteeming themselves conditionally based on displaying the attributes that garnered parental esteem.
- **Knee, Canavello, Bush, & Cook (2008):** Examined *relationship-contingent self-esteem* in adults. This variable was a negative factor: when both partners were high in it, they were committed but the commitment did not lead to closeness or satisfaction. When one partner was high and the other low, the low partner showed very low commitment. Relationship-contingent self-esteem makes people "needy" in the relationship but does not yield positive relational outcomes.
- **Moller, Roth, Niemiec, Kanat-Maymon, & Deci (2016):** Series of studies on association between parental conditional regard and children's later experiences.
  - Students who perceived parents as conditionally regarding also perceived their *peer partners* as conditionally regarding.
  - When mothers and fathers were conditionally regarding, children experienced less psychological need satisfaction -- not only with those parents but also with best friends and romantic partners.
  - At the within-person level, need satisfaction with a specific peer partner predicted security of attachment with that partner.
  - **Kanat-Maymon, Roth, Assor, & Raizer (2016):** Separated *conditional positive regard* from *conditional negative regard*. Both forms predicted poorer quality romantic relationships, even after controlling for perceived negative regard and partners' warmth. These links were mediated by need satisfaction.
  - **Projection/selection finding:** Students who perceived parents as high in conditional regard also tended to have peer partners who were, by their own accounts, conditionally regarding. Correlation between the length of time together and partner's conditional regard was not significant, suggesting *selection* (choosing similar partners) rather than *training*.
  - An experimental paradigm (naive participant + confederate accomplice) showed that students who rated parents high on conditional regard were more likely to rate the (identically behaving) confederate as conditionally regarding -- suggesting defensive *projection* of conditional regard.

### Relating to Selves, Relating to Objects

High-quality relating means interacting openly with each other from one's authentic self. The Buber epigraph -- "Whoever says You does not have something for his object" -- frames this section.

**RMT Proposition VII:** To the degree that an individual in a relationship relates to the partner more as an object, stereotype, or thing, rather than as a person intrinsically worthy of respect, the partner will accordingly experience thwarting of the basic psychological needs, resulting in a lower quality relationship and poorer well-being.

- **Objectification:** Valuing a partner for wealth, looks, status, or other external attributes rather than relating to the person's true self. Roberts and Waters (2012) highlighted that women are often objectified -- viewed and related to in terms of their bodies and contingently valued on appearance. This is internalized as *self-objectification* (Plant & Ryan, 1985), relinquishing autonomy.
- **Kasser & Ryan (2001):** Holding strong extrinsic goals and values was associated with poorer quality romantic relationships and less satisfying friendships. Progress in attaining extrinsic goals was associated with less satisfying relationships.
- **Rempel, Holmes, & Zanna (1985):** Studied dating and married couples' intrinsic vs. extrinsic goals for being in the relationship. The more intrinsic the motives, the more love, faith in the relationship, and trust. The more they perceived their partner as being in the relationship for intrinsic reasons, the more trust they experienced.
- **Kurdek & Schnopp-Wyatt (1997):** Longitudinal study of college dating couples. The strength of the male partner's intrinsic relationship values interacted with the female partner's intrinsic values to predict relationship stability over 6 months. Mutuality in intrinsic values for intimacy and self-development was an important precursor for the relationship's endurance.

### Concluding Comments

The chapter concludes with a seven-point summary of RMT's propositions:
1. The basic psychological need for relatedness underpins people's intrinsic motivation to engage with others in high-quality relationships.
2. Being autonomously motivated for the relationship is essential for high-quality, securely attached relationships.
3. Satisfactions of all three psychological needs within a close relationship promote both psychological and relational well-being at within-person and between-person levels.
4. Having partners who are autonomy-supportive facilitates more satisfying relationships for the recipient.
5. Mutuality of autonomy and autonomy support are key characteristics of high-quality relationships.
6. When the environment turns satisfaction of two needs against each other, people's relationships suffer and they evidence lower well-being.
7. When people relate to others as objects rather than persons, the relationships are low in quality and participants tend to display low wellness.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Autonomy vs. relatedness debate:** Some theorists (e.g., Markus & Kitayama, 2003) have claimed autonomy is irrelevant or harmful in collectivist/interdependent cultures. RMT and cross-cultural data (Lynch et al., 2009; Ryan, La Guardia et al., 2005) firmly refute this, showing autonomy support facilitates relationship quality across Russia, Turkey, South Korea, China, and the U.S.
- **Attachment theory integration:** RMT extends attachment theory by showing that attachment security varies not only between persons (as traditional attachment theory emphasizes) but also *within* persons across relationships, as a function of relationship-specific need satisfaction (La Guardia et al., 2000).
- **Independence vs. autonomy:** RMT sharply distinguishes these. Independence (separation from others) is not the same as autonomy (volitional self-governance). One can be autonomously dependent on others -- and this is healthy.
