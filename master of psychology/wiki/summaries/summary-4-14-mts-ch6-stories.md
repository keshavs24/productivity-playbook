# Made to Stick: Ch.6 Stories -- Summary

**Author(s):** Chip Heath, Dan Heath
**Type:** Book chapter (Ch. 6, pp. 214-247) + Epilogue (pp. 245-265)
**Phase:** 4 -- Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Stories are flight simulators for the brain ([[story-as-simulation]]).** Research shows that hearing a story activates the same brain regions as experiencing the events. Mental simulation of events (not outcomes) improves problem-solving, emotion management, and even physical skill acquisition -- mental practice alone produces about two-thirds of the benefits of actual physical practice.
2. **Stories provide both simulation (knowledge about how to act) and inspiration (motivation to act) -- the dual engines of action ([[story-as-inspiration]]).** The nurse who overruled a heart monitor to save a baby's life provides simulation (a diagnostic pattern to recognize) and inspiration (the courage to challenge authority).
3. **Three plot types account for the vast majority of inspirational stories:** The Challenge Plot (overcoming obstacles -- David and Goliath, Jared the Subway guy), the Connection Plot (bridging social divides -- the Good Samaritan), and the Creativity Plot (innovative problem-solving -- the MacGyver plot, the Grinder Team's Drag Test).
4. **The most powerful stories are spotted, not created.** The Jared Subway campaign became a sensation not because someone invented the story but because a franchise owner (Bob Ocwieja) spotted its potential and a creative director (Richard Coad) invested in pursuing it. Story-spotting is a learnable skill.
5. **Stories naturally embody the SUCCESs framework and single-handedly defeat the Curse of Knowledge.** They are almost always Concrete, usually have Emotional and Unexpected elements. The hardest part is ensuring they are Simple -- that they reflect the core message. A great story must reflect your agenda, not just be entertaining.

## Detailed Notes

### The Nurse and the Heart Monitor

- A neonatal ICU nurse noticed a baby's color changing from pink to blue-black. The medical team assumed a collapsed lung (common for ventilator babies) and prepared to pierce the chest
- The nurse suspected a pneumopericardium (air strangling the heart) because of the baby's color pattern. The heart monitor showed a normal rate of 130 bpm, contradicting her theory
- She pushed everyone's hands away, demanded silence, and used a stethoscope -- there was no heartbeat. The heart monitor was measuring electrical activity, not actual heartbeats
- She started compressions. The chief neonatologist inserted a syringe into the heart to release the trapped air. The baby was saved
- **Story's dual power:** (1) Simulation -- it teaches a diagnostic pattern (when heart monitor data conflicts with visual observation, trust your senses), (2) Inspiration -- a nurse in a hierarchical environment had the courage to overrule the chief neonatologist
- This story was collected by Gary Klein, who studies decision-making in high-pressure environments. Klein says stories in these environments are told and retold because they contain wisdom

### Shop Talk at the Xerox Lunchroom

- Researcher Julian Orr found that Xerox copier repairmen spent significant time swapping stories over lunch
- One story: A new XER board configuration creates misleading E053 error codes that point to an unrelated area of the machine. A repairman and his partner spent four hours chasing the wrong problem before identifying a burned-out dicorotron
- The story format is more interesting than a simple tip because it gives enough information for listeners to mentally test how they would have handled the situation
- Shop talk is part entertainment, part instruction. It teaches nurses not to trust heart monitors blindly and copy repairmen to beware of misleading error codes
- Stories are not just transferring information nuggets -- they put knowledge into a lifelike framework, more like a flight simulator

### The Un-passive Audience -- [[story-as-simulation]]

- When we read or hear stories, we are not passive -- we instinctively empathize, identify with protagonists, and mentally simulate the events
- **Reading-time experiment:** When a story character "put on his sweatshirt before jogging," readers processed a later reference to the sweatshirt faster than when the character "took off his sweatshirt before jogging" (leaving it behind in a more remote mental location). We do not just visualize stories; we simulate them with spatial relationships
- **UCLA problem-solving study:** Three groups of students with a stressful problem:
  - Control group: Got generic problem-solving advice and went home
  - Event-simulation group: Mentally simulated how the problem had unfolded -- the chain of events leading to it
  - Outcome-simulation group: Mentally simulated a positive outcome -- the relief of having solved it
  - Result: Event simulators did better on almost every dimension. They were more likely to have taken specific action to solve their problems, sought advice, reported learning and growth. Outcome simulators did no better than the control group
- **The counterintuitive finding:** Simulating the process (how events unfolded) is far more helpful than simulating the outcome (imagining success). Financial gurus should not be telling us to imagine being rich -- they should be telling us to replay the steps that led to our being poor
- Mental simulation activates the same brain modules as real physical activity. People imagining a flashing light activate the visual cortex; imagining a tapping sensation activates the tactile cortex
- **Mental practice builds skills:** A review of 35 studies with 3,214 participants showed that mental practice alone -- sitting quietly and picturing yourself performing a task -- improves performance significantly. Trombonists improved their playing; figure skaters improved their skating. Mental practice produces about two-thirds of the benefits of actual physical practice
- Stories are like flight simulators for the brain. Hearing the nurse's heart-monitor story is not like being there, but it is the next best thing

### Clinic: Dealing with Problem Students

- **Message 1 (Indiana University tips):** "Remain calm. Slow down and regularize your breathing. Don't become defensive. Attempt to defuse their anger." -- abstract, common-sense, not sticky
- **Message 2 (Professor Buckman's story):** A detailed narrative of how she dealt with a disruptive student -- calling him and his confidante to her office, arranging witnesses, telling him other students were complaining. His body language changed completely. She never had a problem with him again
- The story allows readers to simulate the process of dealing with a problem student. Many of the abstract tips from Message 1 are shown, not told, in the story. The solution (using peer pressure) is both concrete and unexpected
- **Punch line:** A few stories like Buckman's would be much more effective in training professors than any list of bullet points

### [[story-as-inspiration]]: The Tale of Jared

- In the late 1990s, Subway launched "7 Under 6" (seven subs under six grams of fat) -- a modest success based on a statistic
- Then came Jared Fogle: a 425-pound college student who created his own "Subway diet" (a foot-long veggie sub for lunch and a six-inch turkey sub for dinner) and lost 245 pounds in three months
- The chain of events to get Jared on TV was remarkable: a college newspaper article caught a Men's Health writer's eye, a Subway franchise owner (Bob Ocwieja) spotted the potential, a creative director (Richard Coad) investigated, the national marketing director rejected it ("I've seen that before"), lawyers said it was impossible, so regional franchisees ran it themselves, Krause made the ad for free, and when it aired January 1, 2000, Oprah called by the third day
- Subway's sales jumped 18% in 2000 and 16% more in 2001 -- while competitors grew at about 7% per year
- **The Jared story on the SUCCESs checklist:** Simple (eat subs, lose weight), Unexpected (lose weight by eating fast food), Concrete (oversized pants, particular sandwiches), Credible (antiauthority -- the 425-pound guy gives diet advice), Emotional (we care about an individual, not a statistic), Story (inspiration to act)
- The "7 Under 6" campaign had none of these advantages -- it was simple but not unexpected, not concrete, not emotional, not a story
- **Key insight:** We do not always have to create sticky ideas. Spotting them is often easier and more useful. What if we could count on our frontline people to spot great stories?

### The Art of Spotting -- Three Inspirational Plot Types

- Just as there are effective ad templates, there are effective story templates
- The Heaths analyzed hundreds of inspirational stories (from Chicken Soup for the Soul and People magazine) and found three basic plots that classify over 80% of them:

#### The Challenge Plot
- A protagonist overcomes a formidable challenge: David and Goliath, rags-to-riches, underdog stories, triumph of willpower
- The key element is that the obstacles seem daunting to the protagonist. Jared losing 245 pounds is a Challenge plot; his neighbor losing an inch is not
- Examples: Rose Blumkin ($500 to $100 million furniture business), the 1980 US hockey team, Rosa Parks
- Challenge plots inspire us by appealing to our perseverance and courage. They make us want to work harder, take on new challenges, overcome obstacles
- **When to use:** Kickoff parties, new projects, any situation requiring effort and endurance

#### The Connection Plot
- A story about a relationship that bridges a gap -- racial, class, ethnic, religious, demographic, or otherwise
- The Good Samaritan is the prototype -- a Samaritan (despised outsider) helps a wounded traveler when a priest and a Levite pass by. In modern terms, it is like an "atheist biker gang member" helping when respectable people do not
- Also works for romance stories (Romeo and Juliet, Titanic) and small gestures (the Mean Joe Greene Coke commercial)
- Connection plots inspire us in social ways -- to help others, be more tolerant, work with others, love others
- The most common kind of plot in the Chicken Soup series
- **When to use:** Company Christmas parties, team-building, any situation requiring social cohesion

#### The Creativity Plot
- Someone makes a mental breakthrough, solves a long-standing puzzle, or attacks a problem innovatively -- the MacGyver plot
- Newton's apple is the prototype
- **Ingersoll-Rand's Grinder Team:** Tasked with developing a new grinder in one year (normally four). The team needed to decide between plastic and metal casings. Instead of protracted lab testing, they tied samples to the bumper of a rental car and dragged them around a parking lot. This became known as "the Drag Test" -- a Creativity plot that reinforced the team's new culture of speed and pragmatism
- Creativity plots make us want to do something different, to be creative, to experiment with new approaches
- **When to use:** Reinventing culture, encouraging innovation

### Springboard Stories at the World Bank

- Stephen Denning was assigned to "knowledge management" at the World Bank in 1996 -- the corporate equivalent of Siberia
- He heard about a health-care worker in Kamana, Zambia, who logged onto the CDC website to find information on treating malaria -- a perfect example of knowledge management in action
- Denning initially dismissed it as a mere anecdote. Later, he realized it was the ideal springboard story
- When he had 10-12 minutes to sell knowledge management to senior leadership, he told the Zambia story instead of presenting theory and statistics. Immediately after, two executives rushed up to bombard him with implementation ideas. "They've stolen my idea!" Denning thought -- then: "How wonderful! It's become their idea!"
- **Springboard stories:** Stories that let people see how an existing problem might change. They tell people about possibilities
- Springboard stories combat skepticism by engaging the audience's "little voice inside the head" -- rather than trying to silence it, they give it something to do (imagine how the story applies to their own situation)
- Stories mobilize people to act by focusing them on potential solutions -- telling stories with visible goals and barriers shifts the audience into a problem-solving mode
- A springboard story is an exercise in mass customization -- each audience member uses the story as a springboard to slightly different destinations

### The Conference Storybook -- Stories vs. Abstractions

- Gary Klein's firm was asked to summarize a conference. They assigned monitors to each panel to jot down every story told. The resulting "storybook" was vivid and useful
- The conference organizer loved it and requested funds to publish it
- But the presenters were furious -- they did not want to be remembered for stories and anecdotes. They had invested hours distilling their experiences into abstract recommendations like "Keep the lines of communication open"
- **Klein's response:** These abstract slogans are meaningless compared to the story that showed how they had kept the lines of communication open during a plant shutdown
- The presenters were Tapping -- remembering the rich experiences behind their abstractions, not realizing the audience could not hear the same song
- **Key insight:** Stories can almost single-handedly defeat the Curse of Knowledge. They naturally embody most of the SUCCESs framework. The hardest part is making sure they are Simple -- that they reflect your core message

### Epilogue: What Sticks

- Ideas sometimes stick despite our best efforts to stop them (Durocher's "Nice guys finish last," Sherlock Holmes's "Elementary, my dear Watson" -- both misquotations that stuck because they were perfectly suited to their schemas)
- The audience gets a vote in what sticks -- they may change, improve, or partially retain your idea (Carville's "It's the economy, stupid" stuck while his other two phrases vanished)
- **The Communication Framework:** For an idea to stick, it must make the audience: (1) Pay attention -- UNEXPECTED, (2) Understand and remember -- CONCRETE, (3) Agree/Believe -- CREDIBLE, (4) Care -- EMOTIONAL, (5) Be able to act on it -- STORY
- The SUCCESs checklist is a substitute for this framework -- more tangible and less subject to the Curse of Knowledge
- **Stanford speech exercise:** Students gave one-minute persuasive speeches. The most polished speakers got the highest ratings. But when asked to recall ideas ten minutes later, students could barely remember one or two ideas per speech. Only 5% remembered any individual statistic. But 63% remembered the stories. Furthermore, there was almost no correlation between "speaking talent" and the ability to make ideas stick
- **Two villains:** (1) Decision paralysis -- too many choices, need to find the core, (2) The Curse of Knowledge -- you know things others do not, and you cannot remember what it was like not to know them
- **JFK vs. Floyd Lee:** You do not need JFK's charisma or power to make an idea stick. Floyd Lee, a mess hall cook, redefined his role as "in charge of morale" and soldiers drove across Baghdad's most dangerous roads to eat at his table. Normal people with normal jobs can make a difference by crafting sticky ideas
