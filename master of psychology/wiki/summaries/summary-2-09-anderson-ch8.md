# Cognitive Psychology and Its Implications Ch.8 -- Problem Solving

**Author(s):** John R. Anderson
**Type:** Book chapter (9th Edition)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Problem solving is best understood as a **search through a [[problem-space]]** -- a set of states connected by operators -- as formalized by Newell and Simon (1972) in their landmark *Human Problem Solving*
2. Humans select operators using three strategies: **backup avoidance** (avoiding undoing previous moves), **[[difference-reduction]]** (hill climbing toward the goal), and **[[means-ends-analysis]]** (creating subgoals to enable blocked operators) -- means-ends analysis is the most powerful and uniquely human
3. Problem-solving operators can be acquired by **discovery**, by **being told**, or by **example/analogy** -- learning from worked examples is at least as effective as verbal instruction, and the two produce identical brain activation patterns during problem solving (Lee, Fincham, & Anderson, 2015)
4. **[[functional-fixedness]]** -- people's inability to see objects beyond their conventional function -- is a major barrier to creative problem solving (Maier, 1931; Duncker, 1945), but training participants to decompose objects into primitive parts and features improved solution rates from 49% to 83% (McCaffrey, 2012)
5. **[[set-effect]]s** and the **[[einstellung-effect]]** show how prior experience can bias operator selection, sometimes producing failure; the related **[[incubation-effect]]** occurs because stepping away allows inappropriate strategies to be forgotten
6. The **prefrontal cortex** is critical for maintaining goal structures in working memory; patients with prefrontal damage fail specifically at points requiring backward moves (Goel & Grafman, 1995)

## Detailed Notes

### The Nature of Problem Solving
Problem solving has three essential features: (1) **goal directedness** -- behavior organized toward achieving a goal; (2) **subgoal decomposition** -- breaking the overall goal into manageable subgoals; (3) **operator application** -- using actions (operators) that transform problem states. The chapter opens with Kohler's (1927) classic studies of the chimpanzee Sultan, who created a tool by joining two poles to reach bananas -- demonstrating all three features.

### Problem Space and Search
A [[problem-space]] (also called state space) consists of all possible states of a problem plus the operators for transforming one state into another. The **start state** is the initial situation, **intermediate states** are situations on the way to a solution, and the **goal state** is the solved problem. The eight puzzle is used as a primary example: states are tile configurations, operators are tile movements. A **search tree** represents all possible sequences of operator applications from the start state. Newell and Simon (1972) developed this framework, which became the dominant approach in both cognitive psychology and artificial intelligence.

### Acquisition of Operators
Three methods of acquiring new problem-solving operators:
- **Discovery:** Learning by trial and error, as in Thorndike's (1898) puzzle boxes. Two neural systems support this -- the basal ganglia (gradual reinforcement of associations) and the prefrontal cortex (building mental models) (Daw, Niv, & Dayan, 2005)
- **Being told:** Uniquely human, depending on language
- **By example:** Observing someone else use operators. Learning from worked examples is at least as effective as verbal instruction for mathematical concepts (Lee, Fincham, & Anderson, 2015). Brain imaging shows different activity during study (verbal -> occipital; examples -> parietal/frontal) but identical activity during subsequent problem solving

### Analogy and Imitation
**Analogy** is extracting operators from a solved example and mapping them onto a new problem. Rutherford's mapping of the solar system onto atomic structure (Gentner, 1983) is a famous case. Gick and Holyoak (1980) showed that participants could solve Duncker's (1945) radiation problem after reading an analogous military story -- but only when explicitly told to use the analogy. Spontaneous use of analogies is rare and often guided by superficial similarities rather than deep structural parallels (Ross, 1984, 1987). Analogical reasoning is nearly unique to humans and depends on the left anterior prefrontal region (Whitaker, Vendetti, Wendelken, & Bunge, 2018).

### Operator Selection
Three criteria guide operator selection:
1. **Backup avoidance:** Reluctance to undo previous operators, even when necessary
2. **[[Difference-reduction]]** (hill climbing): Selecting the operator that most reduces the difference between current state and goal state. This is myopic -- it considers only whether the next step is an improvement without evaluating the larger plan. The hobbits-and-orcs river-crossing problem shows how difference reduction fails at critical points where the correct move temporarily increases distance from the goal (Jeffries, Polson, Razran, & Atwood, 1977)
3. **[[Means-ends-analysis]]:** A more sophisticated method that creates subgoals to enable blocked operators. The General Problem Solver (GPS) program by Newell and Simon (1972) formalized this: identify the biggest difference from the goal, find an operator to eliminate it, and if that operator is blocked, set a subgoal to remove the block. Applied to the Tower of Hanoi, means-ends analysis produces an optimal 7-move solution for 3 disks. Move time is a function of the number of subgoals that must be created (Anderson, Kushmerick, & Lebiere, 1993; Ruiz, 1987)

### Goal Structures and the Prefrontal Cortex
Complex goal structures with operator subgoals are observed primarily in humans and higher primates. The prefrontal cortex manages these goal structures in working memory. fMRI data show that BOLD response in the right anterior dorsolateral prefrontal cortex varies in synchrony with the number of goals held in working memory during Tower of Hanoi problem solving (Fincham, Carter, van Veen, Stenger, & Anderson, 2002). Patients with severe prefrontal damage show normal IQs but fail specifically at Tower of Hanoi moves requiring "backward" steps -- moves that increase distance from the goal but are necessary for the overall solution (Goel & Grafman, 1995).

### Giving Up on Problem Solving
When to give up depends on: (1) the size of the [[problem-space]] -- participants persist longer with larger problem spaces (more options to explore); (2) beliefs about solvability -- those told 75% of problems are solvable persist longer than those told 25% (Payne & Duggan, 2011). People are often too pessimistic about their ability to solve problems (Lucas & Nordgren, 2015). Premature goal abandonment is worse for those who believe success is innate talent rather than effort (Dweck, 2008), for lower-SES children, and for women in math contexts -- the latter possibly reflecting **stereotype threat** (Spencer, Steele, & Quinn, 1999).

### Problem Representation
How a problem is represented determines which operators are available. The mutilated-checkerboard problem (Kaplan & Simon, 1990) is nearly impossible when represented as covering squares but trivially easy when represented as matching colors (each domino covers one black and one white square). Students who learned physics acceleration problems failed to transfer that knowledge to algebra salary-increase problems with identical structure because they did not represent speed (m/s) and salary (dollars/year) as the same type of quantity (Bassok, 1990; Bassok & Holyoak, 1989).

### Functional Fixedness
**[[Functional-fixedness]]** is the inability to see objects beyond their conventional function. Classic demonstrations:
- **Two-string problem** (Maier, 1931): Only 39% of participants thought to use pliers as a pendulum weight
- **Candle problem** (Duncker, 1945): Participants failed to see a tack box as a platform for a candle; difficulty increased when the box was filled (reinforcing its container function)
- McCaffrey (2012) trained participants to decompose objects into primitive parts and features, improving solution rates on functional-fixedness problems from 49% to 83%

### Set Effects and Einstellung
A **[[set-effect]]** occurs when prior experience biases operator selection. The **[[einstellung-effect]]** (Luchins, 1942) demonstrates this powerfully: after solving five water-jug problems using the B - 2C - A method, 83% of participants continued using this complex method on problems solvable by simpler methods (A + C or A - C), versus less than 1% of control participants. 64% failed problem 8, which could NOT be solved by B - 2C - A, versus only 5% of controls. However, the simple instruction "Don't be blind" reduced the set effect by more than 50%.

### Incubation Effects
**[[Incubation-effect]]s** occur when stepping away from a problem improves subsequent problem-solving success. Silveira (1971) found that with a half-hour break, 64% solved the cheap-necklace problem, versus 55% without a break; with a 4-hour break, 85% solved it. Participants did NOT return with fully formed solutions -- they restarted their approach from scratch. The best explanation: incubation allows inappropriate set effects to dissipate (Smith & Blakenship, 1989, 1991). Participants given misleading hints benefited more from incubation than control participants, and those who had had incubation periods were more likely to have forgotten the misleading hint.

### Insight
An **insight problem** (Metcalfe & Wiebe, 1987) is one where solvers cannot recognize when they are close to a solution. With noninsight problems (like Tower of Hanoi), participants accurately predicted solution proximity 15 seconds before solving; with insight problems (like the cheap-necklace problem), they had no idea they were close even 15 seconds before success. Brain imaging during remote association problems shows increasing prefrontal activity during search, followed by an abrupt drop at the moment of insight, while motor regions show a simultaneous rise as the response is generated (Anderson et al., 2009).
