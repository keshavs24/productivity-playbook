# Contagious Ch.5: Practical Value — Summary

**Author:** Jonah Berger
**Source:** *Contagious: Why Things Catch On* (2013), Chapter 5
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **People share practically valuable information to help others.** Sharing useful content is a modern form of barn raising — it strengthens social bonds and makes the sharer feel good about being helpful. While [[social-currency]] is about how sharing makes the sender look, [[practical-value]] is about how it helps the receiver.
2. **[[prospect-theory]] explains why some deals seem more shareable than others.** People evaluate prices relative to reference points, not in absolute terms. A grill marked down from $350 to $250 (29% off) seems like a better deal than the same grill marked down from $255 to $240 (6% off), even though the absolute saving is similar.
3. **The Rule of 100:** For products under $100, percentage discounts seem larger. For products over $100, absolute dollar discounts seem larger. A $25 shirt at 20% off sounds better than "$5 off." A $2,000 laptop at $200 off sounds better than "10% off."
4. **Narrower, more targeted content may actually be more viral** than broadly relevant content because it reminds people of a specific person who would benefit, creating a stronger urge to share.
5. **False information can spread just as easily as true information** because the drive to share helpful content doesn't include a verification step. The vaccine-autism hoax spread because parents genuinely wanted to help other parents keep their kids safe.

## Detailed Notes

### Ken Craig's Viral Corn Video
Ken Craig is an eighty-six-year-old man from Oklahoma whose video about shucking corn ("Clean Ears Everytime") collected more than 5 million views on YouTube. Unlike most viral videos (made by and for young people), this one skewed to viewers over fifty-five. The video's virality can't be explained by social currency (corn shucking isn't remarkable), triggers (few environmental cues for corn shucking), or emotion (it's a calm, informational video). The answer is simple: **[[practical-value]].** People like to pass along useful information. News others can use.

### Hikers Talking About Vacuum Cleaners
On a hiking trip in the mountains of North Carolina, [[berger]] overheard hikers discussing vacuum cleaners — not the beautiful weather or the rushing waterfall they had just passed. The conversation was driven by practical value: people like to share useful opinions and recommendations that help others make better decisions.

### Sharing Practical Value = Helping Others
People share practically valuable information to help others. Whether saving a friend time, ensuring a colleague saves money, or helping someone make a better choice, useful information helps. Sharing useful things also strengthens social bonds — it's how we show we care. As Berger notes, "If Social Currency is about information senders and how sharing makes them look, Practical Value is mostly about the information receiver."

Sharing practical value is like modern barn raising. In the 18th and 19th centuries, communities would collectively build barns. Today, sharing a useful link or helpful advice serves the same prosocial function.

### The Psychology of Deals — Prospect Theory
[[berger]] uses [[prospect-theory]] (Kahneman and Tversky's Nobel Prize-winning work) to explain why some deals seem more shareable:

**Reference points:** People don't evaluate prices in absolute terms. They evaluate them relative to a comparison standard or reference point. Fifty cents for coffee in New York City seems cheap; in rural India it seems expensive. Reference points explain why older people complain about prices more — they remember when things cost less.

**The barbecue grill experiment:** People were shown two scenarios: (A) a Weber grill originally $350, on sale for $250, or (B) the same grill originally $255, on sale for $240. Even though the price in scenario B was lower, 75% of people in scenario A said they'd buy the grill vs. only 22% in scenario B. The higher reference point ($350) made the $250 price seem like a better deal.

**Infomercial pricing strategy:** "The amazing Miracle Blade knives... you might expect to pay $100 or even $200... but right now you can get this incredible knife set for only $39.99!" By mentioning $100-$200, the infomercial sets a high reference point that makes $39.99 seem like a steal.

**The word "sale" alone increases purchases.** Marketing scientists Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester found that simply labeling items as "Pre-Season SALE" increased sales by more than 50% — even though the prices were identical to the non-sale versions.

**Diminishing sensitivity:** The same $10 savings has a smaller impact the farther it is from the reference point. People will drive twenty minutes to save $10 on a $35 clock radio (29% savings) but not to save $10 on a $650 television (1.5% savings), even though both trips save exactly $10.

### Highlighting Incredible Value
Deals need to cut through the clutter to get shared. Key factors:
- **Exceed expectations.** The deal must seem surprising or surpass what people expect. This connects to [[social-currency]] — the more remarkable the deal, the more it will be discussed.
- **Scarcity and restrictions.** Counterintuitively, making promotions more restrictive can make them more effective. "One per household" or "Limit three per customer" actually increases purchase likelihood by more than 50% — by signaling the deal is so good the store might run out. Quantity purchase limits, time restrictions, and exclusive access all make the promotion seem more valuable.
- **The Rule of 100.** For products priced under $100, frame discounts as percentages (20% off a $25 shirt sounds better than $5 off). For products over $100, frame discounts in dollar terms ($200 off a $2,000 laptop sounds better than 10% off). This leverages people's tendency to anchor on whichever number seems larger.

### More Than Money — Useful Information
Practical value extends beyond deals and discounts. Vanguard's *MoneyWhys* newsletter packages financial expertise into short, useful emails that people forward to friends and colleagues. The practical value makes people pass it along, spreading the word about Vanguard's investment expertise.

Health and education articles were among the most frequently shared on the *New York Times* Most E-Mailed list because they provide useful, actionable information — tips for living longer, getting better education, eating healthier. Practical advice is shareable advice.

**Packaging matters:** Short lists focused around a key topic work better than long, sprawling articles. "Five ways to lose weight" and "Ten dating tips for the New Year" are digestible and shareable.

**Narrow audiences may mean more sharing.** Content that is obviously relevant to a *specific* friend or family member may actually be more viral than broadly relevant content. A piece about Ethiopian restaurants may be shared more vigorously than one about American restaurants because the sharer thinks of the one friend who loves Ethiopian food and feels compelled to send it.

### A Note on Truth
The vaccine-autism hoax (Wakefield's fraudulent 1998 paper) illustrates the dark side of practical value. Parents shared the false information because they genuinely wanted to help other parents protect their kids. The information was practical (seemed useful for child safety) and emotional (fear for children's health). The drive to share helpful things is so powerful it can make even false ideas succeed. **False information can spread just as quickly as the truth.**
