# Influence Ch.6: Authority — Summary

**Author:** Robert B. Cialdini
**Source:** *Influence: Science and Practice*, 5th Edition (2009), Chapter 6
**Phase:** 1 — Cognitive Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Obedience to authority is one of the most powerful forces in human behavior.** [[milgram]]'s experiments demonstrated that roughly two-thirds of ordinary, psychologically healthy adults were willing to deliver what they believed were dangerous, potentially fatal electric shocks to a screaming, pleading victim — simply because a lab-coated researcher told them to continue.
2. **We respond to the *symbols* of authority as much as (or more than) its substance.** Three categories of authority symbols — titles, clothes, and trappings (jewelry, cars, etc.) — can trigger automatic compliance even in the total absence of genuine expertise. The appearance of authority is often enough.
3. **The effect of authority pressure is systematically underestimated.** In every study discussed — Milgram's obedience experiments, the hospital nurses study, the security guard uniform experiment, the prestige automobile study — participants and outside observers predicted far less compliance than actually occurred. Authority influence works forcefully *and* unexpectedly.
4. **Defense against illegitimate authority requires asking two questions:** "Is this authority truly an expert?" (focusing on credentials and relevance, not symbols) and "How truthful can we expect the expert to be?" (evaluating trustworthiness and potential conflicts of interest). The [[sly-sincerity]] tactic specifically exploits the second question.
5. **The deep-seated nature of authority obedience is culturally universal** — Milgram's results were replicated across Holland, Germany, Spain, Italy, Australia, and Jordan (Meeus & Raaijmakers, 1986), and modern replications show no significant difference from the original findings (Burger, in press).

## Detailed Notes

### The Milgram Obedience Experiments

**The Basic Paradigm (Milgram, 1974):**
- Participants responded to a newspaper ad for a "study of memory." Upon arrival at [[milgram]]'s Yale University laboratory, each subject met a second "volunteer" (actually a confederate actor) and a lab-coated researcher
- The subject was assigned the role of "Teacher" and the confederate the "Learner." The Learner was strapped into a chair and fitted with electrodes
- The Teacher's job: test the Learner's memory and deliver escalating electric shocks (increasing by 15 volts per error) for every incorrect answer, up to a maximum of 450 volts
- No real shocks were delivered — the Learner only *pretended* to be shocked

**Results of the basic experiment:**
- At 75, 90, and 105 volts: audible grunts of pain
- At 120 volts: Learner exclaimed shocks were "really starting to hurt"
- At 150 volts: "That's all. Get me out of here. Get me out of here, please. Let me out."
- At 300 volts: refusal to answer, screaming, kicking walls, begging for release
- Beyond 330 volts: total silence (near-paralysis from shock intensity)
- **Two-thirds (approximately 67%) of subjects pulled every one of the 30 shock switches**, continuing all the way to the maximum 450 volts
- Almost none of the 40 subjects stopped when the victim first demanded release, nor when he began begging, nor even when his reaction had become, in Milgram's words, "definitely an agonized scream"

**Pre-study predictions — everyone was wrong:**
- Colleagues, graduate students, and psychology majors at Yale estimated only **1-2%** would go to the maximum shock
- A separate group of **39 psychiatrists** predicted about **one in a thousand** would continue to the end
- No one predicted what actually happened

**Observer account (Milgram, 1963, p. 377):**
> "I observed a mature and initially poised businessman enter the laboratory smiling and confident. Within 20 minutes he was reduced to a twitching, stuttering wreck, who was rapidly approaching a point of nervous collapse. He constantly pulled on his earlobe and twisted his hands. At one point he pushed his fist into his forehead and muttered: 'Oh, God, let's stop it.' And yet he continued to respond to every word of the experimenter and obeyed to the end."

**Evidence that subjects were obeying authority, not acting out aggression:**
- Subjects hated what they were doing — they trembled, perspired, shook, stammered protests, dug fingernails into their own flesh, bit their lips until they bled, fell into fits of uncontrollable nervous laughter
- They implored the researcher to let them stop. When the researcher refused, they went on

### Milgram's Variations — Isolating the Authority Variable

**Switched scripts variation:**
- Researcher told Teacher to stop; Learner insisted Teacher continue
- **Result: 100% of subjects refused to give one additional shock** when it was merely the fellow subject demanding it
- The identical result occurred when the researcher was strapped into the chair and the fellow subject ordered shocks — not one subject touched another lever
- Lesson: it was the *authority* of the researcher, not the victim's demands, that produced obedience

**Two contradictory researchers variation:**
- Two researchers present; one ordered Teacher to stop, the other to continue
- Conflicting authority paralyzed subjects — they frantically tried to determine who was the bigger boss
- "Wait, wait. Which is it going to be? One says stop, one says go. . . . Which is it!?"
- **When both researchers remained at loggerheads, every subject followed his better instincts and ended the shocks**
- This would not be expected if subjects were motivated by sadism or aggression

**Sex of subjects variation:**
- A later experiment showed that subjects' sex was irrelevant — **female Teachers were just as likely** to deliver maximum shocks as male Teachers

**Victim with heart condition variation:**
- Victim announced he had a heart condition and that the shock was affecting his heart: "That's all. Get me out of here. I told you I had heart trouble. My heart's starting to bother me. I refuse to go on. Let me out."
- **Result: 65% still went to maximum shock** — identical to the basic experiment

### The Cross-Cultural Evidence
- Milgram first began his investigations to understand how Germans could have participated in concentration camp atrocities. He had planned to test in the US then move to Germany
- After the first New Haven experiment: "I found so much obedience, I hardly saw the need of taking the experiment to Germany"
- Replications in **Holland, Germany, Spain, Italy, Australia, and Jordan** produced similar results (Meeus & Raaijmakers, 1986, for review)
- A recent replication using several of Milgram's experimental features found **no significant differences** between Milgram's subjects and a modern-day sample (Burger, in press)

### The Story of S. Brian Willson
- **September 1, 1987**, Concord, California: Willson and two other men lay across railroad tracks at the Naval Weapons Station to protest U.S. military shipments to Nicaragua
- They had notified navy and railroad officials three days in advance; protesters were confident the train would stop
- The civilian crew, which had been given orders not to stop, **never even slowed the train** despite seeing the protesters 600 feet ahead
- Two men scrambled clear; Willson had **both legs severed below the knee**
- Navy medical corpsmen at the scene **refused to treat him** or allow him to be taken in their ambulance — his wife and son had to staunch the bleeding for 45 minutes until a private ambulance arrived
- Willson, a Vietnam veteran, blamed neither crewmen nor corpsmen: "They were just doing what I did in 'Nam. They were following orders that are part of an insane policy. They're the fall guys."
- Remarkably, the **train crew filed suit against Willson** for "humiliation, mental anguish, and physical stress" because he hadn't allowed them to carry out their orders without cutting off his legs

### The Allures and Dangers of Blind Obedience

**Why obedience to authority exists:**
- A multilayered system of authority confers an immense advantage upon a society — enabling development of sophisticated structures for production, trade, defense, expansion, and social control
- The alternative (anarchy) would render life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" (Thomas Hobbes)
- We are trained from birth that obedience to proper authority is right and disobedience is wrong — through parental lessons, schoolhouse rhymes, stories, songs, and later through legal, military, and political systems
- Religious instruction reinforces obedience — from Adam and Eve's punishment for defying God to Abraham's willingness to sacrifice his son because God ordered it

**The click, whirr nature of authority obedience:**
- We rarely agonize over the pros and cons of authority demands — obedience frequently takes place in a [[click-whirr]] fashion with little or no conscious deliberation
- Conforming to authority figures has genuine practical advantages: parents, teachers, employers, judges, and government leaders typically have greater knowledge, wisdom, and power
- This paradox attends all major weapons of influence: what is normally adaptive becomes exploitable precisely *because* it is so automatic

### Authority Obedience in Medicine — The "Rectal Earache"

**The Cohen & Davis "rectal earache" case:**
- Temple University professors Michael Cohen and Neil Davis, in their book *Medication Errors: Causes and Prevention*, documented mindless deference to physicians
- A physician ordered ear drops for a patient's right ear, abbreviated on the prescription as "place in R ear"
- The duty nurse **put the required number of ear drops into the patient's anus**
- Neither patient nor nurse questioned it — illustrating that when a legitimate authority has spoken, "what would otherwise make sense is irrelevant"

**The Institute of Medicine statistic:**
- Hospitalized patients can expect to experience **at least one medication error per day** (Szabo, 2007)

### The Hofling Hospital Experiment (Hofling, Brotzman, Dalrymple, Graves, & Pierce, 1966)

- Researchers called 22 separate nurses' stations on surgical, medical, pediatric, and psychiatric wards
- A caller identified himself as a hospital physician and directed the nurse to give 20 milligrams of a drug (Astrogen) to a specific ward patient
- **Four excellent reasons for the nurse to refuse:**
  1. The prescription was transmitted by phone, violating hospital policy
  2. The medication itself was unauthorized — not cleared for use or on the ward stock list
  3. The prescribed dosage was **obviously and dangerously excessive** — medication containers clearly stated "maximum daily dose" was only 10 mg (half of what was ordered)
  4. The directive came from a man the nurse had never met, seen, or even talked with before
- **Result: 95% of regular staff nurses went straight to the ward medicine cabinet** and secured the ordered dosage, heading for the patient's room to administer it — stopped only by a secret observer who revealed the experiment
- **Self-prediction failure:** A separate group of 33 nurses and student nurses, asked what they *would* have done, predicted near-universal refusal — only 2 said they would have given the medication as ordered
- Researchers' conclusion: "In a real-life situation corresponding to the experimental one, there would, in theory, be two professional intelligences, the doctor's and the nurse's, working to ensure that a given procedure be undertaken in a manner beneficial to the patient or, at the very least, not detrimental to him [or her]. The experiment strongly suggests, however, that one of these intelligences is, for all practical purposes, nonfunctioning" (Hofling et al., 1966, p. 176)

### Who's Really the King? — Voice Style Convergence
- Communication researchers found that people unconsciously shift their voice and speech styles toward those of individuals in positions of power and authority (Gregory & Webster, 1996)
- On *Larry King Live*: When King interviewed high-status guests (Bill Clinton, George Bush, Barbara Streisand), his voice style changed to match theirs. When he interviewed lower-status guests (Dan Quayle, Spike Lee, Julie Andrews), he remained unmoved and their voice styles shifted to match his

### Connotation Not Content — The Symbols of Authority

Cialdini identifies three categories of [[authority-symbols]] that trigger compliance without genuine authority:

#### 1. Titles

- Titles are simultaneously the most difficult and the easiest symbols of authority to acquire — earning one takes years, but anyone can simply adopt the label
- Actors in TV commercials and con artists successfully use titles for automatic deference

**The professor who hides his title:**
- A faculty friend who travels frequently has learned to never use his title of professor in social conversations. When he does, "the tenor of the interaction changes immediately. People who have been spontaneous and interesting conversation partners for the previous half hour become respectful, accepting, and dull." He now regularly lies about his occupation.

**Titles alter perceived physical size (P. R. Wilson, 1968):**
- In an experiment at five classes of Australian college students, a man from Cambridge University was introduced with varying status levels: student, demonstrator, lecturer, senior lecturer, professor
- After he left, each class estimated his height
- **With each increase in status, perceived height grew by an average of half an inch** — the "professor" was seen as **2.5 inches taller** than the "student"

**Post-election height distortion:**
- After winning an election, politicians become taller in the eyes of the citizenry (Higham & Carment, 1992)

**The size-status connection:**
- Animals assigned status by dominance use size-enhancing tricks (arching backs, bristling coats, extending fins, unfurling wings) to avoid actual combat
- Since 1900, the U.S. presidency has been won by the taller of the major party candidates in **nearly 90%** of elections
- Tall men earn more and are more likely to rise to leadership positions (Chaiken, 1986; Judge & Cable, 2004)
- Con artists commonly wear lifts in their shoes

#### Reader's Report 6.1 — The "Furmano" Tomatoes
- A Texas professor noticed that nearly all cans of "Furmano Italian diced tomatoes" were sold out at his local supermarket, while the shelf of "Furman brand diced tomatoes" was full
- The company had simply added an "o" to its name when distributing some products — the Italian-sounding name lent authority to an Italian food product
- Author's note: the added "o" also triggered the [[liking-principle]] by making the company appear similar to its Italian-American customers

#### 2. Clothes

**Bickman's uniform studies (1974):**
- A young man asked passersby on the street to comply with odd requests (pick up a discarded paper bag, stand on the other side of a bus-stop sign)
- Half the time in ordinary street clothes; half the time in a security guard's uniform
- **Many more people obeyed the uniformed requester** regardless of request type. Same results when the uniformed requester was female (Bushman, 1988)

**The parking meter variation:**
- Requester stopped pedestrians and pointed to a man at a parking meter 50 feet away
- Same script whether in uniform or street clothes: "You see that guy over there by the meter? He's overparked but doesn't have any change. Give him a dime!"
- Requester then turned a corner and walked away so he was out of sight when the pedestrian reached the meter
- **Nearly all pedestrians complied when requester wore the guard costume; fewer than half when dressed normally**
- The power of the uniform lasted even after the authority figure had left the scene

**Self-prediction failure on uniforms:**
- College students accurately guessed compliance rates for street clothes (50% vs. actual 42%)
- But they **grossly underestimated** the uniform's power — predicting 63% compliance vs. the actual **92%** (Bickman, 1974)

**The business suit and jaywalking (Lefkowitz, Blake, & Mouton, 1955):**
- A 31-year-old man crossed against the traffic light on multiple occasions in Texas
- Half the time: freshly pressed business suit and tie; other half: work shirt and trousers
- Researchers counted how many pedestrians followed him across the street
- **3.5 times as many people followed the suited jaywalker** into traffic

**The bank examiner scheme:**
- Con artists combine the guard uniform and business suit in a classic fraud
- A man in a conservative three-piece business suit (starched white shirt, wingtip shoes, muted tones of business blue/gray/black) appears at a widow's door
- Claims to be a professional bank examiner who needs her cooperation to catch a corrupt bank officer
- She withdraws all her money; a "bank guard" in uniform arrives to "return" it to the vault
- Both the "examiner" and "guard" are fraudsters — the victim loses everything

**Reader's Report 6.2 — From a Florida-Based Physician:**
- The title "MD" carries significantly more authority when placed in the visual context of a white coat
- Physicians are highly aware of this hierarchy — medical students get the shortest white coats, residents get medium length, attending physicians get the longest
- In hospitals where nurses know this hierarchy, they rarely question "long coats" but openly challenge and sometimes treat "short coats" rudely

**Police officers in uniform vs. civilian clothes:**
- Police officers in traditional uniforms were rated as more fair, helpful, intelligent, honest, and good compared to when wearing blazers and slacks (Mauro, 1984)

#### 3. Trappings

**The prestige automobile study (Doob & Gross, 1968):**
- San Francisco Bay area: a car was stopped at a green traffic light, and researchers measured how long following motorists waited before honking
- When the stopped car was a **new, luxury model**: 50% of motorists **waited respectfully** without honking until it moved on its own
- When it was an **older, economy model**: Nearly all motorists sounded their horns, the majority more than once; **two simply rammed into the rear bumper**

**Self-prediction failure on trappings:**
- College students predicted they would honk *faster* at the luxury car than the economy car
- The study showed the exact opposite — consistent with the pattern that people grossly underestimate the effect of authority pressure on their behavior

### Advertisers Exploiting the Authority Principle

**Robert Young / Sanka coffee commercial:**
- Actor Robert Young (who played Marcus Welby, M.D. on television) was hired to warn against caffeine and recommend caffeine-free Sanka brand coffee
- The commercial was **highly successful**, running for years in several versions
- Young had no medical credentials — his "authority" was entirely the fictional role of a TV doctor
- The advertising agency knew the *appearance* of authority was sufficient

**MasterCard "Family Holiday Traditions" promotion:**
- Women could chat online with "authorities" on homemaking for the holidays
- The "experts" were actresses Florence Henderson and Jane Kaczmarek, whose credentials were solely their roles as "TV Moms" on *The Brady Bunch* and *Malcolm in the Middle*

**Martin Sheen / West Wing (footnote 3):**
- In a January 24, 2001 TV interview, host Brian Williams pursued a line of questions about presidential decisions with actor Martin Sheen, whose relevant experience was limited to playing a U.S. president on *The West Wing*

### Defense Against Authority Pressure

**Strategy 1: Heightened awareness**
- Remove the element of surprise by recognizing the profound impact of authority (and its symbols)
- Recognize how easily authority symbols can be faked

**Strategy 2: Ask two diagnostic questions:**

**Question 1: "Is this authority truly an expert?"**
- Focuses attention on *credentials* and their *relevance* to the topic at hand
- The Robert Young Sanka example: his credentials were as an actor, not a doctor — yet in [[click-whirr]] mode, we respond to the *title* ("M.D.") regardless
- The Texas jaywalking pedestrians followed a business-suited man across the street — even if he *were* a business authority, he had no expertise in crossing streets safely
- Forces the crucial distinction between **relevant** and **irrelevant** authorities

**Question 2: "How truthful can we expect the expert to be?"**
- Even legitimate experts may not present information honestly if they have something to gain
- We are more swayed by experts who seem impartial than by those who have something to gain (Eagly, Wood, & Chaiken, 1978)
- This holds true around the world (McGuinnies & Ward, 1980) and in children as young as second-graders (Mills & Keil, 2005)
- Even knowledgeable authorities in a field will not persuade us until we are satisfied that their messages represent the facts faithfully (Van Overwalle & Heylighen, 2006)

### Sly Sincerity — The Trust-Building Tactic

[[sly-sincerity]] is a compliance professional's strategy for appearing trustworthy:
- They argue somewhat against their own interests — mention a small shortcoming in their position or product
- The drawback is always a secondary one, easily overcome by more significant advantages
- Examples:
  - Avis: "We're number two, but we try harder"
  - L'Oreal: "We're more expensive but you're worth it"
- By establishing truthfulness on minor issues, they become more believable on the important aspects of their argument (Hunt, Domzal, & Kernan, 1981; Settle & Gorden, 1974; Ward & Brenner, 2006)

**Letters of recommendation (Knouse, 1983):**
- Letters sent to personnel directors of major corporations produced the **most favorable results** for job candidates when the letters contained **one unflattering comment** about the candidate in an otherwise wholly positive set of remarks

**Reader's Report 6.3 — From a Former Fortune 500 CEO:**
- A business school instructor teaches aspiring CEOs the practice of acknowledging failure as a way to advance one's career
- A former student made his role in a dot-com company failure a prominent part of his resume — detailing what he learned. Before, burying the failure generated no career success; since highlighting it, he has been selected for multiple prestigious positions
- Author's note: Research shows that companies that take blame for poor outcomes in annual reports have **higher stock prices one year later** than companies that don't take blame (Lee, Peterson, & Tiedens, 2004)

### The Waiter Vincent — A Masterclass in Sly Sincerity

Cialdini applied for a busboy position at expensive restaurants to observe compliance professionals firsthand. The most successful waiter was **Vincent**, whose weekly earnings far exceeded all other servers.

**Vincent's technique — tailored approaches by customer type:**
- Families: effervescent, slightly clownish, directing remarks to children as often as adults
- Young couple on a date: formal and slightly imperious, speaking exclusively to the young man to intimidate him into ordering lavishly
- Older married couple: formal but respectful to both members
- Solo diner: friendly, cordial, conversational, warm

**Vincent's signature move — arguing against his own interest (for large parties of 8-12):**
- When the first person (usually a woman) ordered, Vincent's brow furrowed, his hand hovered above his order pad
- After looking over his shoulder for the manager, he leaned conspiratorially toward the table: "I'm afraid that is not as good tonight as it normally is. Might I recommend instead the ___ or the ___?"
- He suggested items that were **slightly less expensive** than the patron's original selection
- This single maneuver accomplished multiple things simultaneously:
  1. **[[reciprocation]]**: Everyone felt he had done them a favor, generating gratitude and obligation toward his tip
  2. **Authority**: He established himself as an expert on the house's current offerings
  3. **Trustworthiness via [[sly-sincerity]]**: By recommending dishes that were *less* expensive, he proved he had the customers' best interests at heart, not his own profit
  4. **Position for upselling**: When the party finished ordering food, Vincent would say, "Very well, and would you like me to suggest or select wine to go with your meals?" — the response was near-universal assent
- Customers' reaction: "Sure. You know what's good here, and you're obviously on our side. Tell us what to get."
- Vincent similarly persuaded patrons who would have skipped dessert to order baked Alaska and chocolate mousse
- By combining reciprocity and credible authority in a single elegant maneuver, Vincent inflated **both** the percentage of his tip and the base charge on which it was figured

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The chapter reveals a tension between the **adaptive value** of authority obedience (it usually leads to good outcomes because authorities typically possess superior knowledge) and its **exploitability** (the same automatic response makes us vulnerable to fake authority). Cialdini does not resolve this tension so much as propose two diagnostic questions as a practical defense.
- There is an implicit tension with the [[fundamental-attribution-error]]: people consistently attribute obedience behavior to dispositional traits (sadism, weakness) when situational authority pressure is the actual cause. This parallels the bystander effect misattribution discussed in the [[social-proof]] chapter.
