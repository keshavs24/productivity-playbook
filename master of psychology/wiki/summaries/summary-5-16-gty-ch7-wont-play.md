# Getting to Yes Ch.7: What If They Won't Play? — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 7
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **There are three basic approaches for focusing the other side's attention on the merits.** First, you yourself can concentrate on the merits (principled negotiation). Second, you can use [[negotiation-jujitsu]] — a strategy that counters the basic moves of positional bargaining by deflecting their attacks onto the problem. Third, you can bring in a third party who uses the [[one-text-procedure]].
2. **Negotiation jujitsu: don't push back.** When they assert their position, don't reject it — look behind it for interests. When they attack your ideas, don't defend them — invite criticism and advice. When they attack you personally, don't counterattack — recast the attack on you as an attack on the problem.
3. **The one-text procedure is the most effective third-party tool.** A mediator prepares a single draft, asks each side for criticism (not approval), and revises iteratively until no further improvement can be made. Each side has only one decision: yes or no. Camp David used this procedure — 23 drafts over 13 days.
4. **The Jones Realty case study is the chapter's centerpiece.** Frank Turnbull demonstrates principled negotiation and negotiation jujitsu against a hostile landlord, using stock phrases like "Please correct me if I'm wrong," "Our concern is fairness," and "Let me get back to you."

## Detailed Notes

### Negotiation Jujitsu

If pushing back does not work, how can you prevent the cycle of action and reaction? **Do not push back.** When they assert their positions, do not reject them. When they attack your ideas, don't defend them. When they attack you, don't counterattack. Break the vicious cycle by refusing to react. As in the Oriental martial arts of judo and jujitsu, avoid pitting your strength against theirs directly; instead, use your skill to step aside and turn their strength to your ends.

Their "attack" will consist of three maneuvers: asserting their position forcefully, attacking your ideas, and attacking you. Here is how to handle each:

**Don't attack their position, look behind it.** When the other side sets forth their position, neither reject it nor accept it. Treat it as one possible option. Look for the interests behind it, and think about ways to improve it. Ask questions: "What exactly are the budget trade-offs involved?" "Why do you feel a need to maintain complete control over layoffs?"

**Assume every position they take is a genuine attempt to address the basic concerns of each side.** Ask them how they think it addresses the problem at hand. Treat their position as one option and objectively examine the extent to which it meets the interests of each party.

**Don't defend your ideas, invite criticism and advice.** Instead of asking them to accept or reject an idea, ask them what's wrong with it. "What concerns of yours would this salary proposal fail to take into account?" Examine their negative judgments to find out their underlying interests. Reword your ideas in light of what you learn from them, and thus turn criticism from an obstacle into an essential ingredient of the process.

**Recast an attack on you as an attack on the problem.** When the other side attacks you personally — as frequently happens — resist the temptation to defend yourself or to attack them. Instead, sit back and allow them to let off steam. Listen to them, show you understand what they are saying, and when they have finished, recast their attack on you as an attack on the problem. "When you say that a strike shows we don't care about the children, I hear your concern about the children's education."

**Ask questions and pause.** Those engaged in negotiation jujitsu use two key tools. The first is to use questions instead of statements. Statements generate resistance; questions generate answers. Questions allow the other side to get their points across and let you understand them. Questions do not criticize, they educate. Silence, too, is one of your best weapons. If you have asked an honest question, wait. People tend to feel uncomfortable with silence.

### Consider the One-Text Procedure

**When a third party is needed.** If neither principled negotiation nor negotiation jujitsu gets them to play, the third approach focuses on what a *third party* can do.

**The house-building example.** A husband wants a two-story house with a chimney and a bay window. The wife wants a modern ranch-style house with a den. In positional bargaining, each asks the other "What are your views on the living room?" and "Do you really insist on having it your way?" Two separate plans become more and more detailed, each more firmly dug in.

**How the one-text procedure works.** An independent architect:
1. Asks about interests (not positions): "Is it for morning sun or afternoon sun?" "What things do you need to store?" "What do you expect to do in your den?"
2. Develops a list of interests and needs of both
3. Prepares a first sketch — asks each spouse to criticize it (not approve it)
4. Revises based on criticism — "Personally, I am dissatisfied with it, but before working on it further I thought I would get your criticisms"
5. Goes through a third plan, a fourth, a fifth
6. Presents the final plan: "This is the best I can do. Here it is. I recommend you accept this plan."
7. Each spouse now has only one decision: yes or no

**Camp David was the most famous use.** The United States listened to both sides, prepared a draft to which no one was committed, asked for criticism, and improved the draft again and again until the mediators felt they could improve it no further. After thirteen days and some twenty-three drafts, the United States had a text. President Carter did recommend it, Israel and Egypt accepted. As a mechanical technique for limiting decisions, reducing uncertainty, and preventing parties from getting increasingly locked into positions, it worked remarkably well.

**The one-text procedure is a great help for two-party negotiations involving a mediator.** It is almost essential for large multilateral negotiations — 150 nations cannot constructively discuss hundreds of proposals. Nor can they make concessions contingent upon mutual concessions by everybody else.

### The Jones Realty Case Study

Frank Turnbull rented an apartment from Jones Realty for $300/month. The legal maximum rent was $233 — $67 less than he had been paying. Through several negotiating sessions using principled negotiation, Turnbull got Mrs. Jones to agree to reimburse him.

Key stock phrases used as [[negotiation-jujitsu]] moves:
- **"Please correct me if I'm wrong"** — presenting facts as questions, not accusations
- **"We appreciate what you've done for us"** — separating people from problem, personal support
- **"Our concern is fairness"** — standing on principle, not selfish interest
- **"Trust is a separate issue"** — deflecting manipulation attempts
- **"Could I ask you a few questions to see whether my facts are right?"** — using questions instead of statements
- **"What's the principle behind your action?"** — questioning reasons, not attacking positions
- **"Let me see if I understand what you're saying"** — active listening, demonstrating understanding
- **"Let me get back to you"** — avoiding premature decisions under pressure
- **"Let me show you where I have trouble following some of your reasoning"** — presenting reasons before proposals
- **"One fair solution might be..."** — not *his* proposal but *a* fair option
- **"If we agree... If we disagree..."** — making the alternative to agreement clear
- **"We'd be happy to see if we can leave when it's most convenient for you"** — incorporating her interests

## New Concepts Introduced
- [[negotiation-jujitsu]] — deflecting attacks onto the problem rather than pushing back
- [[one-text-procedure]] — single negotiating text iterated through criticism

## Contradictions or Tensions
- Negotiation jujitsu is essentially a set of [[framing-effects]] techniques — reframing attacks as problem descriptions, positions as one option among many
- The one-text procedure circumvents [[reactive-devaluation]] by having proposals come from a neutral third party rather than from the opposing side
- The emphasis on questions over statements connects to research on [[cognitive-barriers-to-resolution]] — statements trigger defensive reactions; questions engage analytical thinking
