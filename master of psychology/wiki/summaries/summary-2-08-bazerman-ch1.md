# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.1 -- Introduction to Managerial Decision Making

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Edition)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. The rational decision-making model prescribes six steps (define problem, identify criteria, weigh criteria, generate alternatives, rate alternatives, compute optimal decision) but people rarely follow them optimally
2. [[system-1-system-2]] thinking (Stanovich & West, 2000; Kahneman, 2003) distinguishes fast/automatic/intuitive System 1 from slow/deliberate/logical System 2; most managerial decisions rely on System 1 even when System 2 would be more appropriate
3. Herbert Simon's [[bounded-rationality]] concept established that human judgment is bounded -- we satisfice rather than optimize because of cognitive, time, and information constraints
4. Richard Thaler extended Simon's framework to identify bounded willpower (favoring present over future) and bounded self-interest (caring about others' outcomes)
5. Bazerman and Chugh (2005) added [[bounded-awareness]] -- the tendency to overlook obvious, important, and readily available information
6. Four general heuristics structure the book: [[availability]], [[representativeness]], the [[confirmation-heuristic]], and the affect heuristic

## Detailed Notes

### The Rational Model
The prescriptive model of rational decision making involves six steps: (1) define the problem, (2) identify criteria, (3) weigh criteria, (4) generate alternatives, (5) rate each alternative on each criterion, (6) compute the optimal decision by multiplying ratings by weights and choosing the highest sum. This model assumes perfect problem definition, complete criteria identification, accurate weighting, knowledge of all alternatives, accurate assessment, and accurate calculation. People rarely achieve any of these.

### System 1 and System 2
Stanovich and West (2000) coined the distinction. System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless, implicit, emotional. System 2 is slower, conscious, effortful, explicit, logical. Most life decisions use System 1, and this is often sufficient (e.g., grocery shopping). But System 2 should govern our most important decisions. Managers are more likely to rely on System 1 when busier, more rushed, and with more on their minds (Chugh, 2004). Key insight: System 2 does not fully override System 1 -- people have trouble overcoming the impulse to avoid eating from a container labeled "cyanide" even when they wrote the label themselves (Rozin, Markwith, & Ross, 1990). The Shepard (1990) table illusion demonstrates how System 1 visual processing can fail: two tables with identical parallelogram tops appear different shapes.

### Bounded Rationality
Herbert Simon (March & Simon, 1958; Simon, 1957) introduced the concept: individual judgment is bounded in its rationality. We satisfice -- searching until we find a satisfactory solution rather than examining all possible alternatives to find the best. Time, cost, memory, intelligence, and perceptual limitations constrain our ability to calculate optimally from the universe of available alternatives.

### Beyond Bounded Rationality
Thaler (2000) added two dimensions: (1) bounded willpower -- giving greater weight to present concerns over future, leading to failures like inadequate retirement saving; (2) bounded self-interest -- caring about others' outcomes, unlike the stereotypic economic actor. Bazerman and Chugh (2005) added bounded awareness -- the tendency to overlook obvious and important information. The book develops a systematic structure: bounded rationality, bounded willpower, bounded self-interest, bounded awareness, and bounded ethicality.

### Introduction to Heuristics
Tversky and Kahneman (1974) continued Simon's work by filling in the specific systematic biases. People rely on simplifying strategies (heuristics/rules of thumb). Heuristics are helpful but their misuse leads to severe errors. When managers become aware of the adverse impact, they can decide when and where to use them. Four general heuristics: (1) availability, (2) representativeness, (3) confirmation heuristic, (4) affect heuristic.

### The Availability Heuristic
People assess frequency/probability by ease of recall. Events that evoke emotions and are vivid, easily imagined, or specific are more available. A subordinate near the manager's office gets a more critical performance review because errors are more available. Peter Lynch (Fidelity Magellan Fund) advocated buying stock in firms unavailable to most investors' minds -- the more available, the more overvalued.

### The Representativeness Heuristic
People judge probability by similarity to stereotypes. Managers predict performance based on representativeness to "the best salesperson" prototype. Venture capitalists predict success based on similarity to past successful ventures. The germ theory of disease was slow to be accepted because the idea of invisible organisms causing plagues was not "representative." The heuristic can also work unconsciously, producing racial discrimination.

### The Confirmation Heuristic
People intuitively use selective data -- searching for instances where the variable of interest is present (Klayman & Ha, 1987). They assume statements are true in the absence of contrary evidence (Gilbert, 1991), search for and interpret evidence confirming prior beliefs (Nickerson, 1998), and trigger hindsight bias by positive hypothesis testing.

### The Affect Heuristic
Most judgments follow an affective evaluation before higher-level reasoning (Kahneman, 2003; Slovic, Finucane, Peters, & MacGregor, 2002). The affect heuristic is a manifestation of System 1 thinking, stronger when people are busy or under time constraints. Stock prices go up on sunny days. Juries decide penalties based on outrage rather than logical harm assessment (Kahneman, Schkade, & Sunstein, 1998).

### Lewin's Change Model
Kurt Lewin (1947) proposed that for lasting change, individuals must (1) unfreeze existing processes, (2) provide information to promote change, and (3) refreeze new processes. The book aims to unfreeze by demonstrating systematic deviations from rationality.
