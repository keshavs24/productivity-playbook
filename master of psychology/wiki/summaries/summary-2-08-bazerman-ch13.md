# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.12 — Improving Decision Making

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.12, pp.206-230)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Seven complementary strategies can correct systematic deficiencies in decision making: (1) use decision-analysis tools, (2) acquire expertise, (3) debias your judgment, (4) reason analogically, (5) take an outsider's view, (6) understand biases in others, and (7) nudge people toward wiser and more ethical decisions
2. The first three strategies aim to create broad change in intuitive responses to decision-making situations by heightening awareness of cognitive limitations and susceptibility to bias; the last four provide techniques for improving specific decisions in specific contexts
3. Linear models consistently outperform expert intuition across domains (admissions, hiring, medical diagnosis, investment) because they integrate information consistently and avoid the random noise in human judgment
4. Experience alone does not eliminate biases -- Tversky and Kahneman (1986) argue that basic judgmental biases are unlikely to self-correct because feedback is delayed, noisy, and rarely counterfactual; true expertise requires deliberate conceptual learning, not passive experience
5. Debiasing follows Lewin's three-stage change model: unfreezing (accepting that your judgment is flawed), change (learning specific bias-correction techniques like "consider the opposite"), and refreezing (internalizing new strategies through repeated practice and periodic self-audits)

## Detailed Notes

### The Moneyball Opening
The chapter opens with Billy Beane and the Oakland Athletics (1999-2002), who achieved the second-best record in Major League Baseball with one of the two smallest payrolls. Their success came from replacing biased expert intuition with statistical analysis. Baseball executives were guilty of three mistakes: (1) overgeneralizing from personal experience, (2) overweighting recent performance, and (3) trusting what they saw with their own eyes over multiyear statistical records. Thaler and Sunstein (2003) compare this to the broader tendency of personnel managers to base hiring on "gut" reactions to interviews rather than hard data.

### Strategy 1: Use Decision-Analysis Tools
**Core idea:** When decision quality really matters, rely on formal procedures rather than intuition. Decision analysis uses expected-value logic -- multiply an option's value by its probability to compute expected value.

**Linear models** are formulas that weight and add relevant predictor variables. They consistently outperform expert judgment across domains because:
- People are good at **selecting** and **coding** information but poor at **integrating** it (Dawes, 1979)
- Human judgment is inconsistent -- affected by mood, deadlines, random fluctuations -- while a linear model always produces the same output from the same inputs
- Experts are subject to biases triggered by specific cases; linear models use only empirically validated data

**Key evidence:**
- Einhorn (1972): Physicians coding biopsies of Hodgkin's disease made accurate individual ratings but could not predict survival time; a multiple regression model using their own selected variables could
- Dawes (1971): A linear model for graduate admissions using GRE scores, GPA, and school quality could rule out 55% of the applicant pool without rejecting anyone the committee accepted, and was better than the committee at predicting future faculty ratings
- Employment interviews predict only about 14% of variability in job performance (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998), yet managers resist replacing them due to a "perfect storm" of [[availability]], [[representativeness]], and [[confirmation-heuristic]] biases

**Resistance to linear models** comes from: (1) ethical objections ("reducing people to numbers"), (2) the belief that intuition and "gut feelings" capture information models cannot, and (3) organizational inertia (what will people do when computers make the decisions?). In practice, people play a crucial role in choosing variables, setting weights, and monitoring model performance.

**Applications to admissions and hiring:**
- Graduate schools with lenient grading practices produce inflated GPAs that admissions committees mistake for high performance -- the [[fundamental-attribution-error]] (correspondence bias) at work; a linear model easily corrects for grading differences
- Structured interviews (same questions, same order, same interviewers) outperform unstructured ones at lower cost

### Strategy 2: Acquire Expertise
**Core question:** Does experience eliminate biases?

**The pessimistic view:** Tversky and Kahneman (1986) argue that learning requires accurate and immediate feedback, which is rarely available because: (i) outcomes are delayed and hard to attribute, (ii) environmental variability degrades feedback, (iii) counterfactual information is absent, and (iv) most important decisions are unique. Even when accurate feedback is available, we anchor to current states and misremember our prior predictions (Meyvis, Ratner, & Levav, 2010).

**The Acquiring a Company experiment:** Ball, Bazerman, and Carroll (1991) gave MBA participants 20 trials with full feedback and real money at stake. Across all 20 trials, there was no obvious learning trend -- only 5 of 72 participants learned. Bereby-Meyer and Grosskopf (2008) found that even hundreds of trials do not solve it.

**Experience vs. expertise distinction (Neale & Northcraft, 1989):** Experience is merely repeated feedback. Expertise requires a "strategic conceptualization" of rational decision-making -- understanding the biases that limit rationality. Northcraft and Neale (1987) showed real-estate agents were just as biased by [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] as undergraduate students, yet denied it.

**The key lesson:** Expertise requires much more than unclear, uncontrollable, and often delayed feedback. It demands constant monitoring and awareness of decision-making processes. Experience without expertise is not transferable and can even be dangerous in new contexts.

**Confucius quoted:** "By three methods we may learn wisdom: First, by reflection, which is noblest; Second, by imitation, which is easiest; and third, by experience, which is the bitterest."

### Strategy 3: Debias Your Judgment
**Fischhoff (1982) four debiasing steps:** (1) offer warnings about the possibility of bias, (2) describe the direction of the bias, (3) provide a dose of feedback, (4) offer extended training with feedback, coaching, and whatever else it takes.

**Larrick (2004) review:** Most successful [[debiasing-strategies]] tend to be context- and bias-specific; training and testing must be closely linked and occur in close time proximity. "Consider the opposite" reduces [[overconfidence-in-calibration]], [[confirmation-heuristic]], and [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] effects. Groups, training in statistical reasoning, and accountability (Lerner & Tetlock, 1999) also help.

**The three-stage Lewinian model applied to debiasing:**

1. **Unfreezing** -- Breaking through resistance to change. Three forces resist unfreezing: (a) intuitive strategies have been relied on for years, (b) successful people have received positive reinforcement for past decisions, (c) balance theory (Heider, 1958) makes "there is something wrong with my decision-making" clash with "I am a successful professional." The book's quiz-and-feedback format is designed specifically to unfreeze.

2. **Change** -- Three critical steps: (a) clarify the specific deficiency, (b) explain the roots of the bias (the underlying heuristic), (c) reassure that these deficiencies are universal and do not imply poor decision-making character. The most general-purpose technique is **"consider the opposite"** (Lord, Lepper, & Preston, 1984): play devil's advocate with yourself and think about why your tentative conclusion could be wrong. Baron (1994) offers two specific sub-strategies: (i) ask "how likely is a yes answer if my hypothesis is false?" and (ii) think of alternative hypotheses and choose the test most likely to distinguish them.

3. **Refreezing** -- New strategies must develop into intuitive habits through repeated application. Schedule routine "checkups" to evaluate recent important decisions. Without frequent reinforcement, old biases return.

### Strategy 4: Reason Analogically
**Core finding:** People learn far more from cases, simulations, and real-world experiences when they abstract the underlying principle rather than focusing on surface features.

**Thompson, Gentner, Loewenstein, & Thompson research:** Participants who compared two analogous problems (e.g., learning to overcome the [[preference-reversal]] of negotiation following a house purchase and a workplace negotiation) created more generalizable insight than those who analyzed a single case. When you compare, you focus on structural similarities and form an abstract schema that transfers to new contexts.

**Idson et al. (2004):** Training based on differences (not just similarities) between related problems can reduce bias even in the notoriously resistant "Acquiring a Company" problem. Comparing the Monty Hall problem's two versions and the Dividing a Pie problem's two versions as pairs helped participants generalize the importance of focusing on the other party's decisions and the rules of the game.

**Moran, Bereby-Meyer, and Bazerman (2008):** More diverse analogical training -- comparing two cases that illustrate different value-creating strategies (e.g., logrolling and compatibility) -- fosters greater learning of general principles than specific training on a single strategy. However, if training becomes too diverse, the applicability of the message may be lost.

### Strategy 5: Take an Outsider's View
**Kahneman and Lovallo (1993) insider/outsider distinction:**
- The **insider** is the biased decision maker who views each situation as unique
- The **outsider** generalizes across situations and identifies patterns

The insider view leads to systematic [[overestimation]] because optimism and [[overconfidence-in-calibration]] combine with the human tendency to view each decision as unique, overlooking historical base rates.

**Key examples:**
- Kahneman's own curriculum team estimated 18-30 months; a team member revealed that 40% of comparable projects were never completed and none finished in less than seven years. The project took eight years.
- Cooper, Woo, and Dunkelberg (1988): 80%+ of entrepreneurs perceived their success probability at 70% or better, one-third considered success certain. Meanwhile, the five-year survival rate for new businesses is only about 33%.
- People predict their friends' home renovations will go 20-50% over budget (outsider view) while believing their own project will finish on time (insider view).

**Practical advice:** For decisions that really matter, ask friends or colleagues with relevant experience for their honest estimate. Or imagine the decision is a friend's and ask what advice you would give. The key is to give the outsider a stronger voice.

### Strategy 6: Understand Biases in Others
**Core shift:** Auditing others' decisions, not just your own. The same biases operate in everyone, but detecting them in others requires a different analytical stance.

**Site-location analyst example:** A retail chain's analyst predicts $3.8M in annual sales for a new store. The mean of all stores is $3M. The director should adjust the prediction toward the mean, proportionally based on the analyst's historical accuracy (the correlation between her predictions and actual sales). If correlation = 0.5, the adjusted estimate is $3.4M. If correlation = 0, the best estimate is simply the mean ($3M).

**Kahneman and Tversky (1982) five-step corrective process:**
1. Select a comparison group
2. Assess the distribution of the comparison group
3. Incorporate intuitive estimation (identify the initial forecast)
4. Assess the predicted results of the decision (determine the correlation between forecast and reality)
5. Adjust the intuitive estimate using the formula: adjusted estimate = group mean + correlation x (initial estimate - group mean)

**Three-phase model for adjusting biased decisions in others:**
1. Accurately perceive the context in which the decision is being made
2. Distinguish the potential bias(es) surrounding the decision and the decision makers
3. Identify and make the appropriate logical adjustments

**Competitive application:** Richard Thaler and Russell Fuller created the Fuller-Thaler mutual funds, which buy securities by exploiting the predictable biases of other market participants -- e.g., most analysts underreact to new positive information about firms, creating temporary undervaluation.

### Strategy 7: Nudge Wiser and More Ethical Decisions
**Thaler and Sunstein (2008) -- "libertarian paternalism":** Design choice architectures that nudge people toward better decisions while preserving freedom of choice.

**Organ donation example:** Most Americans prefer option (a) -- heart used to save a life, with 90% chance of receiving one if needed. Yet U.S. policy resembles option (b) -- buried with heart, 45% chance. Why? [[Loss-aversion]] makes opt-out feel like losing something. European countries with opt-out programs have organ donation rates of 86-100% vs. 4-28% for opt-in countries.

**Save More Tomorrow (Thaler & Benartzi, 2004):** Workers commit in advance to increase 401(k) contributions when they get future raises. Leverages [[hyperbolic-discounting]] (easier to commit to future sacrifice), inertia/status quo bias (people rarely opt out once enrolled), and [[loss-aversion]] (savings increase comes from new income, not current take-home). Result: more than tripled savings rates in just over two years. 39% of large U.S. employers had adopted it by 2007.

**Nudging hiring decisions (Bohnet, van Geen, & Bazerman, 2012):** When evaluating candidates one at a time, people rely on gender stereotypes (men for math, women for verbal). When two or more candidates are evaluated jointly, decision makers shift to focusing on individual capabilities, producing both more ethical and more meritocratic hiring.

### Conclusion
The first three strategies seek broad change in intuitive responses; the last four provide techniques for improving specific decisions in specific contexts. Together, they provide tools for changing and "refreezing" intuitive decision-making processes.

The responsibility for refreezing lies with the reader. Lasting improvement requires persistent monitoring and vigilance across the complex world of decisions. It is far easier to identify a bias while reading a book than when in the midst of an organizational crisis. Raiffa (1984) found that his students applied decision-making strategies on exams but failed to generalize to courses taught by other instructors.

In addition to improving your own decisions, the ideas should inform you about the decisions of others -- reporters, politicians, and other information providers constantly exemplify biased processes.

The authors conclude: managers tend to reward results rather than good decision processes. Because decisions involve uncertainty, good decisions sometimes turn out badly and bad decisions sometimes turn out well. To the extent that a manager rewards results and not sound decision making, the manager rewards behaviors that may not work in the future.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- **Expertise vs. experience:** Neale and Northcraft (1989) distinguish these sharply -- experience (passive feedback) may actually increase [[overconfidence-in-calibration]], while expertise (strategic conceptualization) can reduce bias. This challenges the folk intuition that "more experience = better judgment."
- **Disclosure as cure for [[self-serving-bias-in-decisions]]:** Cain, Loewenstein, and Moore (2005) (from Ch.8) showed disclosure can actually increase bias by giving advisers moral license. The current chapter's Strategy 7 (nudging) provides a structural alternative to mere disclosure.
- **Individual debiasing vs. systemic nudges:** The chapter implicitly acknowledges tension between Strategies 1-5 (individual improvement) and Strategy 7 (system design). Fischhoff and Larrick both note that individual debiasing has limited durability without systemic support.
