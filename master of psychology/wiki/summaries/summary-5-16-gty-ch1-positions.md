# Getting to Yes Ch.1: Don't Bargain Over Positions — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 1
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Positional bargaining is the default mode of negotiation, and it fails on all three criteria of a good method.** Any method should be judged by whether it produces a wise agreement (if agreement is possible), is efficient, and improves or at least does not damage the relationship. Positional bargaining fails all three.
2. **Arguing over positions produces unwise agreements.** As negotiators lock into positions, ego becomes identified with the position. The more you defend a position, the more committed you become to it. Attention shifts from meeting underlying concerns to "saving face" — making it less likely that any agreement will wisely reconcile the parties' original interests.
3. **Positional bargaining is inefficient.** It creates incentives for stalling, extreme opening positions, deception, and small incremental concessions — all of which increase the time and cost of negotiation, and raise the risk of no agreement at all.
4. **Positional bargaining endangers ongoing relationships.** It becomes a contest of will. Anger and resentment build as one side feels "bulldozed" by the other's rigid position. Commercial enterprises that have been doing business together for years may part company.
5. **Being nice is no answer.** Soft positional bargaining — emphasizing agreement over victory, making concessions to cultivate the relationship — makes you vulnerable to anyone playing hard. A hard game dominates a soft one.
6. **There is an alternative: principled negotiation.** Developed at the Harvard Negotiation Project, this method can be boiled down to four basic points: (1) Separate the people from the problem, (2) Focus on interests, not positions, (3) Invent options for mutual gain, (4) Insist on using objective criteria.

## Detailed Notes

### The Classic Positional Dance
Fisher and Ury open with a haggling vignette — a customer and shopkeeper bargaining over a brass dish. Each takes a position, makes concessions, and they may reach agreement. But this approach treats negotiation as a zero-sum ritual of successive concessions.

Three criteria for judging any negotiation method:
- It should produce a **wise agreement** if agreement is possible (one that meets legitimate interests of each side, resolves conflicting interests fairly, is durable, and takes community interests into account)
- It should be **efficient**
- It should **improve or at least not damage the relationship**

### Why Positional Bargaining Fails

**Unwise agreements:** The Kennedy-Khrushchev breakdown over on-site nuclear inspections is the key example. The Soviets agreed to three inspections; the U.S. insisted on no fewer than ten. They broke down over numbers — positions — despite the fact that neither side had tried to design an inspection procedure that would reconcile the U.S. interest in verification with the Soviet interest in minimal intrusion. The result is typically a mechanical splitting of the difference rather than a creative solution.

**Inefficiency:** Each negotiator must make a large number of individual decisions — what to offer, what to reject, how much to concede. This is difficult and time-consuming. The more extreme the opening positions and the smaller the concessions, the more time and effort it takes.

**Relationship damage:** Positional bargaining becomes a contest of will. "If you want to go to the movies with me, it's *The Maltese Falcon* or nothing." Anger and resentment result. Neighbors may stop speaking; bitter feelings may last a lifetime.

**Multi-party problem:** When many parties are involved, positional bargaining is even worse. With 150 countries at the UN, it may take all to say yes but only one to say no. Reciprocal concessions are impossible. Coalition formation produces positions that are more symbolic than substantive.

### Soft vs. Hard vs. Principled

Fisher and Ury present a three-column table contrasting soft, hard, and principled negotiation:

| Soft | Hard | Principled |
|------|------|-----------|
| Participants are friends | Participants are adversaries | Participants are problem-solvers |
| Goal is agreement | Goal is victory | Goal is a wise outcome reached efficiently and amicably |
| Make concessions to cultivate relationship | Demand concessions as condition of relationship | Separate the people from the problem |
| Be soft on people and problem | Be hard on people and problem | Be soft on people, hard on problem |
| Trust others | Distrust others | Proceed independent of trust |
| Change position easily | Dig in to position | **Focus on interests, not positions** |
| Make offers | Make threats | Explore interests |
| Disclose bottom line | Mislead about bottom line | Avoid having a bottom line |
| Accept one-sided losses | Demand one-sided gains | **Invent options for mutual gain** |
| Search for answer *they* will accept | Search for answer *you* will accept | Develop multiple options to choose from; decide later |
| Insist on agreement | Insist on position | **Insist on using objective criteria** |
| Yield to pressure | Apply pressure | Reason and be open to reasons; yield to principle, not pressure |

### The Meta-Game of Negotiation
The game of negotiation takes place at two levels: substance and procedure. The second negotiation — how you will negotiate — is a "meta-game" that usually escapes notice. Every move you make is both a substantive move and a procedural one that helps structure the rules of the game. [[principled-negotiation]] changes the game by offering a third option beyond soft and hard positional bargaining.

### Three Stages of Application
The four propositions of principled negotiation apply throughout three stages:
1. **Analysis** — diagnosing the situation, gathering information, identifying people problems and interests
2. **Planning** — generating ideas, deciding what to do about people problems, setting realistic objectives
3. **Discussion** — communicating back and forth, acknowledging differences in perception, generating mutually advantageous options

- The soft/hard dichotomy maps to [[framing-effects]] — by framing negotiation as only two options, people miss the third alternative. Fisher and Ury are explicitly deframing this false dichotomy.
