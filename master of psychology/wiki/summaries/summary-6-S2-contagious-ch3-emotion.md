# Contagious Ch.3: Emotion — Summary

**Author:** Jonah Berger
**Source:** *Contagious: Why Things Catch On* (2013), Chapter 3
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **When we care, we share.** Emotional content is more likely to be shared than unemotional content. The reason people shared Denise Grady's obscure article about cough aerodynamics was not social currency or practical value — it was the *emotion* the article evoked.
2. **Not all emotions boost sharing equally.** The critical variable is not positivity/negativity but **physiological arousal**. [[high-arousal-emotions]] like awe, excitement, amusement (positive) and anger, anxiety (negative) all increase sharing. Low-arousal emotions like sadness and contentment decrease sharing.
3. **Awe is the most powerful sharing emotion.** Awe-inspiring articles were 30% more likely to make the *New York Times* Most E-Mailed list, even after controlling for other factors. Science articles disproportionately make the list because they frequently evoke awe.
4. **Any source of physiological arousal can boost sharing** — even physical exercise. Students who jogged in place for 60 seconds were nearly twice as likely to share an article as those who sat still, even though the arousal had nothing to do with the content.
5. **Focus on feelings, not features.** Marketing messages tend to focus on information (features, facts, statistics). But information alone rarely changes behavior. The key is to evoke the underlying emotions that motivate action.

## Detailed Notes

### Denise Grady's Viral Cough Article
On October 27, 2008, Denise Grady, a science writer at *The New York Times*, published an article about schlieren photography — a technique that captures visible images of invisible phenomena like a human cough. The article rocketed to the top of the Most E-Mailed list. It scored low on social currency and practical value. What it had was *emotion* — specifically, awe. The image of a cough made visible was simply amazing. When we care, we share.

### Systematically Analyzing the Most E-Mailed List
[[berger]] and colleague Katherine Milkman created a Web crawler to automatically scan the *New York Times* home page every fifteen minutes for six months, recording nearly 7,000 articles with their content characteristics, positioning, and whether they made the Most E-Mailed list.

**Initial findings on topic and content:**
- Education and health articles were more viral than sports or politics articles
- More interesting articles were 25% more likely to make the list
- More useful articles were 30% more likely to make the list
- But one topic defied expectations: science articles made the Most E-Mailed list more than politics, fashion, or business news, despite not being particularly high in social currency or practical value

### The Power of Awe
Awe is the sense of wonder and amazement that occurs when someone is inspired by great knowledge, beauty, sublimity, or might. According to psychologists Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt, awe is the experience of confronting something greater than yourself. It expands one's frame of reference, drives self-transcendence, and encompasses admiration, inspiration, and even fear.

Awe is a complex emotion frequently involving surprise, unexpectedness, or mystery. As Einstein noted: "The most beautiful emotion we can experience is the mysterious."

**Research findings:** Awe-inspiring articles were 30% more likely to make the Most E-Mailed list. Articles previously judged to have low social currency and practical value — like Grady's cough piece or an article about gorillas grieving like humans — nevertheless made the list because of the awe they inspired.

**Susan Boyle's *Britain's Got Talent* audition** is one of the most viral videos ever — 100+ million views in nine days. The awe came from the contrast between her frumpy appearance and her transcendent singing voice. It was moving *and* awe-inspiring, and that emotion drove people to pass it on.

### Does Any Emotion Boost Sharing?
No. [[berger]] and Milkman tested whether sadness boosts sharing. **Sadder articles were actually 16% *less* likely to make the Most E-Mailed list.** Sadness was making people *less* likely to share.

**The positive/negative distinction was insufficient.** If positive emotions increased sharing and negative emotions decreased it, then anger and anxiety should also decrease sharing. But they didn't — articles evoking anger or anxiety were *more* likely to make the Most E-Mailed list. Something more complicated than simple valence was at work.

### Kindling the Fire: Physiological Arousal
The key dimension is not positivity/negativity but **physiological arousal** — the state of activation and readiness for action. Arousal involves elevated heart rate, blood pressure, and muscle tension. It evolved from the fight-or-flight response.

**The arousal framework for sharing:**

| | HIGH AROUSAL | LOW AROUSAL |
|---|---|---|
| **POSITIVE** | Awe, Excitement, Amusement (Humor) | Contentment |
| **NEGATIVE** | Anger, Anxiety | Sadness |

**High-arousal emotions kindle the fire** — they activate people and drive them to take action, including sharing. Low-arousal emotions deactivate — sad people curl up on the couch; content people relax.

This explains why:
- Funny videos go viral (amusement is high-arousal)
- Anger-provoking articles spread (anger is high-arousal)
- Sad stories don't spread as well (sadness is low-arousal)
- Contentment doesn't drive sharing (contentment is low-arousal)

### United Breaks Guitars
Musician Dave Carroll's $3,500 guitar was smashed by United Airlines baggage handlers. After nine months of futile complaints, he channeled his anger into a song — "United Breaks Guitars" — posted on YouTube. Within four days it had 1.3 million views. Within ten days, 3 million views and 14,000 comments. Within four days of the video being posted, United's stock price fell 10% — the equivalent of $180 million. The high-arousal emotion of anger (both Carroll's and viewers') drove massive sharing.

### Focus on Feelings
Marketing messages tend to focus on information — features, facts, statistics. But information alone is rarely enough to change behavior. Most teens know smoking is bad; most people know they should eat vegetables. Additional information won't tip the scales. **Emotion is what motivates action.**

**Google's "Parisian Love":** Google's Creative Lab team, led by designer Anthony Cafaro, wanted to humanize the search interface. Rather than showcasing features, they created a video showing a budding love story told entirely through Google searches — studying abroad in Paris, learning French, searching for churches, assembling a crib. The emotional resonance was so powerful that Google's CEO's wife loved it, and it was released as a Super Bowl ad. By focusing on feelings rather than features, Google turned a utilitarian product into a viral hit.

**The Three Whys technique** (from Chip and Dan Heath's *Made to Stick*): Ask "Why is this important?" three times to drill down past features to the emotional core. Why is search important? To find information quickly. Why? To connect with people, achieve goals, fulfill dreams. *Now* it's emotional.

### Kindling the Fire with High-Arousal Emotions
Both positive and negative high-arousal emotions can drive sharing. BMW's 2001 short film series *The Hire* featured kidnappings, FBI raids, and near-death experiences — evoking fear and anxiety. The clips got 11+ million views in four months and increased BMW sales by 12%.

**Disgust is high-arousal.** The "Man Drinks Fat" antisoda PSA showed fat pouring out of a soda can — gross, but it encouraged people to talk about and share the PSA. Messages that make people anxious or disgusted (high arousal) rather than sad (low arousal) will boost transmission.

### Babywearing, Boycotts, and Blunting Bad Buzz
The Motrin babywearing ad debacle illustrates how high-arousal negative emotions (anger) can drive rapid social transmission. Mothers were offended by implications that they wore babies as "fashion statements." Within hours, thousands of angry posts appeared. The anger was high-arousal, so it spread rapidly.

**Predicting which negative comments will snowball:** Certain types of negativity are more likely to escalate because they evoke high-arousal emotions. Angry tirades about bad customer service or anxious rumors about benefit cuts are more likely to circulate than expressions of sadness or disappointment. Monitoring for high-arousal language ("pissed off," "angry," "mad") can help identify issues before they snowball.

### Exercise Makes People Share
In a Wharton lab experiment, half the participants sat still for 60 seconds while the other half jogged in place. Then both groups were given an article and the option to email it. **Among joggers, 75% shared the article — nearly twice the rate of the seated group.** Running doesn't evoke emotion, but it is physiologically arousing. Any sort of arousal, whether from emotional or physical sources, can boost transmission.

This has practical implications: ads during exciting TV shows or at the gym may get more sharing. Work groups taking walks together may share more ideas. Even a turbulent plane ride can make seatmates overshare personal details.
