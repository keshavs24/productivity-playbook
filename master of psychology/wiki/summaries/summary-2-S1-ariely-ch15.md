# Predictably Irrational Ch.15: Beer and Free Lunches — Summary

**Author(s):** Dan Ariely
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. When people order food or drinks sequentially in public, they choose differently than when they order privately — public ordering increases variety-seeking (in the US) or conformity (in Hong Kong)
2. People who order publicly to convey uniqueness sacrifice their own enjoyment — they end up with choices they don't actually prefer
3. The first person to order in a group is as happy as private orderers because they are uninfluenced by others
4. Behavioral economics offers "free lunches" — systematic irrationalities create opportunities for tools, policies, and methods that help people make better decisions

## Detailed Notes

### The Carolina Brewery Experiment (Ariely and Levav)
**Location:** Carolina Brewery, Franklin Street, Chapel Hill, NC
**Researchers:** Dan Ariely and Jonathan Levav (Columbia)

**Setup:** Ariely and Levav served as waiters, offering free beer samples (four craft beers: Copperline Amber Ale, Franklin Street Lager, India Pale Ale, Summer Wheat Ale) to tables of customers.

**Condition 1 — Sequential ordering (public):** Each person stated their choice aloud, one by one, as in normal restaurant ordering. 50 tables.

**Condition 2 — Private ordering:** After hearing the beer descriptions, each person wrote down their preferred beer on a card, privately, without knowing others' choices. 50 tables.

**Results:**
- **Public ordering:** More variety — people chose more types of beer per table. When someone's preferred beer was "taken," they switched to a different one (e.g., the less popular Summer Wheat Ale) to show they had a mind of their own.
- **Private ordering:** Less variety — people ordered what they genuinely wanted, often resulting in duplicates.
- **Enjoyment:** Public orderers were LESS happy with their beer and more likely to regret their choice. The first person to order publicly was the exception — they were just as happy as private orderers because they were unconstrained.

### The Need for Uniqueness
**Duke wine study (Ariely and Levav):** Measured personality traits alongside ordering behavior. Found a correlation between the tendency to order different drinks and a personality trait called "need for uniqueness." People more concerned with portraying their uniqueness were more likely to order something nobody else at the table had chosen — sacrificing personal utility to gain reputational utility.

### Cross-Cultural Differences
**Hong Kong replication:** In collectivist cultures where need for uniqueness is not valued, public ordering showed the OPPOSITE pattern — conformity rather than differentiation. People were MORE likely to select the same item as those before them when ordering aloud. But they were equally unhappy — conforming to match others also meant ending up with something they didn't prefer.

### Practical Advice
1. When going to a restaurant, plan your order before the waiter arrives and stick to it
2. If worried about being swayed, announce your order to the table before the waiter comes
3. Best option: order first
4. Restaurant owners should consider having customers write orders privately — this is the cheapest and simplest way to increase dining enjoyment

### Standard Economics vs. Behavioral Economics
**Standard economics assumes:**
- People are rational, informed, and cognitively unhindered
- Wrong decisions are self-correcting through learning and market forces
- There are no "free lunches" — any existing opportunities would already be exploited

**Behavioral economics shows:**
- Irrational behaviors are systematic and predictable, not random
- We all make the same types of mistakes over and over due to the basic wiring of our brains
- Context effects, irrelevant emotions, shortsightedness, and other irrationalities influence decisions
- Economics should be based on how people actually behave, not how they should behave

### The Concept of "Free Lunches"
The good news about systematic mistakes: they create opportunities for improvement. If we all make predictable errors, we can develop strategies, tools, and policies to help us make better decisions. These improvements provide net benefits — "free lunches."

**Example 1 — Self-control credit card** (from Ch.7): A credit card that lets people pre-commit to spending limits.

**Example 2 — "Save More Tomorrow" (Thaler and Benartzi):** When employees join a company, they commit to investing a percentage of their future salary raises in retirement. It's psychologically easier to sacrifice future consumption than current consumption, and even easier to give up money you don't yet have. Result: savings rates increased from about 3.5% to around 13.5%.

### The Big Picture
- We are pawns in a game whose forces we largely fail to comprehend
- We think of ourselves as sitting in the driver's seat with ultimate control, but this perception has more to do with our desires than with reality
- Each chapter describes a force (emotions, relativity, social norms, etc.) that exerts power over our behavior, and our natural tendency is to vastly underestimate or completely ignore this power
- Just as we can't help being fooled by visual illusions, we fall for "decision illusions" — our visual and decision environments are filtered by our senses and brain, and the input we base decisions on is not necessarily a true reflection of reality
- **Silver lining:** If we all make mistakes, there are also ways to improve our decisions — and therefore there are opportunities for "free lunches"
