# Pre-Suasion Ch.3: The Importance of Attention... Is Importance — Summary

**Author:** Robert Cialdini
**Source:** *Pre-Suasion: A Revolutionary Way to Influence and Persuade* (2016), Chapter 3
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Whatever receives focused attention is perceived as more important.** This is the central mechanism of pre-suasion — the persuader who draws outsize attention to the most favorable feature of an offer becomes a successful pre-suader by making audiences both consider and overvalue that feature.
2. **Kahneman's "focusing illusion" is the core principle.** When asked to name the one scientific concept that would most improve everyone's understanding of the world, Kahneman wrote a full essay on what he called "the focusing illusion": "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."
3. **Agenda-setting theory shows how media pre-suades at scale.** The press may not tell people *what* to think, but it is "stunningly successful in telling people what to think *about*" (Bernard Cohen). German and US studies show that perceived importance of issues tracks media coverage almost perfectly, rising and falling with the volume of stories.
4. **Background information can powerfully shape foreground decisions without awareness.** Mandel and Johnson's online furniture study showed that website wallpaper of fluffy clouds made visitors prefer comfortable (expensive) sofas, while pennies wallpaper made them prefer cheap sofas — and participants denied the wallpaper influenced them at all.
5. **Attention-drawing techniques can backfire if the case has no merit.** Drawing attention to a weak argument makes people see it as *more important* but also more obviously wrong. "Any practice that pulls attention to an idea will be successful only when the idea has merit."

## Detailed Notes

### What's Salient Is Important

Cialdini opens with Queen Elizabeth II's Golden Jubilee anecdote: when a young woman's phone rang during a personal audience with the queen, Elizabeth leaned forward and said, "You should answer that, dear. It might be someone important." The queen's graciousness aside, the remark reveals a universal tendency — whatever draws focused attention is perceived as important, even relative to the Queen of England.

### The Focusing Illusion

[[kahneman]]'s answer to the question "What scientific concept would most improve everyone's understanding?" was not about System 1 and System 2 or prospect theory — it was the **focusing illusion**: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it." The implications for pre-suasion are direct: a communicator who focuses an audience on a key element of a message pre-loads it with importance.

### Agenda Setting

This form of pre-suasion accounts for the media's principal role in influencing public opinion. The central tenet of **agenda-setting theory**: media rarely produce change directly by presenting compelling evidence; instead, they persuade indirectly by giving selected issues and facts better coverage than others. Key evidence:
- **Dusseldorf pipe bomb (2000)**: Right-wing extremism ratings spiked from ~0% to 35% as "most important issue" during heavy coverage, then sank back to zero
- **9/11 tenth anniversary**: 9/11 rated as "especially important event of past 70 years" by ~30% before anniversary coverage, spiked to 65% during coverage, then returned to 30% two weeks later

### Why Attention = Importance

The association is adaptive: normally, we direct attention to things that genuinely are significant — a strange noise, smoke, a CEO speaking. Rhesus monkeys will pay (in food) to look at high-status colony members. The problem is the heuristic can be hijacked: "All too often, people believe that if they have paid attention to an idea or event or group, it must be important enough to warrant the consideration." This explains the Hollywood saying "There's no such thing as bad publicity" — raw attention anoints subjects with presumed importance.

### Three Back Roads to Attention

**1. Managing the Background** — Mandel & Johnson (2002) online furniture store study:
- Fluffy clouds wallpaper → visitors rated comfort as more important, searched for comfort info, chose comfortable (costlier) sofas
- Pennies wallpaper → visitors rated price as more important, searched for cost info, chose cheap sofas
- Participants denied the wallpaper influenced them

**2. Banner ads** — Repeated exposure to banner ads near an online article increased favorability toward the brand, with no wear-out effect. The key: the ads were *never consciously processed*, so they couldn't be identified as tedious or untrustworthy. The absence of recognition/recall — normally considered advertising failure — was actually the source of their power.

**3. Shifting the Task at Hand** — The Iraq War embedded reporter program: 93% of embedded journalists' stories came from the soldiers' perspective; 71% of front-page coverage went to embedded reporters; only 2% of all stories mentioned the absence of WMDs. By making the *task* of journalism molecular (daily troop activities) rather than molar (strategic justification), the program pre-suasively shifted attention from the war's contested rationale to its relatively successful combat operations. The focusing illusion ensured it.

### Inviting Favorable Evaluation

The key to benefiting from focused attention is to keep the focus *unitary*. Research across multiple settings shows that merely asking consumers to evaluate *only* one brand — without prompting comparison — enhances their ratings, purchase intentions, and support. Decision makers assigned to evaluate *only one* of four strategic options recommended that option as the best. The antidote: the "consider-the-opposite" debiasing technique — systematically asking "What could go wrong?" and "What would happen if it did?"

### Environmental Noise and Learning

Background noise is not merely annoying — it measurably reduces cognitive performance:
- NYC students near elevated subway tracks had significantly lower reading scores; installing noise-dampening materials raised scores back up
- Munich airport relocation: children near the new airport saw memory and reading scores plummet; those near the old location saw scores rise
- Heavily decorated classrooms reduce test scores for young children learning science
