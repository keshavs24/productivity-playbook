# Age of Propaganda Ch.27: What Is the Influence of One Flower Given? (Phantoms) — Summary

**Authors:** Anthony Pratkanis & Elliot Aronson
**Source:** *Age of Propaganda: The Everyday Use and Abuse of Persuasion* (Revised Edition), Chapter 27
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **A [[phantom-alternative]] is an unavailable option that shifts preference for available options.** Pratkanis and Farquhar's experiments showed that introducing an attractive but unavailable product made other brands look less attractive (contrast effect) *and* changed the decision criteria — the attribute on which the phantom excelled became the most important factor, shifting choice toward the available brand strongest on that attribute.
2. **The [[phantom-trap]]** is the tendency to marshal most or all of one's resources attempting to obtain something that cannot be reached. Phantom fixation (Mischel's delay-of-gratification research) shows that concentrating on the unavailable creates unbearable conflict, while distraction allows better decisions.
3. **Scarcity and unavailability trigger a cascade of psychological effects:** the unavailable becomes exotic (simple heuristic: rare = valuable); emotions are aroused (uniqueness, inadequacy, reactance); and phantom fixation directs attention away from available alternatives.
4. **The "consumer Catch-22 carousel"** (Snyder): once we define ourselves through scarce material goods, we enter an endless treadmill of dissatisfaction — acquiring the rare product makes you like everyone else, heightening the need for the next phantom.

## Detailed Notes

### The Power of Unavailability

When an object is made unavailable (a *phantom* choice), our first inference is: if it is rare, it must be valuable. Research evidence:
- Female college students believed a scarce pair of nylon hosiery should cost more than a readily available pair
- Children rated cookies in scarce supply as more desirable than identical cookies in abundance
- News of an upcoming shortage of imported beef led food stores to order 2-6x normal quantities

### Pratkanis and Farquhar's Phantom Experiments

College students were asked to choose among brands of products. When a very attractive alternative was included but marked unavailable:
1. **Contrast effect:** Other brands looked *less* attractive (opposite direction from decoy effects)
2. **Criteria shift:** The attribute on which the phantom excelled became the most important decision criterion
3. **Choice shift:** Students chose the available brand that rated highest on the phantom's best attribute

### The Phantom Trap

**Phantom fixation:** Walter Mischel's delay-of-gratification research illustrates the danger. Children could get a small candy bar immediately or wait for a big one. Children who *distracted* themselves (thinking of other things) waited longest and got the better prize. Children who *fixated on the prize* experienced unbearable conflict and took the immediate lesser option.

In broader terms, the phantom trap means marshaling most resources to obtain the unobtainable. Examples:
- Early settlers pursuing El Dorado — lured by propaganda about fountains of youth and gold
- Computer industry "vaporware" — preannounced but unavailable products that freeze consumer decisions
- Not obtaining a phantom can lead to aggression (one explanation for riots and revolutions), sour grapes (devaluing the phantom), or psychological reactance (desperate attempts to restore lost freedom)

### The Paper Clip Experiment

Pratkanis, Freeman, and Farquhar: Students earned money through manual labor, then went to purchase paper clips. Some encountered a "surprise phantom" — after selecting their preferred option, they were told they hadn't earned enough to purchase it.

Two manipulation conditions:
1. **Direct pressure:** "I'll give you the next most similar clip. It's your only choice." Result: subjects *refused* and chose something deliberately different (reactance)
2. **Subtle pressure:** "You might want to consider the next most similar clip." Result: almost all subjects chose the recommended clip — guided not by product merit but by phantom-aroused emotions

In both cases, decisions were based on emotions aroused by the phantom rather than product merits.

### Censorship and Phantom Effects

Making something "unavailable" through censorship can increase its perceived value:
- *The Satanic Verses* might have sold a few thousand copies without the Ayatollah's death decree — instead it became a bestseller
- Zhou Weihui's *Shanghai Baby* was banned by China, which caused pirated copies and film rights to proliferate
- Zellinger et al.: students told a book had a "21-year-old age restriction" rated it as more desirable

### Three Defenses Against the Phantom Trap

1. **Anticipate phantoms** — develop contingency plans in advance. If the car you want is "unavailable," be prepared to walk off the lot
2. **Imagine alternative scenarios** — considering other phantoms or accepting available options reduces fixation on the one phantom
3. **Use phantom emotions as a warning signal** — the strong feelings that accompany a phantom (urgency, inadequacy, fear of missing out) are a cue that something is being manipulated. React to circumstances, not emotions
