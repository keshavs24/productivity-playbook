# Getting to Yes Ch.8: What If They Use Dirty Tricks? — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 8
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Tricky bargaining tactics are one-sided proposals about negotiating procedure.** They fail the test of reciprocity — they are designed to be used by only one side. To counter them, you want to engage in principled negotiation about the negotiating *procedure*.
2. **Three steps to negotiating about the rules of the game:** (1) Recognize the tactic, (2) Raise the issue explicitly and question its legitimacy and desirability, (3) Negotiate over it — this negotiation focuses on procedure, not substance, but the goal remains a wise agreement.
3. **Tricky tactics fall into three categories:** deliberate deception, psychological warfare, and positional pressure tactics.
4. **The principled response to every category is the same:** Separate the people from the problem (question the tactic, not their integrity). Focus on interests, not positions. Invent options for mutual gain. Insist on objective criteria. As a last resort, turn to your [[batna|BATNA]].

## Detailed Notes

### How Do You Negotiate About the Rules of the Game?

Most people respond to dirty tricks in one of two ways:
1. **Put up with it** — hope for the best, keep quiet, tolerate it (the Neville Chamberlain approach — at Munich, Chamberlain thought he had an agreement, but Hitler raised his demands)
2. **Respond in kind** — if they are deceptive, be deceptive too; if they make threats, make counterthreats (leads to escalation or breakdown)

The principled response: **Recognize the tactic, raise it explicitly, and negotiate about it.** Simply raising a question about a tactic may be enough to get them to stop using it. The most important purpose of bringing the tactic up explicitly is to give you an opportunity to negotiate about the rules of the game.

Apply the four principles to procedure itself:
- **Separate the people from the problem.** Don't attack them personally for using a tactic. Question the tactic, not their integrity.
- **Focus on interests, not positions.** "Are you protecting yourself from criticism? Or are you protecting yourself from changing your position?"
- **Invent options for mutual gain.** Suggest alternative games to play. "How about our undertaking to make no statements to the press until we reach agreement or break off the talks?"
- **Insist on using objective criteria.** "Is there a theory behind having me sit in the low chair with my back to the open door?" Try out the principle of reciprocity.
- As a last resort, turn to your **BATNA** and walk out.

### Deliberate Deception

**Phony facts.** The oldest form of negotiation trickery. "This car was driven only 5,000 miles by a little old lady from Pasadena." Verify factual assertions independently. A practice of verifying factual assertions reduces the incentive for deception.

**Ambiguous authority.** The other side may allow you to believe they have full authority to compromise when they don't. After they have pressed you as hard as they can, they announce they must take it to "someone else" for approval. This is designed to give them a "second bite at the apple." Before starting, find out about the authority of the other side.

**Dubious intentions.** One possible misrepresentation is of intention to comply with the agreement. Build compliance features into the agreement itself. A divorce lawyer's client doesn't believe her husband will pay child support — build in a contingent agreement: "If for some inexplicable reason he misses two payments, my client will get the equity in the house."

**Less than full disclosure is not the same as deception.** Good faith negotiation does not require total disclosure. "Let's not put ourselves under such a strong temptation to mislead."

### Psychological Warfare

**Stressful situations.** Be sensitive to whether a meeting is being held at your place or theirs, or on neutral territory. Contrary to accepted wisdom, it is sometimes advantageous to accept an offer to meet on the other side's turf. If you feel under stress, say why. Suggest changing chairs, taking a break, adjourning.

**Personal attacks.** They can comment on your clothes, your appearance, make you wait, interrupt you, refuse to make eye contact. Recognize the tactic and raise it explicitly; bringing it up explicitly will probably prevent a recurrence.

**Good-guy/bad-guy routine.** One person takes a tough stand, another appears reasonable. Recognize it: "I appreciate that you are trying to be reasonable, but I still want to know why you think that's a fair price."

**Threats.** Threats are pressure. Good negotiators rarely resort to them. Warnings are much more legitimate than threats and are not vulnerable to counterthreats. "Should we fail to reach agreement, it seems highly probable to me that the news media would insist on publishing the whole sordid story." Threats can be ignored, taken as unauthorized, or answered with principled responses.

### Positional Pressure Tactics

**Refusal to negotiate.** When the American diplomats were taken hostage in Tehran in November 1979, the Iranian government refused to negotiate. Communicate through third parties. Don't attack them for refusing; find out their interests in not negotiating. Insist on using principles.

**Extreme demands.** An opening demand of $75,000 for a house worth $200,000. Bringing the tactic to their attention works well. Ask for principled justification.

**Escalating demands.** A negotiator raises one of his demands for every concession he makes. Call it to their attention and perhaps take a break while considering whether to continue.

**Lock-in tactics.** Thomas Schelling's two trucks on a single-lane road — one driver throws his steering wheel out the window. In response, try to interrupt the communication ("I see you told the papers your goal was to settle for $200,000. Well, we all have our aspirations"). Alternatively, crack a joke and not take the lock-in seriously.

**Hardhearted partner.** "I would personally have no objection but my hardhearted partner will not let him." Recognize the tactic. Get his agreement to the principle involved, then if possible speak directly with the "hardhearted partner."

**A calculated delay.** One side postpones a decision until the last few hours before a deadline, relying on psychological pressure. Look for objective conditions to establish deadlines.

**"Take it or leave it."** There is nothing inherently wrong with confronting the other side with a firm choice. Keep talking as if you didn't hear it, or change the subject. If you bring up the "take it or leave it" tactic and negotiating about it, let them know what they have to lose if no agreement is reached.

### Don't Be a Victim

It is often hard to decide what it means to negotiate in "good faith." You must decide on your own internal values whether you want to use tactics you would consider improper and in bad faith if used against you. It may be useful at the beginning to say, "Look, I know this may be unusual, but I want to know the rules of the game we're going to play. Are we both trying to reach a wise agreement as quickly and with as little effort as possible? Or are we going to play 'hard bargaining' where the more stubborn fellow wins?" Be prepared to fight dirty bargaining tactics. You can be just as firm as they can, even firmer. It is easier to defend principle than an illegitimate tactic.

## New Concepts Introduced
- Dirty tricks taxonomy: deliberate deception, psychological warfare, positional pressure tactics

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The good-guy/bad-guy routine is a specific deployment of [[contrast-principle]] from Cialdini's influence research — the "good guy" seems reasonable only in contrast with the "bad guy"
- Lock-in tactics exploit [[commitment-and-consistency]] — once publicly committed to a position, the negotiator faces strong pressure to maintain it
- Extreme demands work through [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — even an absurd anchor pulls the final number toward it
- Escalating demands exploit [[loss-aversion]] — each concession you've already won feels like something you'll lose if you don't give in to the new demand
- The principled response to all dirty tricks is essentially a meta-application of the four principles of [[principled-negotiation]] — applied to the negotiation process itself rather than to substance

## Quotes Worth Keeping
> "Such tricky tactics are illegitimate because they fail the test of reciprocity. They are designed to be used by only one side; the other side is not supposed to know the tactics or is expected to tolerate them knowingly." — Fisher & Ury, p.64

> "It is easier to defend principle than an illegitimate tactic. Don't be a victim." — Fisher & Ury, p.71
