# Predictably Irrational Ch.13: The Context of Our Character, Part I — Summary

**Author(s):** Dan Ariely
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Most people cheat, but only a little — the majority of dishonesty comes from many people cheating by small amounts, not a few bad apples cheating a lot
2. The probability of being caught has almost no effect on the level of cheating — people cheat the same amount whether they shred their papers or not
3. Moral reminders (Ten Commandments, honor codes) completely eliminate cheating, even when the reminder is secular and even when no actual honor code exists
4. Dishonesty is not driven by rational cost-benefit analysis but by an internal tension between wanting to benefit from cheating and wanting to see ourselves as honest

## Detailed Notes

### The Scale of Everyday Dishonesty
- Total cost of all US robberies in 2004: $525 million (average $1,300 per robbery)
- Employee theft and fraud: ~$600 billion per year — dramatically higher than all robbery, burglary, larceny-theft, and auto theft combined ($16 billion)
- Insurance fraud: $24 billion in bogus property claims per year
- IRS tax gap: $350 billion per year
- Retail "wardrobing" (buy, wear, return): $16 billion per year

### Two Types of Dishonesty (Mazar, Amir, and Ariely)
1. **Rational criminal type:** Cost-benefit calculation — how much money, probability of getting caught, severity of punishment. Decides to rob or not based on expected value.
2. **Ordinary dishonesty:** Generally honest people who take a pen from a conference, exaggerate on insurance claims, or pad expense reports. This is the type Ariely studies.

### The Harvard Business School Cheating Experiment (Mazar, Amir, and Ariely)
**Setup:** 50 multiple-choice questions (standardized test style). 15 minutes. 10 cents per correct answer. Four conditions:

**Condition 1 — Control (no cheating possible):** Submit worksheet AND bubble sheet. Average: 32.6 correct.

**Condition 2 — Self-check:** Pre-marked bubble sheet shows correct answers. Students transfer their answers, could change wrong ones. Submit both sheets. Average claimed: 36.2 (cheated by about 3.6 questions).

**Condition 3 — Self-check + shredding:** Same as above, but shred the worksheet. Only submit bubble sheet. Average claimed: 35.9 — about the SAME level of cheating as Condition 2.

**Condition 4 — Self-check + shredding + money jar:** Shred both sheets. Walk to a jar of coins and self-pay. Zero chance of being caught. Average claimed: 36.1 — still about the same.

**Two critical findings:**
1. **Most people cheated, but only a little.** The majority cheated by about 3.5 questions, not by the maximum possible. Nobody claimed a perfect score or took all $100 from the jar.
2. **Risk of getting caught had almost zero effect.** Whether they shredded nothing, their worksheet, or both sheets and paid themselves from a jar — the level of cheating barely changed. This contradicts the rational cost-benefit model of dishonesty.

Similar results were replicated at MIT, Princeton, UCLA, and Yale.

### What Is Honesty? — The Superego Model
- **Adam Smith's view:** Honesty is a cost-benefit calculation — people are honest when it's in their interest to be so.
- **Freud's superego:** As we internalize society's ethics, the superego is pleased when we comply and unhappy when we don't. Honest acts stimulate reward centers (nucleus accumbens, caudate nucleus).
- **Ariely's synthesis:** Our internal honesty monitor is active only for big transgressions (stealing a whole box of pens). For small transgressions (taking one pen), we don't even consider whether it reflects on our honesty — our superego stays asleep.
- In a survey of 36,000 US high school students, 98% said honesty was important to them — yet they were frequently dishonest.

### The Ten Commandments Experiment (Mazar, Amir, and Ariely)
**Setup:** UCLA lab. 20 math problems (find two numbers that add to 10). Five minutes. Lottery for $10 per problem solved. Two cheating conditions:

**Control group (no cheating):** Average 3.1 problems solved.
**Books group (recall 10 high school books, then cheat):** Average 4.1 — cheated by about 33%.
**Ten Commandments group (recall as many Commandments as possible, then cheat):** Average 3 — NO cheating at all. Zero.

**Critical detail:** They didn't even remind participants what the Commandments were — just asked them to recall them. Almost nobody could recall all 10. Students who remembered only 1-2 Commandments were as affected as those who remembered nearly all 10. It was not the Commandments themselves but the mere contemplation of a moral benchmark.

### The MIT Honor Code Experiment
**Setup:** Three groups at MIT. (1) Control — hand in answers directly. Average: 3 problems. (2) Cheat condition — pocket answers, self-report. Average: 5.5 problems. (3) Sign honor code then cheat — sign statement "I understand that this study falls under the MIT honor system," pocket answers, self-report. Average: 3 problems — exactly like the control.

**Key finding:** Signing an honor code statement eliminated cheating completely. This is particularly striking because MIT does not even have an honor code.

### Professional Oaths and Their Decline
- The word "profession" comes from Latin *professus* meaning "affirmed publicly"
- Professional oaths historically regulated behavior in law, medicine, banking
- 1960s deregulation movement dismantled many professional codes as "elitist"
- Consequences: California bar study — two-thirds of lawyers said they "compromise professionalism as a result of economic pressure." 80% said the bar "fails to adequately punish unethical attorneys." Half wouldn't become attorneys again.
- Florida bar: "money-grabbing, too clever, tricky, sneaky, and not trustworthy"
- Medical profession: unnecessary surgeries, kickbacks from labs, pharmaceutical industry influence
- Petroleum geologists: bootlegged seismic data, stolen maps, exaggerated oil deposits
- Professional ethicists: "Moral Expertise: A Problem in the Professional Ethics of Professional Ethicists"

### Practical Solutions
- Bring back moral reminders at the moment of temptation — not just at the beginning of a career
- Have people sign honor codes, oaths, or commitments at the point of decision
- Recognize that removing ethical benchmarks causes us to stray into dishonesty, and getting social norms back once lost is very difficult
- When financial self-interest opposes moral standards, we can "bend" reality — solution is to avoid such situations structurally (prohibit physicians from ordering profitable tests, accountants from consulting for audit clients, etc.)

### Reflections: Dishonesty and Toilet Paper
A UC Berkeley woman read about Ariely's experiments and posted a note in her bathroom asking housemates not to take toilet paper for private use. A fresh roll magically reappeared within hours — the moral reminder worked. She placed the note in only one bathroom (experimental control!), and the untreated bathroom continued to lose rolls.
