# Predictably Irrational Ch.2: The Fallacy of Supply and Demand — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ariely|Dan Ariely]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. Initial prices are largely **arbitrary**, but once established they become **coherent** -- shaping future willingness to pay (Ariely calls this **[[arbitrary-coherence]]**).
2. Completely random numbers (last two digits of social security numbers) significantly influenced how much people would pay for products
3. The first anchor holds across subsequent decisions, even when new anchors are introduced
4. Demand is not independent of supply -- market prices (supply-side) anchor willingness to pay, challenging standard economics

## Detailed Notes

### The Story of Salvador Assael and Black Pearls
Ariely opens with the story of how Salvador Assael created the market for Tahitian black pearls. Initially worthless (no market existed), Assael placed them in Harry Winston's Fifth Avenue window with outrageously high price tags and ran ads positioning them alongside diamonds, rubies, and emeralds. The pearls became coveted luxury items. The lesson: **initial price anchors are arbitrary** -- Assael literally created the anchor from nothing.

### Imprinting and Anchoring
- Konrad Lorenz discovered that goslings imprint on the first moving object they see -- and follow it loyally
- Humans do the same with prices: the first price we encounter for a new product category becomes our anchor
- Once we buy a product at a particular price, we become anchored to that price, and all future purchases in that category are judged relative to it

### The Social Security Number Experiment
Ariely, Drazen Prelec, and George Loewenstein conducted this experiment at MIT's Sloan School with 55 MBA students:

1. Students were presented with six items: two bottles of wine, a cordless trackball, a cordless keyboard, a design book, and Belgian chocolates
2. Students wrote the last two digits of their SSN and converted them to a dollar amount
3. They indicated yes/no whether they would pay that amount for each item
4. Then they bid their actual maximum willingness to pay

**Results:** Students with SSNs ending in 80-99 bid **216-346% higher** than those ending in 00-19. For the cordless keyboard: top quintile bid $56, bottom quintile bid $16. The correlation between SSN digits and bids ranged from 0.32 to 0.52 across products.

Critically: **within** product categories, relative pricing was coherent (everyone paid more for the keyboard than the trackball, more for the Hermitage than the Cotes du Rhone). Initial prices are arbitrary but subsequent prices are coherent -- **[[arbitrary-coherence]]**.

### Anchoring Persistence: The Annoying Sounds Experiment
To test whether anchors stick or can be replaced, Ariely ran a multi-phase experiment:
1. Participants heard three annoying sounds (high-pitched squeal, white noise, oscillating tone)
2. **Phase 1:** One group was offered 10 cents, another 90 cents, to listen again. Then both bid their real price. Those anchored at 10 cents demanded 33 cents on average; those at 90 cents demanded 73 cents -- **over twice as much** for the same experience
3. **Phase 2:** Both groups were offered 50 cents for a new sound. The 10-cent group still demanded lower prices than the 90-cent group -- the **first anchor dominated** even with an identical new anchor
4. **Phase 3:** Anchors were flipped (10-cent group got 90 cents, and vice versa). Still, the **original first anchor exerted the greatest influence**

This demonstrates that first decisions resonate across a long sequence of subsequent decisions.

### Self-Herding and Starbucks
- **Herding:** We assume something is good based on others' behavior (seeing a line at a restaurant)
- **Self-herding:** We assume something is good based on our own past behavior -- "I went to Starbucks before, so it must be good"
- **Starbucks strategy:** Howard Shultz deliberately made everything about the Starbucks experience different from Dunkin' Donuts (sizes, names, ambience) so consumers would not use Dunkin' prices as an anchor. This created a fresh anchor opportunity

### The Tom Sawyer / Poetry Reading Experiment
Ariely tested whether the same ambiguous experience could be framed as either positive or negative:
- Half the students were asked: "Would you pay me $10 for a 10-minute poetry reading?"
- Other half: "Would you listen to me read poetry for 10 minutes if I paid you $10?"

Those anchored on paying offered to pay $1-$3 for readings. Those anchored on being paid demanded $1.30-$4.80. **The same experience was arbitrarily made into something worth paying for or being paid for**, simply by the initial framing.

### Implications for Economics
- Traditional economics assumes supply and demand are independent forces. Ariely's experiments show **demand depends on past prices and anchors** -- supply-side manipulations (MSRPs, promotions) directly shape demand
- Price changes may have less long-term effect than expected if people readjust to new anchors
- **Free markets depend on rational preferences**, but if preferences are anchored by arbitrary initial conditions, the mutual benefits of trade are not guaranteed
