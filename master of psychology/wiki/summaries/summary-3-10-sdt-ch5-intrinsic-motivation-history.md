# A Brief History of Intrinsic Motivation — Summary

**Author(s):** Richard M. Ryan and Edward L. Deci
**Type:** Book chapter (Ch. 5 in *Self-Determination Theory: Basic Psychological Needs in Motivation, Development, and Wellness*, 2017)
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **The concept of intrinsic motivation emerged as a critical reaction to behaviorist theories** (operant and drive theories) that could not account for the spontaneous, exploratory, curious behaviors of animals and humans -- behaviors performed without external reinforcement or drive reduction. The very existence of these behaviors undermined the universality claims of both Skinner's operant theory and Hull's drive theory.

2. **Harlow (1950) was the first scientist to use the term "intrinsic motivation"** in his observations that rhesus monkeys would solve puzzles without any external reward, that food-rewarded monkeys were no more efficient than unrewarded ones, and that food rewards actually interfered with puzzle-solving persistence -- a direct precursor to SDT's later findings on reward undermining.

3. **White's (1959) effectance motivation was the pivotal theoretical breakthrough.** White proposed that exploration, play, and mastery behaviors reflect innate psychological tendencies (not drives), producing feelings of "effectance" -- an inherent satisfaction in producing effects on the environment. This concept became the direct theoretical forerunner of SDT's [[competence-need]].

4. **De Charms's (1968) personal causation concept added the autonomy dimension.** De Charms proposed that humans have a primary propensity to be an "origin" (rather than a "pawn") of their behavior -- to experience an internal perceived locus of causality (I-PLOC). This became the basis for SDT's [[autonomy-need]]. His 1976 classroom study demonstrated that teachers who supported students' sense of being origins fostered self-motivation and achievement even in underprivileged settings.

5. **Two definitional traditions for intrinsic motivation emerged from two behavioral traditions:** (a) from operant theory: intrinsically motivated activities are those for which the reward is in the activity itself (not operationally separable); (b) from drive/learning theory: intrinsically motivated activities are those that satisfy innate psychological needs. SDT integrates both perspectives.

## Detailed Notes

### Early Psychological Concepts of Activity

#### Groos (1898)

Groos observed that animals of many varieties engage in spontaneous and unrewarded exercise of their capacities -- rough-and-tumble wrestling, solitary vocalizations, curious manipulations. He argued these forms of play serve the purpose of *exercising capacities* for adaptive advantages in development. As he put it: "animals do not play because they are young, but they have their youth because they must play" (Groos, 1898, p. 67). Groos recognized that play must involve an inherent experience of pleasure, which he attributed to "the feeling of freedom which is closely connected with the joy in being a cause" (Groos, 1901, p. 82). This idea of inherent pleasure in activity is reiterated across SDT's writings and in Csikszentmihalyi's flow concept.

#### Woodworth (1918)

Woodworth's *behavior-primacy theory* emphasized the importance of spontaneous activity connected to the "pleasure of being a cause." He proposed that the ongoing stream of behavior was primarily directed toward having effects on one's environment, and that drive-oriented motives were better thought of as perturbations to this ongoing activity rather than as drivers of it. He posited general motives such as curiosity, constructiveness, and self-assertion that are their own ends. Critically, Woodworth argued that extrinsic motives (such as pursuing a reward) may initiate a behavior, but "only when it is running on its own drive ... can (it) run freely and effectively." This notion was later referred to as *functional autonomy* by Allport (1937). Key SDT precursors from Woodworth: the concept of inherent activity ([[intrinsic-motivation]]), the idea that non-intrinsically-interesting activities can become functionally autonomous (internalization and integration), and the postulate that activities that seem self-rewarding relate to underlying needs.

#### Dewey (1922)

Dewey employed a *functionalist* perspective with a primary role of *interest* in the development of mind and culture. Interest is spawned by novelty and challenge in relation to the already familiar. Pragmatically and dynamically, mind is ever striving to exceed itself. In Dewey's work, one finds intrinsic activity, an inherent growth tendency, and the dialectic between active organisms and social forces that can either diminish or nurture natural activity and growth -- all concepts that find correspondences within SDT. Dewey's work formed the basis of progressive education.

### Behavioral Theories

These early viewpoints were overshadowed by the advent of behaviorism (Thorndike, 1913; Watson, 1913), which emphasized measurement of observable variables and control of behavior through manipulation of external variables.

#### Operant Theory (Skinner)

Operant psychology (Skinner, 1953) focused on response rates as a function of contingencies of *reinforcement*. Reinforcers (positive or negative) increase the rate of responding. When terminated, behavior is expected to return to baseline (*extinction*). A critical but often overlooked element: Skinner acknowledged that each behavior has a *baseline* or *operant level* -- animals explore cages, manipulate levers -- before any reinforcement. These baseline levels are not under the control of reinforcements. This baseline level of responding is, in a muffled form, what SDT thinks of as intrinsic motivation.

**The operant theory implication for intrinsic motivation:** If reinforcers (extrinsic motivators) are additive to baseline-level responding (intrinsic motivation), then when reinforcers are removed, responding should return to its operant level -- implying that intrinsic motivation is not affected by extrinsic motivators. This extrapolation became a major point of contention between operant theory and SDT (Chapter 6).

There is no concept of *rewards* within operant theory (reinforcers are defined functionally), and technically no motivational concepts either. Yet in practice, reinforcers in laboratory studies gain their power because they tie into conditions within the organism that are motivational (hunger, social approval needs). Skinner intuitively knew about needs, even though they were not discussed as such.

#### Drive/Learning Theory (Hull, 1943)

Hull's theory, unlike Skinner's, addressed the *content* of nature -- *why* something is reinforcing. All behaviors can be reduced to four physiological needs: food, water, sex, and freedom from pain. *Drives* (energization from need deprivation) and *reinforcements* (drive reductions that strengthen stimulus-response bonds) are the central concepts. *Secondary reinforcement* occurs when initially neutral stimuli are paired with drive reducers. Unlike operant theory, Hull's theory is falsifiable regarding reinforcement (a reinforcer must reduce a drive).

#### Differences Between the Two

Both explain behavior in terms of external reinforcers separable from the behavior itself. Both view animal and human behavior as essentially an economic transaction -- behavior exchanged for attainment of rewards or avoidance of punishments. However, Hull uses the motivational concept of *drive* while Skinner does not; Hull's theory is testable regarding what reinforces while Skinner's is circular (a reinforcer is defined as whatever strengthens behavior).

### Empirical Challenges to Behaviorist Theories

Observations that raised doubts about behaviorism:
- Infants engage in exploratory behavior soon after birth (Stern, 1985)
- Human adults engage in sport, artistic, or intellectual leisure activities with no identifiable reinforcing conditions
- Many psychologists found it increasingly problematic to attribute all behavior to reinforcement processes

#### Key Anomalous Findings

**Nissen (1930):** Rats would cross an electrified grid (endure pain) to reach a novel maze area. Neither the grid crossing nor the novel space had been paired with reinforcements. Pain was a drive *enhancer* rather than a reducer -- directly counter to Hull's theory.

**Butler (1953):** Rhesus monkeys would learn discrimination problems simply for the opportunity to visually explore the environment, with no drive-related reinforcement. Butler posited an innate drive for visual exploration.

**Montgomery (1955):** Rats given a choice between a maze area and their home nest showed preference for exploration, even when drive-sated. No pairing with primary reinforcement.

**Harlow (1950, 1953b):** The landmark studies. Rhesus monkeys would solve discrimination tasks for the sole reward of being able to manipulate novel objects. This "manipulation drive" was remarkably resistant to extinction. Critically:
- Monkeys reinforced with food for puzzle solving were *no more efficient* than those who solved puzzles without food
- Food-rewarded monkeys tended to *abandon* a puzzle once solved, whereas *unrewarded monkeys were more likely to continue playing* with puzzles for long periods
- The seeming *interference* of rewards with curiosity and exploration is a finding that SDT later extensively documented

Harlow (1950) is the first scientist known to have used the term *intrinsic motivation*. It appeared in the title of a 1950 report: "Learning and satiation of response in intrinsically motivated complex puzzle performance by monkeys" in the *Journal of Comparative Psychology*.

Harlow (1953a) stated in a poignant passage: "An informal survey of neo-behaviorists who are also fathers (or mothers) reveals that all have observed the intensity and omnipresence of the curiosity-investigatory motive in their own children. None of them seriously believes that the behavior derives from a second-order drive. After describing their children's behavior, often with surprising enthusiasm and frequently with the support of photographic records, they trudge off to their laboratories to study, under conditions of solitary confinement, the intellectual processes of rodents" (p. 29).

#### Drive-Theory Accounts of Exploration

Several attempts were made to explain exploratory behaviors within drive-theory:

1. **Anxiety reduction:** Novel stimuli are anxiety-provoking, so exploration reduces anxiety and fear. Fails because: the more obvious response to anxiety would be to *avoid* rather than explore; fear inductions typically *reduce* rather than enhance exploration (Montgomery, 1955); animals facing novel stimuli typically display excitement and interest rather than fear.

2. **Secondary reinforcement:** Exploration has been paired with primary drive reduction in the past. Fails because: young animals exhibit exploratory urges before having a reasonable chance to pair exploration with drive reduction; exploratory behaviors show great resistance to extinction with no pairing with primary reinforcers.

3. **Drive-naming approach:** Positing new drives -- an "exploratory drive" (Montgomery, 1952), "visual-exploration drive" (Butler, 1953), "manipulation drive" (Harlow, 1953b), "boredom drive" (Isaac, 1962). Problem: lacks parsimony, and accepting these as drives would require a major redefinition of drive (they are not based in non-nervous-system-tissue deficits and do not produce consummatory behaviors that result in drive reduction). These tendencies defy extinction, unlike conventional drives.

### An Alternative: White's Effectance Motivation (1959)

White (1959) contributed a seminal paper that cemented a crisis in the dominant paradigms. He concluded that accounts based on physiological needs (drives) were not compelling, nor were accounts based on functionally defined reinforcements. White proposed that exploratory and mastery behaviors reflect *innate psychological tendencies* -- not drives -- and summarized them under the concept of *competence*, described as a motive to produce effects on the environment. He termed this *effectance motivation*.

White suggested that effectance motivation involves the feeling of satisfaction and pleasure in doing something, in being active -- a basic biological endowment. He made the critical point that the adaptive consequences of exploration and play are *not the reason* organisms are active. Rather, they "play and explore because there is something inherently satisfying about it -- not because it is going to have value at some future time" (p. 35). This emphasizes that organisms can have in their natures motivational processes through which growth and development are facilitated because activities can have *experientially rewarding consequences* (Ryan & Deci, 2000d).

White (1963) in his work with psychoanalytic theory argued not for a new instinct or neutralized libidinal energy, but for the recognition of a psychological tendency that operates primarily when drives are relatively quiescent -- a non-drive-derivative tendency and energy for growth. White was attempting to resolve the paradigmatic dilemmas of both behaviorism and psychoanalysis by positing a type of non-derivative growth energy that complemented the drives within each tradition.

White's theory of effectance motivation represents the direct theoretical forerunner of SDT's [[intrinsic-motivation]] and the [[competence-need]] that underlies it. White tended to avoid the concept of "need" because the term had been so closely linked to physiological drive states, but he did suggest that competence-promoting behavior "satisfies an intrinsic need to deal with the environment" (1959, p. 318). This set the stage for subsequent work on [[basic-psychological-needs]].

Hilgard (1987) cited White's (1959) paper as "the end of motivation as a field" and "the death knell of motivation theory." Ryan and Deci disagree: White's paper refocused motivation theory on its proper object -- the active, developing organism with propensities to exceed itself and expand its structures through further activity.

### Psychoanalytic Theory: Parallel Developments

Within psychoanalytic ego psychology, a remarkably parallel struggle was occurring. The problem: Freud's (1923) assumption that all motivational energy is ultimately derived from sexual or erotic drives. How could these libidinal origins account for the play, exploration, and curiosity behaviors central to normal development?

**Hendrick (1942):** Posited an "instinct to master" -- "an inborn drive to do and learn how to do" with a *primary pleasure* in effective action. Parallel to the drive-naming approach in experimental psychology.

**Fenichel (1945):** Proposed anxiety reduction accounts of mastery behaviors -- paralleling similar approaches in Hullian literature. As with drive theory, anxiety reduction did not fit the observable curious, excited demeanor of organisms during exploratory episodes.

**White (1963):** Applied his effectance motivation concept to psychoanalytic theory. Argued for recognition of a psychological tendency operating primarily when drives are quiescent. He suggested the ego has *independent ego energy* -- an inherent energy that underlies adaptation and growth, independent of the id and not derivative of physiological drives. This natural, independent ego energy is essential for understanding developmental progress.

### Optimal Stimulation and Discrepancy Theories (1960s)

Following White's work, two approaches emerged as responses to the problems of behaviorism:

**Optimal Incongruity (Hunt, 1965):** A neo-Piagetian approach. People are attracted to stimuli that are optimally incongruent with existing cognitive structures, then operate to reduce that inconsistency. The assimilation schema is naturally operative and works most effectively with optimally discrepant information. However, this approach did not postulate needs or proximal satisfactions, nor did it provide a satisfactory conceptualization of the energization of behavior.

**Optimal Arousal (Hebb, 1955):** A neo-Hullian approach. The optimal state involves a moderate (rather than minimal) amount of physiological arousal. Behaviors that increase arousal (when below optimal) are strengthened; behaviors that decrease arousal (when above optimal) are strengthened. At the psychological level, Hebb suggested humans find novelty and excitement positively motivating when not overstimulated.

**Berlyne (1967, 1973):** Viewed humans as information-processing systems in continual interaction with the environment. Addressed motivating properties of *collative stimulus properties* (novelty, incongruity) that provide stimulation that is intrinsically motivating when the organism is below its optimal level.

Each of these theories emphasized mastery and assimilation of incongruity/novelty. They are included mainly for historical interest. They converge on intrinsic motivation directed at mastery and assimilation of incongruity or novelty.

### Early SDT and the Significance of Intrinsic Motivation

**Definition:** Intrinsic motivation refers to activities done for their inherent satisfactions -- play, exploration, sport, games, avocations. It exists in the nexus between individuals and activities. Each individual is intrinsically motivated for some activities and not others, in some social contexts and not others. An individual will be intrinsically motivated for an activity to the degree that it is found inherently interesting and enjoyable, which is a function of proximal [[basic-psychological-needs|basic need satisfactions]].

**Two definitional traditions:**
1. **From operant theory:** All behaviors are motivated by operationally separable rewards (reinforcements), so intrinsically motivated activities are those for which the reward is *in the activity itself* (not operationally separable). This led researchers to investigate the characteristics (e.g., optimal challenge) that make activities inherently rewarding.
2. **From drive/learning theory:** All behaviors are motivated by physiological drives and their derivatives, so intrinsically motivated activities are those that provide satisfaction of innate *psychological needs*. This led researchers to explore what basic psychological needs are satisfied by intrinsically motivated behaviors.

#### Needs and Affects

Deci and Ryan (1980a) distinguished the needs for [[competence-need|competence]] (effectance) and [[autonomy-need|autonomy]] as each being integral to intrinsic motivation:
- The concept of a need for competence was a short extrapolation from White's (1959) assertion of an innate tendency toward effectance
- The concept of a need for autonomy was a similarly short extrapolation from de Charms's (1968) proposal that humans exhibit a primary propensity for having an I-PLOC (internal perceived locus of causality) and experiencing personal causation

**Csikszentmihalyi (1975):** Introduced the concept of *flow* -- an experience of absorption with the activity and non-self-conscious enjoyment. Flow occurs when action opportunities are well matched with the individual's capacities. Although Csikszentmihalyi did not explicitly postulate a need for competence, his theorizing was compatible with SDT's need-for-competence component.

**Harter (1978a):** Focused on *perceived competence* as the central element of intrinsically motivated actions across particular domains (cognitive, social, physical). Her work on the positive relations between perceived competence and intrinsic motivation was compatible with SDT's framework.

**De Charms (1968, 1976):** Stressed that to be intrinsically motivated, the individual must feel like an "origin" of action -- have an I-PLOC. His classic 1976 classroom study demonstrated that teachers who supported voice, choice, and engagement while supplying optimal challenge fostered self-motivation and performance, even in underprivileged urban school settings. Much subsequent research supported his view that intrinsic motivation is affected by social contextual conditions that support or undermine the individual's sense of being an origin ([[autonomy-need|autonomy]]).

**Relatedness and intrinsic motivation:** Work with infants and young children showed that children display curiosity and intrinsic exploration when they feel a sense of security with respect to others (Anderson, Manoogian, & Reznick, 1976; Bernier, Matte-Gagne, Belanger, & Whipple, 2014; Frodi, Bridges, & Grolnick, 1985). This suggests [[relatedness-need|relatedness]] is also involved in intrinsically motivated activity. While competence and autonomy are invariably proximally implicated in maintaining intrinsic motivation for a particular activity, relatedness provides the developmental support and security for exploration.

### Concluding Comments

The dual concept of needs for competence and autonomy, derived from White (1959) and de Charms (1968) respectively, were incorporated into SDT as the necessary conditions for supporting intrinsic motivation. This model was used to develop the specific propositions of *cognitive evaluation theory* (CET; Deci & Ryan, 1980a, 1985b) -- the empirically oriented theory of the determinants of intrinsically motivated actions that is the topic of the next two chapters.

The need for competence, however, does not sufficiently account for intrinsic motivation because there are many activities for which we have competence but no interest. De Charms (1968) stressed that the individual must also feel like an origin -- have an I-PLOC, or perceived autonomy. Early experimental work (Deci, 1971; Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett, 1973) verified that intrinsic motivation depends upon perceived autonomy as well as perceived competence.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Operant theory vs. SDT on reward effects:** Operant theory predicts that extrinsic and intrinsic motivation are additive -- removing reinforcers simply returns behavior to its baseline operant level. SDT research (Chapter 6) demonstrates that extrinsic rewards can actually *undermine* intrinsic motivation below baseline -- a finding that directly contradicts operant theory's additivity assumption. Harlow's (1950) observation of food rewards interfering with puzzle-solving persistence was the earliest empirical precursor of this finding.
- **Drive theory vs. psychological needs:** Hull's (1943) drive theory reduces all motivation to four physiological needs. SDT argues for psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) that are not reducible to physiological drives and that do not operate through drive-reduction mechanisms. Unlike drives, [[basic-psychological-needs]] do not require deficiencies to motivate action.
- **SDT and [[deliberate-practice]]:** Ericsson's concept of deliberate practice involves effortful, structured training that is often *not* intrinsically enjoyable. SDT would predict that deliberate practice sustained only through controlled motivation would be less effective long-term than practice that is at least partially autonomously motivated. The integration of deliberate practice into one's identity (identified/integrated regulation) may be the mechanism by which experts sustain it over decades.
