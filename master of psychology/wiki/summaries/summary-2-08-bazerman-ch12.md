# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.11 — Negotiator Cognition

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.11, pp.192-205)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. The [[fixed-pie-bias]] -- the assumption that the other party's interests are diametrically opposed to yours -- is the single most destructive cognitive error in negotiation, blocking the search for mutually beneficial tradeoffs
2. Whether a negotiator frames outcomes as gains or losses (relative to different reference points) determines whether they will be risk-averse or risk-seeking, directly affecting concessionary behavior and the probability of settlement
3. Negotiators consistently overestimate their own value and the probability that their positions will prevail, leading them to miss deals within the positive bargaining zone
4. Self-serving biases cause each party to sincerely believe their preferred outcome is "fair," creating genuine impasses that communication and perspective-taking can help reduce
5. Anchoring effects in negotiation are powerful: first offers, listing prices, and even the order in which possible agreements are presented systematically shift final outcomes

## Detailed Notes

### The Mythical Fixed Pie of Negotiation

The [[fixed-pie-bias]] is the assumption that one's own interests directly conflict with the other party's. When negotiators believe the pie is fixed, they focus entirely on claiming the largest possible share and never search for creative solutions.

**Why it occurs:** Many genuine win-lose situations exist (athletic competitions, school admissions). People generalize these zero-sum expectations to mixed-motive situations where value creation is possible. The claiming component becomes disproportionately salient.

**Cold War example (Rep. Floyd Spence):** "The Russians will not accept a SALT treaty that is not in their best interest, and it seems to me that if it is in their interests, it can't be in our best interest." This reasoning is logically confused -- mutual benefit is precisely the point of negotiation. Yet with hindsight, we see that SALT benefited both sides by reducing defense spending and nuclear risk.

**Thompson (2001):** Even when two sides want the exact same outcome, negotiators often settle on a different outcome or reach impasse, because the [[fixed-pie-bias]] causes what Thompson calls the "incompatibility bias" -- the assumption that one's own interests are at odds with the other party's.

**[[reactive-devaluation]] (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner, & Ross, 1990):** Negotiators tend to devalue concessions made by the other side simply because the other side proposed them. Curhan, Neale, and Ross (2004): negotiators liked a possible outcome MORE after they proposed it and LESS after it was proposed by the other side. "If she is willing to make this concession, the issue must not be very important."

### The Framing of Negotiator Judgment

Whether negotiators view outcomes as gains or losses -- relative to which reference point they adopt -- powerfully shapes their behavior.

**Labor-management example:** Union demands increase from $16 to $18/hour. Management offers $17/hour. If the union frames $17 as a gain (vs. the $16 status quo), they will be risk-averse and accept. If framed as a loss (vs. the $18 goal), they will be risk-seeking and prefer the gamble of arbitration.

**Neale and Bazerman (1985):** Negotiators with positive frames (viewing outcomes as gains) are significantly more likely to make concessions and reach mutually beneficial outcomes than their negatively framed counterparts.

**Which anchor determines the frame?** Consider a union negotiator's available reference points:
1. Last year's wage (positive frame)
2. Management's initial offer (positive frame)
3. Estimate of management's reservation point (could go either way)
4. Union's reservation point (could go either way)
5. Union's publicly announced demand (negative frame)

As the reference point moves from (1) to (5), a $1/hour raise shifts from feeling like a gain to feeling like a loss.

**Strategic implications:**
- To induce concessions, create anchors that lead the other side toward a positive (gains) frame
- When your counterpart has a negative frame, help them see they are adopting a risky strategy in a situation where a sure gain is available
- **For mediators:** Meet each party separately and present different anchors to create risk aversion in both. Also emphasize the realistic risk of the situation to make a sure settlement more attractive than the gamble of continued conflict.

### Escalation of Conflict in Negotiation

The [[escalation-of-commitment]] findings from Chapter 8 apply directly to negotiation.

**1994 MLB strike:** Destroyed approximately $1 billion in value. Owners lost $375M in 1994, $326M in 1995, and $100-200M in 1996. Players lost money, status, and bargaining power. Sebenius and Wheeler (1994) proposed an elegant alternative: continue the season but pool all revenues until agreement, with portions going to charity if settlement was delayed. Neither side was rational enough to adopt it.

**2011 NBA lockout:** Repeating baseball's mistakes, a 202-day lockout cost owners over $1 billion and players over $500 million. Both sides focused on beating the other rather than reaching agreement.

**Diekmann, Tenbrunsel, Shah, Schroth, and Bazerman (1996):** In real-estate negotiations, the price the seller originally paid (a sunk cost) affected both sellers' and buyers' expectations, reservation prices, and final outcomes. Sunk costs should be irrelevant but systematically distort negotiated outcomes.

**Strategic implications:**
- Avoid eliciting bold, firm positional statements that trap the opponent into escalation (Staw, 1981)
- If the other party has taken a rigid position on price, find creative ways to help them concede without losing face (e.g., adding non-price concessions)

### Overestimating Your Value in Negotiation

**Matt Harrington case:** A top 2000 MLB draft prospect (7th pick by Colorado Rockies), Harrington's agent demanded $4.95M. The Rockies offered less, and Harrington refused. Over the next 7 years he was drafted in progressively lower rounds (7th, 58th, 374th, 711th, 36th round, then undrafted), refusing increasingly smaller offers. By 2006 he was earning less than $1,000/month in independent baseball. His BATNA was terrible, yet [[negotiator-overconfidence]] destroyed a career.

**Bazerman and Neale (1982):** In final-offer arbitration studies where there was objectively a 50% chance of either offer being accepted, the average individual estimated a 68% probability that their own offer would be accepted.

**Neale and Bazerman (1985):** "Appropriately confident" negotiators -- those who made accurate assessments -- exhibited more concessionary behavior and were more successful than overly confident negotiators. **Cure:** Seek objective value assessments from a neutral party.

### Self-Serving Biases in Negotiation

Self-serving biases cause each party to sincerely define "fairness" in ways that favor themselves, creating genuine -- not strategic -- impasses.

**Thompson and Loewenstein (1992):** In a simulated labor dispute, self-serving biased attention to available information affected each party's perceptions of what constituted a fair settlement. The magnitude of the bias predicted the length of the resulting strike.

**Babcock, Loewenstein, Issacharoff, and Camerer (1995):** Participants assigned as plaintiffs or defendants in a lawsuit predicted the judge's ruling in self-serving ways. Neither financial incentives for accuracy nor essay-writing about the other side's perspective reduced the bias. The mechanism: people distort their interpretation of evidence -- viewing arguments supporting their own position as more convincing. When roles were assigned AFTER reading the evidence, the bias was substantially reduced (Loewenstein & Moore, 2004).

**Tragedy of the commons connection (Hardin, 1968):** Self-serving biases make common-resource problems worse. Wade-Benzoni, Tenbrunsel, and Bazerman (1996) created a fishery simulation where asymmetric parties exhibited self-serving fairness interpretations. Discussion reduced the bias somewhat.

**Climate change negotiations:** U.S.-China impasse on emissions mirrors self-serving fairness -- each nation believes it deserves less blame and more development rights.

### Anchoring in Negotiation

**Northcraft and Neale (1987):** Real-estate brokers claimed listing prices did not influence their assessments of home value. But when randomly assigned to see listing prices at -11%, -4%, +4%, or +11% of the appraised value, both brokers and students were significantly anchored by the listing price. Students admitted the anchor's influence; brokers flatly denied it.

**Ritov (1996):** Even the order in which possible agreements are presented anchors outcomes. When prices are listed from $1,000 down to $0, negotiations result in higher prices than when listed from $0 up to $1,000.

**Precision matters (Janszewski & Uy, 2008):** Precise first offers (e.g., $4,875) anchor more strongly than round offers ($5,000). Houses with list prices to the nearest hundred dollars sold closer to the list price than those rounded to the nearest thousand.

**Defense against anchoring (Galinsky & Mussweiler, 2001):** Focus on your own alternatives and objectives, not the other side's offer. This neutralizes the anchor's effect.

### Conclusions

Chapters 10 and 11 together constitute Raiffa's "asymmetrically prescriptive/descriptive" approach to negotiation. Chapter 10 provided the prescriptive analytic structure. Chapter 11 identified the systematic cognitive errors that prevent negotiators from following prescriptive advice -- and that create predictable patterns in opponents' behavior that can be anticipated and exploited.
