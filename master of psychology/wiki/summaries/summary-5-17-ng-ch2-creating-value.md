# Negotiation Genius Ch.2 — Creating Value in Negotiation

**Author(s):** [[malhotra|Deepak Malhotra]], [[bazerman|Max Bazerman]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 5
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. Negotiators who focus only on claiming value reach worse outcomes than those who also create value — the pie must be expanded before it is divided.
2. Value creation occurs through three primary mechanisms: logrolling (trading across issues of differential importance), adding issues to the negotiation, and using [[contingent-contracts]] to leverage differences in beliefs about the future.
3. Negotiation is *not* about compromise — compromising across all issues simultaneously destroys value. The goal is the best possible *package deal*, not the best outcome on any single issue.
4. Post-settlement settlements (PSS) are a powerful and underutilized tool — after signing the deal, propose revisiting it to seek Pareto improvements, using the signed agreement as the new BATNA for both parties.

## Detailed Notes

### The Holbrooke UN Negotiation
Ambassador Richard Holbrooke faced a seemingly zero-sum negotiation: reducing U.S. assessments from 25% to 22% while convincing 189 countries to pick up the slack. The breakthrough came from discovering that the *timing* of assessment changes was a separate issue from the *size*. Many countries would accept higher assessments — but not before their next fiscal year. Solution: reduce U.S. assessments immediately, delay other nations' increases until 2002. Both sides got what mattered most. Ted Turner covered the one-year budget shortfall with a $30M donation.

### The Moms.com Case — Creating Value Through Logrolling
A TV syndication negotiation with two issues: licensing fee and runs per episode. The seller (Hollyville) values each run at $250K; the buyer (WCHI) values each run at $800K. When parties compromise at 6 runs, they *burn* $1.1M in value that could have been captured by agreeing to 8 runs and adjusting the licensing fee.

**Key insight:** The licensing fee is a purely distributive (zero-sum) issue — it only determines who captures value. Runs per episode is an *integrative* issue — it creates or destroys value based on the agreement. Logrolling means giving up something you value less (runs) for something you value more (higher fee).

**Value creation progression:**
| Agreement | Seller Value | Buyer Value | Total Created |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|---------------|
| O (original: $5.5M fee, 6 runs) | $2.0M | $1.0M | $3.0M |
| X (revised: $6.5M fee, 8 runs) | $2.5M | $1.6M | $4.1M |
| Y (with Juniors sale at $1.5M) | $3.0M | $2.1M | $5.1M |
| Z (with contingency clause) | $3.5M | $2.6M | $6.1M |

### Creating Value by Adding Issues
More issues = more currency for logrolling. In Moms.com, adding the sale of *Juniors* (a second show) created an additional $1M in value. Neither party needed to make a substantive concession — the additional issue provided new opportunities for mutually beneficial trade.

Potential issues to add when stuck on price: delivery date, financing, quality, contract length, last-look provisions, arbitration clauses, exclusivity, service support, warranties, future business.

### Creating Value Through Contingency Contracts
When Hollyville predicted ratings of 5-6 and WCHI predicted 3-4, rather than argue or compromise, they could structure a contingency clause: if ratings > 5, WCHI pays $1M surcharge; if ratings < 4, WCHI receives $1M rebate. Because each party believes they have a 70% chance of "winning the bet," both parties expect to gain $500K from the clause — a Pareto improvement.

**Three caveats for contingency contracts:**
1. Dangerous if the other party has better information than you
2. Useful only when uncertainty can be resolved objectively (measurable outcomes)
3. Must be *incentive compatible* — the clause should not create perverse incentives for the other party to sabotage outcomes

### Pareto Efficiency
A deal is Pareto efficient when no party can be made better off without making another party worse off. Pareto improvements are changes that make at least one party better off without making anyone worse off. The goal of value creation is to reach a Pareto-efficient outcome — only then should you shift to claiming your share of the created value.

**Warning:** Pareto efficiency says nothing about fairness. A deal where one party gets $1M and the other gets $4.1M can still be Pareto efficient.

### Preparation Strategies for Value Creation
1. **Identify your multiple interests** — think about *all* things you value, not just the obvious issue
2. **Create a scoring system** — weight issues by importance, convert to common metric (points or dollars), rank your priorities
3. **Calculate a package reservation value (PRV)** — a single threshold for the entire deal, not per-issue limits that reduce flexibility
4. **Identify the other party's multiple interests** — discover what they care about that you do not (and vice versa)

### Execution Strategies for Value Creation
1. **Negotiate multiple issues simultaneously** — issue-by-issue negotiation makes each issue zero-sum and prevents logrolling
2. **Make package offers** — propose deals across all issues rather than demands on individual issues
3. **Leverage differences of all types** — different priorities (logrolling), different risk tolerances (insurance), different time preferences (banking), different predictions (contingency contracts), different capabilities (complementary skills)

### Post-Negotiation Strategies (Post-Settlement Settlements)
After signing a deal, propose revisiting it to seek Pareto improvements. The signed agreement serves as the new BATNA — neither party will accept a revision that makes them worse off.

**How to propose a PSS (4 steps):**
1. Acknowledge the progress made in reaching the initial agreement
2. Suggest that aspects could be improved; acknowledge they probably feel the same
3. Offer to think "outside the box" to help the other party
4. Emphasize you are looking for an *improved* agreement, not a new one

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The chapter's emphasis on value creation through information sharing and cooperation exists in productive tension with Chapter 1's emphasis on aggressive anchoring and strategic claiming. The authors explicitly address this: "Even the most self-interested negotiators must rely on others to satisfy their own interests."
- People in close relationships often negotiate *worse* outcomes than strangers because they compromise across all issues to avoid appearing greedy — they destroy value rather than create it.
