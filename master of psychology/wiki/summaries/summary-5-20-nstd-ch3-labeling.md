# Never Split the Difference Ch.3: Don't Feel Their Pain, Label It — Summary

**Author(s):** Chris Voss, Tahl Raz
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 5
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Emotions are the means, not the obstacles.** Traditional negotiation advice says "separate the people from the problem" and keep a poker face. Voss rejects this: when emotions *are* the problem, you cannot separate them. Good negotiators identify and influence emotions rather than denying or ignoring them. The relationship between negotiator and counterpart is essentially therapeutic -- like a psychotherapist with a patient.
2. **[[tactical-empathy]] is empathy on steroids.** It goes beyond understanding the other's perspective (standard empathy) to understanding the feelings *behind* those feelings, bringing attention to both emotional obstacles and potential pathways to agreement. It is not about agreeing, being nice, or giving hugs -- it is about understanding the other side's position so you can influence it.
3. **[[labeling-emotions]] is the core technique.** Identify the other's emotion, then verbalize it with neutral phrases: "It seems like...", "It sounds like...", "It looks like..." Avoid "I" statements ("I'm hearing that...") which center you instead of them. After labeling, go silent and let the label do its work. Labeling an emotion -- applying rational words to a fear -- disrupts its raw intensity (supported by fMRI research from Matthew Lieberman at UCLA showing labeling moves brain activity from the amygdala to rational thinking areas).
4. **Labeling negatives diffuses them; labeling positives reinforces them.** Emotions have two levels: the "presenting" behavior (surface) and the "underlying" feeling (the driver). Good negotiators label the underlying emotion. When negatives are brought into the open and labeled, the amygdala's fear response begins to soften.
5. **The [[accusation-audit]] is labeling taken to the strategic level.** Before a difficult negotiation, list every terrible thing your counterpart could say about you, then say it first. This "takes the sting out" (a technique defense lawyers use in opening statements). Because the accusations often sound exaggerated when spoken aloud, the other party is compelled to claim the opposite is true. Anna's case: by opening with ABC Corp's biggest grievances ("You may feel like we have treated you unfairly"), she turned a hostile negotiation into a $1 million win.
6. **Clear the road before advertising the destination.** Label fears first to clear obstacles, then present solutions. Fear is the amygdala's domain -- the faster you interrupt it with labels, the faster you generate safety, well-being, and trust.

## Detailed Notes

### I. The Harlem High-Rise Standoff (1998)

Voss is head of the NYC FBI Crisis Negotiation Team. Three heavily armed fugitives (who had been in a shoot-out with a rival gang days earlier) are holed up on the 27th floor of a Harlem high-rise. No phone number to call in -- so Voss speaks through the apartment door for six straight hours using his [[late-night-fm-dj-voice]], rotating with two FBI trainees.

The repeated message: "It looks like you don't want to come out. It seems like you worry that if you open the door, we'll come in with guns blazing. It looks like you don't want to go back to jail."

After six hours of radio silence, the apartment door opens. All three fugitives come out. When Voss asks why, they answer: "We didn't want to get caught or get shot, but you calmed us down. We finally believed you wouldn't go away, so we just came out."

### II. Tactical Empathy Defined

Empathy = "the ability to recognize the perspective of a counterpart, and the vocalization of that recognition." It is paying attention to another human being, asking what they are feeling, and making a commitment to understanding their world. Empathy is **not** agreeing, sympathizing, or being nice.

**[[tactical-empathy]]** goes one step beyond: understanding the feelings and mindset of another *in the moment* and hearing what is *behind* those feelings, so you increase your influence in all moments that follow. It brings attention to both emotional obstacles and potential pathways to agreement. "It's emotional intelligence on steroids."

**Neural resonance:** fMRI research at Princeton shows that when people pay close attention, their brains align with the speaker's brain (neural resonance). Good listeners can actually anticipate what the speaker will say before they say it.

### III. Labeling

**[[labeling-emotions]]** is a way of validating someone's emotion by acknowledging it. Give someone's emotion a name and you show you identify with how they feel. It gets you close to someone without asking about external factors.

**The labeling formula:**
- "It seems like..."
- "It sounds like..."
- "It looks like..."
- **Never** "I'm hearing that..." (the word "I" centers you, not them, and raises their guard)

**After the label:** Go silent. Let the label work. Resist the temptation to elaborate or follow up with a question. A label's power is that it invites the other person to reveal themselves.

**If they disagree with the label:** Step back and say, "I didn't say that was what it was. I just said it seems like that."

**Spotting emotions:** Pay attention to the trinity of "words, music, and dance" -- verbal content, tone of voice, and body language. Watch for changes when you mention specific topics.

**Neuroscience support:** Matthew Lieberman (UCLA) -- brain imaging shows that when people label an emotion (apply rational words to a fear), activity moves from the amygdala (fear center) to the areas governing rational thinking. Labeling disrupts the raw intensity of the emotion.

### IV. Neutralize the Negative, Reinforce the Positive

**Presenting vs. underlying emotion:** A grumpy grandfather at a family dinner is *presenting* crankiness, but the *underlying* emotion is loneliness from feeling left out. Label the underlying emotion: "We don't see each other all that often. It seems like you feel like we don't pay any attention to you."

**On anger:** Anger is rarely productive -- it releases stress hormones, disrupts evaluation, blinds you to the fact that you're angry, and gives a false sense of confidence. Negative feelings should not be ignored but should be teased out through labeling.

**Diffusing a mistake:** Voss entered Canada without "country clearance" (notifying the top FBI official). He called and opened with: "Bless me, Father, for I have sinned." By naming the negative dynamic immediately, he diffused it. The official granted clearance.

**The fastest way to establish a working relationship:** Acknowledge the negative and diffuse it.

### V. The Washington Redskins Case Study

TJ, an assistant controller at the Washington Redskins, needed to collect from 40 delinquent season ticket holders during a terrible economy and a terrible season. The original script was aggressive, impersonal, "me, me, me" from TJ -- no empathy. No one called back.

TJ rewrote the script using labeling: "YOUR Washington Redskins" (ownership language), "The home-field advantage created by you each and every Sunday at FedEx Field does not go unnoticed" (recognizing their contribution), "In these difficult times, we understand our fans have been hit hard and we are here to work with you" (labeling the economic pain). Every ticket holder called back and set up payment plans.

### VI. Clear the Road Before Advertising the Destination

The amygdala generates fear in reaction to threats. Label fears to "bathe them in sunlight, bleaching them of their power." In the Harlem case, Voss didn't say "It seems like you want us to let you go" (obvious but not emotionally resonant). He went right at the amygdala: "It seems like you don't want to go back to jail."

**The Girl Scout fundraiser:** A student's usual donors wrote easy checks -- until she met an immovable donor who rejected every project. Instead of pushing harder, she labeled: "I'm sensing some hesitation with these projects." The donor exclaimed she wanted to support direct Girl Scout programming specifically. A second label -- "It seems that you are really passionate about this gift and want to find the right project reflecting the opportunities and life-changing experiences the Girl Scouts gave you" -- uncovered the real driver: the donor's own childhood Girl Scout memories. The "difficult" woman signed a blank check: "You understand me. I trust you'll find the right project."

**Key insight:** Labels help uncover and identify the primary emotion driving almost all of your counterpart's behavior -- the emotion that, once acknowledged, seems to miraculously solve everything else.

### VII. The Accusation Audit

**[[accusation-audit]]:** Before a difficult negotiation, list every terrible thing your counterpart *could* say about you, then say them aloud before your counterpart can. This is "taking the sting out" -- defense lawyers do it in opening statements.

**The classroom exercise:** Voss's "sixty seconds or she dies" icebreaker. Instead of asking for volunteers (which triggers anxiety), he labels the fear preemptively: "In case you're worried about volunteering to role-play with me in front of the class, I want to tell you in advance... it's going to be horrible." He always ends up with more volunteers than he needs.

**Anna's $1 million negotiation:** Anna represents a major government contractor whose subcontractor (ABC Corp.) has been underperforming but feels entitled. Anna needs to cut ABC's pay from 5.5 to 3 people while improving the relationship.

Preparation: Anna and partner Mark list every negative charge ABC could level -- "You are the typical prime contractor trying to force out the small guy," "You promised us we would have all this work and you reneged." They roleplay, practice the labels, plan meaningful pauses.

At the meeting, Anna opens by acknowledging ABC's biggest gripes: "We understand that we brought you on board with the shared goal of having you lead this work. You may feel like we have treated you unfairly, and that we changed the deal significantly since then." ABC nods emphatically.

By labeling ABC's fears and asking for input, Anna elicits that ABC expected high-profit work. This gives Mark the opening to explain that the client's new demands have turned profits into losses. Anna then labels the biggest accusation: "It sounds like you think we are the big, bad prime contractor trying to push out the small business." Angela (ABC rep) responds: "No, no, we don't think that."

Result: Contract adjusted, ABC's payout cut, Anna's firm saved $1 million. Angela's closing remark: "This is not a good situation but we appreciate the fact that you are acknowledging what happened, and we don't feel like you are mistreating us. And you are not the 'Big Bad Prime.'"

### VIII. Get a Seat on a Sold-Out Flight (Ryan's Story)

Ryan B. needs to get from Dallas to Austin for a critical Friday-morning contract signing, but a lightning storm has delayed his connections. At the gate, an angry couple is yelling at gate agent Wendy. Ryan uses the heated exchange to his advantage:

1. **Labels the negative:** "Hi, Wendy, I'm Ryan. It seems like they were pretty upset." (Establishes empathetic connection after the hostile encounter)
2. **Mirrors:** "The weather?" -- encouraging Wendy to elaborate about the delays
3. **Labels again:** "It seems like it's been a hectic day." Wendy reveals "irate consumers" and a sold-out football weekend
4. **Mirrors:** "The big game?" / "Booked solid?" -- drawing out information
5. **Labels empathy for her situation:** "Well, it seems like you've been handling the rough day pretty well"
6. **Labels his situation + asks:** "It seems like this flight is likely booked solid, but with what you said, maybe someone affected by the weather might miss this connection. Is there any possibility a seat will be open?"

Result: In under two minutes, Wendy prints Ryan a boarding pass and upgrades him to Economy Plus.

**Pattern:** Label, tactical empathy, label. And only then a request.

## New Concepts Introduced

- [[labeling-emotions]] -- identifying and verbalizing the other person's emotions using "It seems like..." / "It sounds like..."
- [[accusation-audit]] -- listing every terrible thing the other side could say about you and saying it first

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Extends [[feelings-conversation]] (Stone/Patton/Heen):** *Difficult Conversations* identifies the feelings conversation as one of three layers. Voss goes further by making emotions not just a layer to acknowledge but the primary tactical lever. Both approaches emphasize that feelings must be surfaced -- but Voss's approach is more directive (you label *for* them) whereas Stone/Patton/Heen emphasize the other party expressing their own feelings.
- **Aligns with [[emotional-intelligence]]:** Labeling maps closely to the "appraisal and expression of emotion in others" branch of the Salovey/Mayer EI model.
