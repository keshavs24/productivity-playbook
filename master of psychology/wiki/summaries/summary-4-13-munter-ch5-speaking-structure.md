# Guide to Managerial Communication Ch.5: Speaking — Verbal Structure — Summary

**Author(s):** Mary Munter, Lynn Hamilton
**Source:** *Guide to Managerial Communication*, 10th Edition (Pearson, 2014), Chapter 5
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Three speaking situations require fundamentally different structures.** Tell/sell presentations (you speak most, purpose is to inform or persuade), Q&A sessions (you react to the audience), and consult/join meetings (your audience speaks most, purpose is to discuss or decide). Each requires different preparation and different verbal techniques.
2. **Openings must grab attention and provide a preview.** The "grabber" or "hook" in the first minute is essential because you have imposed a time and place on your audience. The preview (agenda/roadmap) is "without a doubt, the most important part of your presentation" -- it lets listeners understand and remember what follows.
3. **Spoken transitions must be longer and more explicit than written ones.** Listeners cannot reread, so use "backward look/forward look" transitions between every section. What feels repetitive to the speaker is clarifying for the listener.
4. **The Audience Memory Curve applies even more strongly to speaking.** Listeners remember beginnings and endings. The closing should be a summary (tell), action steps (sell), or a circle-back to the opening -- never just "thank you" or a trailing "I guess that's about it."
5. **Consult/join meetings require preparation, participation management, and follow-up.** Set agendas in advance, delegate roles (facilitator, timer, scribe), use open-ended questions and minimal encouragers during the meeting, and close with explicit decisions and action plans.

## Detailed Notes

### I. Tell/Sell Presentations

#### 1. Preparing What to Say

Structure every tell/sell presentation with: (1) effective opening, (2) preview, (3) clearly demarcated main points, and (4) effective closing.

**Effective openings** -- choose based on audience analysis:
- *Tell them why you're speaking* -- what they'll learn or what you hope they'll do
- *Grab their attention* -- provocative question, problem definition, promise, story, vivid image, striking example or statistic
- *Show them WIIFT* -- "what's in it for them" (connects to [[audience-analysis]])
- *Build your credibility* -- introduce yourself, establish expertise
- *Use humor with caution* -- only if it fits your personality, is appropriate and inoffensive, and relates to the topic

**The preview** is the single most important structural element. Unlike readers who can skim headings, listeners have no idea what you will cover unless you tell them. The preview should state what you will discuss and in what order, alert the audience to WIIFT, and provide a Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF).

**State main points clearly** -- three guidelines:
- **Limit main points** to a maximum of about five (people can't process more)
- **Use strong transitions** -- longer and more explicit than in writing. Not just "Second, ..." but "The second recommendation is ..." or "Let's move on to the second recommendation."
- **Use backward look/forward look transitions** between every major section: "Now that we have looked at the three elements of the marketing plan (backward look), let's turn to the financial implications of that plan (forward look)."

**Keep interest high** every 10-20 minutes (the length of an audience attention span):
- Include stories, analogies, examples -- not just numbers
- Involve them -- rhetorical questions, show of hands, promise of later input
- Get them to speak or write -- small group breakouts, note-taking
- Recapture attention -- flag importance ("the main idea to remember from this slide is..."), remind of WIIFT
- Do something unexpected -- change delivery, mention names, walk closer, show unusual visual

**Effective closings:**
- *Summary* -- for tell presentations; summarize main points
- *Action steps* -- for sell presentations; "close the sale" with specific next steps and remind of WIIFT
- *Circle back to opening* -- refer to the rhetorical question, image, or story from your opening
- *If ending with Q&A* -- add a second closing after the Q&A so you get the last word
- *If running out of time* -- concentrate on main points and summary slide only; don't rush through everything

#### 2. Remembering Your Content

Do not read from a document (boring, dull vocal style) or memorize word-for-word (causes focus on recall rather than connection).

- **Prepare limited notes** -- short phrases, large lettering, lots of white space
- **"Own" your content** -- practice explaining points in different ways with colleagues or friends; once you can explain without exact words, you've made the point your own
- **Connect notes to slides** -- use "Notepage" and "Presenter View" features to link your outline to corresponding visuals

### II. Questions and Answers

**When to take questions:**
- *At the end* -- maintains control of schedule and flow, but risks losing audience attention and creating awkward situations when important people interrupt
- *Throughout* -- more meaningful questions, more immediate feedback, but can derail the presentation

**How to take questions:**
- *Prepare in advance* -- anticipate possible questions; bring extra information; consider having a colleague role-play a hostile audience member
- *Show understanding* -- listen carefully, paraphrase complex questions, maintain eye contact, do not interrupt
- *Stick to your objective* -- answer the question but stay on message; divert tangential questions back to main ideas
- *Keep everyone involved* -- call on people from various locations; maintain eye contact with full audience, not just the questioner

**When you don't know the answer:**
- Admit it honestly; suggest where to find the answer; offer to follow up
- If you need thinking time: (1) Repeat the question, (2) Turn it around ("How would *you* deal with this?"), (3) Turn it outward ("How would the rest of you...?"), (4) Reflect ("Good question. Let's think about that."), (5) Write the question on a visual aid as you think

**Handling challenging questions:**
- *Confusing questions* -- paraphrase to refocus
- *Controlling questions* (really statements of opinion) -- thank them, paraphrase, proceed; decide whether to regain control or shift focus to their needs
- *Hostile questions* -- identify the hostility, take a deep breath, acknowledge the feeling ("I understand you feel upset about this"), answer nonemotionally, find common ground, or agree to disagree

**The backchannel** (audience tweeting/texting during presentations):
- Decide in advance: reject it, ignore it, or accept and leverage it
- If accepting: make ideas Twitter-friendly (3-5 bite-sized chunks), create a hashtag, arrange for a session moderator, take periodic Twitter breaks instead of traditional Q&A

### III. Consult/Join Meetings

Unlike tell/sell (you do most speaking) or Q&A (you react), consult/join meetings involve extensive group interaction. Purpose is to discuss or decide.

#### 1. Preparation Before the Meeting

- **Set the agenda** carefully and in advance. Agenda should state the meeting objective and let participants know how to prepare and what they will contribute. Meetings should be reserved for situations requiring group discussion -- not routine announcements or presenting finalized ideas.
- **Delegate roles:**
  - *Facilitator* -- may ask someone else to facilitate if you want to participate actively
  - *Timer* -- assign separately since it's hard to concentrate on discussion and time simultaneously
  - *Minutes writer* -- appoint someone to document
  - *Scribe* -- have someone record participant ideas on a board or chart in public view (frees you to focus on discussion)

#### 2. Participation During the Meeting

**Opening the meeting:**
- Start on time
- Explain the agenda
- Get agreement on ground rules (e.g., "we will start and stop on time," "we will not interrupt," "all information is confidential")
- Involve people early -- the earlier participants speak, the more likely they continue to participate

**During the meeting:**
- Ask open-ended questions ("How should we attack this problem?" not "Do you agree?")
- Paraphrase responses concisely ("So what you're suggesting is...")
- Record responses publicly on a board or chart
- Use "minimal encouragers" -- "I see," "OK," "uh huh" -- to keep discussion going
- Handle disagreement carefully -- disagree with ideas, not people; avoid premature negative judgments ("That won't work because...")
- Don't talk too much -- you called the meeting for their input

#### 3. Decision Making and Follow-Up

**Decision methods:**
- *Majority vote or one person decides* -- fast but may leave people feeling excluded, risking sabotaged implementation
- *Consensus* -- not unanimity but a compromise everyone can live with. Reached by discussion, not vote. No participant has veto power. More time-consuming but builds group commitment.

**Follow-up:**
- Create a permanent record (minutes) documenting issues discussed, alternatives considered, decisions reached, and action plan
- Agree on an action plan with (1) actions to be taken, (2) responsible person(s), and (3) time frame

### IV. Other Speaking Situations

**Manuscript speaking:** Use "spoken style" -- avoid phrases no one would say in conversation; use large font, short sentences; leave bottom third of page blank; finish sentences before page breaks.

**Impromptu speaking:** (1) Anticipate -- try to avoid truly impromptu situations, (2) Relate to your experience, (3) Keep remarks short, (4) Start with a preview if possible.

**Online presentations:** Plan with technician for tech checks; start early with welcome slide; provide visual clues if no webcam; speak naturally but slightly slower and louder; look at webcam, not screen, for eye contact; assume you're always "on stage."

**Media interviews:** Cultivate relationships with reporters; analyze both the reporter and their audience; plan responses as "sound bites"; use "bridging" to move from their questions to your message points.

**Team presentations:** Coordinate structure and transitions between speakers; ensure visual consistency.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The emphasis on controlling the backchannel during presentations creates a tension with [[autonomy-support]] principles. Munter offers strategies to manage audience digital behavior (including rejecting devices entirely), which could undermine the very audience engagement she advocates for in consult/join situations. The recommendation to "accept and leverage" the backchannel is most aligned with autonomy-supportive principles.
- Munter's advocacy for consensus decision-making in meetings aligns with [[autonomy-support]] and [[intrinsic-motivation]] research -- people who feel heard and involved in decisions show greater commitment to implementation. However, Munter treats consensus as one option among several, while SDT would predict it reliably produces better outcomes for complex decisions requiring buy-in.
