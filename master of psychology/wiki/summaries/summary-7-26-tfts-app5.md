# Think Faster Talk Smarter App.5: Feedback That Doesn't Flop — Summary

**Author:** Matt Abrahams
**Source:** *Think Faster, Talk Smarter* (2023), Application #5
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Use the 4 I's framework for feedback:** Information (concrete observations), Impact (how it affects you), Invitation (collaborative request for change), Implications (positive/negative consequences of acting or not).
2. **Frame feedback as an invitation to solve a problem together**, not as passing judgment. This shifts the dynamic from hierarchical to collaborative, reducing defensiveness.
3. **Word choice and tone dramatically change how feedback lands.** Using "we" and posing questions creates collaboration; declarations create authority. Choose the appropriate register for the situation.
4. **Balance, timing, context, and focus are essential.** Lead with something positive. Deliver timely feedback. Respect the setting. Limit to 1-2 changes per session.

## Detailed Notes

### The 4 I's Framework
- **Information:** Present concrete, specific, verifiable observations about the behavior. Stick to facts, leave out emotion. Delineate what you're NOT going to discuss.
- **Impact:** Explain how the action affects you personally using first-person ("I think," "I feel"). Own your reactions to reduce defensiveness.
- **Invitation:** Make a specific, concise request. Can be framed as a question ("How can we ensure...?") or a declaration ("I encourage you to..."). Questions invite deeper participation; declarations add urgency.
- **Implications:** Detail positive and/or negative consequences. "By ensuring the NPS scores are in the board deck, we'll demonstrate our commitment to customers." Or: "If we don't find a way to improve your test scores, you might find it hard to make the grades required to play your sport."

### Tips for Better Feedback
1. **Prepare** -- ask yourself: Why might they behave this way? What do we gain/lose by giving feedback? What behavior would we *like* to see?
2. **Be timely** -- speak up soon after the triggering event, but allow time for strong emotions to settle first
3. **Respect context** -- deliver in person when possible; choose a time/place conducive to receptive listening
4. **Adopt the right tone** -- collaborative (question-based) vs. directive (declaration-based) depending on urgency and relationship
5. **Stay balanced** -- lead with genuine positive feedback before critical feedback
6. **Monitor emotions** -- if the recipient becomes defensive or emotional, acknowledge without naming the emotion ("I can hear in your voice how important this is to you")
7. **Stay focused** -- limit to 1-2 key changes; too much feedback overwhelms

### Case Study: Alice's Teaching Evaluations
A Stanford PhD student teaching at an Ivy League school received poor evaluations. Rather than judging, Abrahams applied the 4 I's: pointed out specific syllabus issues (Information), shared that she needn't feel threatened (Impact), provided his own syllabus as a model (Invitation), and suggested acting on feedback would improve evaluations (Implications). Alice implemented changes and received much better evaluations the next semester.
