# Contagious: Introduction — Why Things Catch On — Summary

**Author:** Jonah Berger
**Source:** *Contagious: Why Things Catch On* (2013), Introduction
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Virality isn't born, it's made.** Even ordinary products can become contagious if engineered correctly. The Blendtec "Will It Blend?" campaign turned a boring blender into a viral sensation with a $50 budget, generating 300 million views and a 700% increase in retail sales.
2. **Quality, price, and advertising are insufficient explanations** for why things catch on. Baby names like Olivia vs. Rosalie show that popularity cannot be explained by objective superiority, price, or ad spend.
3. **Word of mouth is the primary driver of 20-50% of all purchasing decisions.** It is more persuasive than advertising (because friends are more credible and more targeted) and more prevalent than most realize — only 7% of word of mouth happens online; the vast majority is offline, face-to-face conversation.
4. **The message matters more than the messenger.** Contrary to Malcolm Gladwell's *Tipping Point* thesis about influential "mavens, connectors, and salesmen," [[berger]] argues that contagious content spreads regardless of who is doing the talking because the *content itself* is inherently shareable.
5. **Six key STEPPS drive contagiousness.** After analyzing hundreds of contagious messages, Berger and colleagues identified six principles — [[social-currency]], [[environmental-triggers]], [[high-arousal-emotions]], [[behavioral-residue]], [[practical-value]], and [[trojan-horse-stories]] — that recur across viral products, ideas, and behaviors. Together they form the [[stepps-framework]].

## Detailed Notes

### The $100 Cheesesteak — Opening Case
Howard Wein moved to Philadelphia in 2004 to launch Barclay Prime, a luxury boutique steakhouse. Knowing that good food alone wouldn't cut it (more than 25% of restaurants fail within twelve months; 60% within three years), Wein created a $100 cheesesteak — Kobe beef on a house-made brioche with lobster tail, truffles, and Veuve Clicquot champagne. The sandwich became a conversation piece that generated massive word of mouth. Media coverage followed (USA Today, Wall Street Journal, Discovery Channel, David Letterman). The restaurant thrived.

The cheesesteak worked because it hit multiple STEPPS: it provided [[social-currency]] (remarkable and impressive to share), was [[environmental-triggers|Triggered]] (cheesesteaks are everywhere in Philly), was [[high-arousal-emotions|Emotional]] (surprising), was [[practical-value|Practically Valuable]] (useful information about a great steakhouse), and was wrapped in a [[trojan-horse-stories|Story]].

### Why Quality, Price, and Advertising Fall Short
- **Quality:** Helps but doesn't explain differential success. Many better products lose to inferior ones.
- **Price:** Cheaper isn't always more popular. Baby names are free, YouTube videos are free — yet some catch on and others don't.
- **Advertising:** Correlation between ad spend and popularity is weak. Most viral YouTube videos have no advertising push behind them.

### Social Transmission — The Power of Word of Mouth
- People share more than 16,000 words per day. Every hour there are more than 100 million conversations about brands.
- Word of mouth is more effective than advertising for two reasons: (1) it is more *persuasive* (friends are trusted more than ads), and (2) it is more *targeted* (people share information with those most likely to find it relevant).
- Customers referred by friends spend more, shop faster, and are more profitable overall.
- Only 7% of word of mouth happens online — a fact that surprises most people who guess around 50%. Offline conversations are more prevalent, more in-depth, and potentially more impactful.

### Studying Social Influence — Berger's Background
[[berger]] is a marketing professor at the Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania. After reading Gladwell's *The Tipping Point* and finding it descriptive but lacking in explanatory mechanisms, Berger spent more than a decade conducting research on what drives social influence. His research examined why certain *New York Times* articles go viral, why some products generate more word of mouth, why certain political messages spread, and when negative publicity helps vs. hurts sales.

### The Six Principles of Contagiousness (STEPPS)
Products or ideas that are **S**ocial Currency-laden, **T**riggered, **E**motional, **P**ublic, **P**ractically Valuable, and wrapped in **S**tories are more likely to spread. These principles are independent — not all six are required, and different principles are easier to apply in different contexts. They function more like "salad toppings" than a recipe — more is better, but any combination can work.

- **[[social-currency]]:** People share things that make them look good — smart, cool, in-the-know
- **[[environmental-triggers]]:** Top of mind leads to tip of tongue. Environmental cues remind people to talk about things
- **[[high-arousal-emotions]]:** When we care, we share. Emotional content gets shared more
- **[[behavioral-residue]]:** Making the private public. Observable behaviors are easier to imitate
- **[[practical-value]]:** News you can use. People share useful information to help others
- **[[trojan-horse-stories]]:** Information travels under the guise of idle chatter. Stories are vessels for messages

## Contradictions or Tensions
- **Berger vs. Gladwell on influentials:** Berger explicitly challenges Gladwell's *Tipping Point* thesis that special influential people drive social epidemics. Berger argues the empirical evidence shows little support for the "influentials" hypothesis — the message matters more than the messenger. This contrasts with [[social-proof]], which emphasizes who is doing a behavior, not what the behavior is. However, Berger doesn't deny that some people have more friends — he simply argues this doesn't make them disproportionately influential in spreading information.
