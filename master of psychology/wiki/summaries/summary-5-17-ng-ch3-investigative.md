# Negotiation Genius Ch.3 — Investigative Negotiation

**Author(s):** [[malhotra|Deepak Malhotra]], [[bazerman|Max Bazerman]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 5
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. Investigative negotiation is both a mind-set and a methodology — negotiators approach deals the way a detective approaches a crime scene, seeking to learn as much as possible about the situation and the people involved.
2. The seven principles of investigative negotiation provide a systematic framework for uncovering hidden information, resolving impasses, and creating value that positional bargaining cannot achieve.
3. The difference between "selling" and "negotiating" is critical — selling focuses on the merits of your case, while negotiating also focuses on the other side's interests, needs, priorities, constraints, and perspective.
4. Five strategies for eliciting information from reticent negotiators build on each other in escalating sequence.

## Detailed Notes

### The Chris Story — The Power of "Why"
A Fortune 500 executive named Chris was called in to break a deadlocked negotiation between his firm and a small European supplier over exclusivity terms. His U.S. colleagues had tried sweetening the deal with higher prices and guaranteed minimum orders, but the supplier refused. Chris asked one question: "Why won't you provide exclusivity?" The supplier explained he had to honor a commitment to sell 250 pounds annually to his cousin. Solution: exclusivity with an exception for a few hundred pounds to the cousin. Deal closed immediately.

### The Seven Principles of Investigative Negotiation

#### Principle 1: Don't Just Ask What — Ask *Why*
- Too much focus on *what* people want distracts from discovering *why* they want it
- Chris succeeded by shifting from *what* (exclusivity vs. no exclusivity) to *why* (protecting a promise to his cousin)
- Holbrooke discovered UN member states resisted assessment increases not because of the amount but because of the *timing* relative to their budget cycles
- Shikha's cab ride: asking the off-duty cabdriver *why* he refused revealed their destinations were blocks apart — both parties' interests were served without any concession

#### Principle 2: Seek to Reconcile Interests, Not Demands
- Demands may be incompatible, but *interests* are often entirely reconcilable
- 2000 U.S. presidential election: Gore wanted electoral votes, Nader wanted popular votes — vote-trading across battleground and safe states could serve both interests

#### Principle 3: Create Common Ground with Uncommon Allies
- Brandenburger and Nalebuff's concept of *co-opetition*: it is possible to simultaneously cooperate and compete
- Those who view relationships as one-dimensional ("He is my enemy") forgo opportunities for value creation

#### Principle 4: Interpret Demands as Opportunities
- Builder facing last-minute penalty clause for late completion: reframed the demand as revealing the buyer valued *early* completion. Proposed *higher* penalties for delays in exchange for a bonus for early completion — both parties were happier.
- Ask: "What can I *learn* from this demand? What does it tell me about their needs?"

#### Principle 5: Don't Dismiss Anything as "Their Problem"
- HomeStuff/Kogs negotiation: the supplier's constraint was high shipping costs ($1.2M), not manufacturing. HomeStuff had favorable shipping terms and could solve it for $500K, saving $700K for both parties.
- In negotiation, their problem quickly becomes *your* problem

#### Principle 6: Don't Let Negotiations End with a Rejection of Your Offer
- Linda lost a deal, then called the VP of purchasing to ask *why*. She learned the client valued features she had stripped out. She submitted a revised offer and won the deal a week later.
- Negotiations should end with a "yes" or with an *explanation* of "why not"
- "Why not" is often as important a question as "why"

#### Principle 7: Understand the Difference Between "Selling" and "Negotiating"
- Selling = making a pitch, arguing the merits of your case
- Negotiating = also focusing on the other side's interests, needs, priorities, constraints
- Negotiation geniuses understand that their ability to structure value-maximizing deals hinges on their ability to *listen*, not just to persuade

### Five Strategies for Eliciting Information from Reticent Negotiators

1. **Build trust and share information** — speak their language, increase economic/social ties, build trust when you are *not* negotiating, actually *be* trustworthy
2. **Ask questions — especially if surprised or skeptical** — use indirect questions that yield the same information as direct ones
3. **Give away some information** — leverage reciprocity; share *relative* priorities without minimizing *absolute* importance of any issue
4. **Negotiate multiple issues simultaneously** — forces the other party to implicitly reveal which issues they prioritize
5. **Make multiple offers simultaneously** — two offers of *equal value to you* that differ in structure; the other party's preference reveals their priorities
