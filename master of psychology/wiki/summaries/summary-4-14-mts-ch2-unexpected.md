# Made to Stick: Chapter 2 (Unexpected) — Summary

**Author(s):** Chip Heath and Dan Heath
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Getting attention requires breaking patterns; keeping it requires creating curiosity.** The chapter addresses two sequential problems: (1) how to grab attention — via surprise (breaking schemas), and (2) how to sustain it — via interest (opening knowledge gaps). Surprise is fleeting; interest endures. See [[schema-breaking]], [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]].
2. **Surprise = schema failure + postdictability.** Surprise is triggered when our mental "guessing machines" (schemas) fail. But for surprise to be *satisfying* rather than *frustrating*, it must be "post-dictable" — the twist makes sense in retrospect, even though it was not predicted. PHRAUG (surprising + post-dictable = "Oh!") vs. HENSION (surprising + not post-dictable = frustration). See [[schema-breaking]].
3. **The gap theory of curiosity drives sustained interest.** George Loewenstein (Carnegie Mellon, 1994) proposed that curiosity arises when we feel a gap in our knowledge. Gaps cause pain — like an itch that needs scratching. The trick is to *open* gaps before *closing* them with the answer. See [[gap-theory-of-curiosity]].
4. **Common sense is the enemy of sticky messages.** When messages sound like common sense, they float in one ear and out the other. The communicator's job is to expose the parts of the message that are *uncommon* sense — the counterintuitive implications that break people's existing schemas.
5. **Unexpected ideas create knowledge gaps that sustain motivation for years.** Sony's "pocketable radio" (1953) and JFK's "man on the moon" speech (1961) both created surprise, insight, and massive knowledge gaps — audacious but not insurmountable goals that motivated thousands of people for years.

## Detailed Notes

### A. The Flight Safety Announcement (pp. 73-74)

- Flight attendant Karen Wood made the most boring message imaginable — the FAA safety briefing — engaging by breaking the pattern with humor: "If you haven't been in an automobile since 1965, the proper way to fasten your seat belt is..."
- Scattered applause from passengers who normally ignore the announcement
- Breaking a pattern is the most basic way to get attention — humans adapt to consistent stimulation (the hum of an air conditioner, the sight of a bookshelf) and notice only when something *changes*

### B. Surprise and Interest: Two Essential Emotions (pp. 75-76)

- *Surprise* gets our attention — fleeting, triggered by unexpected facts or plot twists (Great Wall visible from space, 10% of your brain)
- *Interest* keeps our attention — sustained, driven by conspiracy theories, gossip, unfolding mysteries
- The chapter explores both: first how to *get* attention (surprise), then how to *keep* it (interest/curiosity)

### C. The Enclave Minivan Ad (pp. 77-78)

- The Ad Council's Enclave minivan commercial sets up a schema of a typical car commercial (happy family, features, suburban streets) then violates it with a horrific broadside collision
- "Didn't see that coming?" — "No one ever does." — "Buckle up... Always."
- The ad is unexpected in two ways: it violates the car-commercial schema *and* the real-life schema of safe neighborhood trips
- "Our schemas are like guessing machines" — they predict what will happen. When they fail, surprise grabs our attention so we can repair them

### D. The Surprise Brow (pp. 79-80)

- Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen coined "the surprise brow" — a cross-culturally consistent facial expression (raised eyebrows, widened eyes, dropped jaw, slack muscles)
- The surprise brow is the body's "emergency override" — it widens our field of vision, forces us to stop moving and talking so we can take in new information
- Surprise jolts us to attention. It is triggered when our schemas fail. It prepares us to understand *why* the failure occurred so we can repair our schemas for the future
- Unexpected ideas are more likely to stick because surprise makes us pay extra attention and think harder

### E. Avoiding Gimmickry: Surprise Must Serve the Core (pp. 80-81)

- Super Bowl 2000 ad: college marching band on a football field — then ravenous wolves rush out and attack them. Memorable, but nobody could remember what product it advertised
- Gimmicky surprise (wolves attacking band members) is nongermane to the core message — it produces memorability without stickiness
- The Enclave ad uses surprise that *reinforces* its core message about buckling up — "uncommon sense in the service of a core message"

### F. Hension and Phraug: Postdictability (pp. 81-83)

- Researchers Bruce Whittlesea and Lisa Williams created four fake words: HENSION, BARDLE, PHRAUG, TAYBL
- PHRAUG and TAYBL cause the surprise brow then an "Oh!" — they *sound* like FROG and TABLE when sounded out. The surprise is followed by insight (post-dictable).
- HENSION and BARDLE *look* familiar (they borrow common letter patterns) but lead nowhere — surprise without insight (not post-dictable). This produces frustration, not stickiness.
- **Key formula:** To be surprising, an event can't be predictable. To be *satisfying*, surprise must be "post-dictable" — the twist makes sense after the fact. Think *The Sixth Sense* vs. "it was all a dream."
- **Three-step process:** (1) Identify the core message. (2) Figure out what is counterintuitive about it. (3) Communicate the message in a way that breaks the audience's guessing machines along the counterintuitive dimension, then help them rebuild.

### G. Tire Chains at Nordstrom (pp. 84-86)

- Nordstrom uses unexpected stories to transform new employees' schemas of "good customer service": the Nordie who ironed a customer's shirt, who gift-wrapped a Macy's purchase, who warmed customers' cars in winter, who refunded money for tire chains (Nordstrom doesn't sell tire chains)
- These stories attack unspoken assumptions: "Service stops at the door." "Don't waste time on non-buyers."
- The stories are post-dictable — once you understand that Nordstrom means "make customers happy at the expense of efficiency," each story makes perfect sense
- "Common sense is the enemy of sticky messages" — "Great customer service" is common sense. Warming customers' cars in winter is uncommon sense.

### H. Journalism 101: "No School Next Thursday" (pp. 87-88)

- Nora Ephron's high school journalism teacher gave students a set of facts: the principal of Beverly Hills High, a college president, Margaret Mead, and the California governor would travel to Sacramento next Thursday for a colloquium
- Students wrote leads listing the facts. Teacher collected them, paused, then said: "The lead to the story is *There will be no school next Thursday.*"
- "It was a breathtaking moment. In that instant I realized that journalism was not just about regurgitating the facts but about figuring out the point."
- The teacher's method: (1) he knew the students had a defective schema of journalism; (2) he made them commit to it publicly; (3) he pulled the rug out with a well-structured surprise — post-dictable once you think about it

### I. Clinic: Foreign Aid (pp. 88-90)

- Americans' schema: the U.S. is a generous nation that spends heavily on foreign aid
- The counterintuitive truth: of all industrialized nations, the U.S. spends proportionally the *least* on foreign aid
- Message 1 buried the lead amid billions-dollar statistics. Message 2 broke the "generous America" schema directly and used concrete, tangible comparisons: "If everyone gave up one soft drink a month, we could double our aid to Africa"

### J. Keeping People's Attention: The Mystery of Saturn's Rings (pp. 91-93)

- Robert Cialdini studied what made science writing compelling. The best pieces all began with a mystery: a puzzling state of affairs that invited the reader into the material
- An astronomer asked: "What are the rings of Saturn made of?" Three internationally acclaimed groups reached wholly different conclusions — gas, dust particles, ice crystals. The answer unfolded like a detective story. (Answer: dust — specifically, ice-covered dust.)
- Cialdini used mysteries in his own classes. In one lecture, students protested when the bell rang before the mystery was resolved — normally students start packing up ten minutes before the end
- Schema violation is a onetime transaction (first-level unexpectedness). Mystery is created from an unexpected *journey* (second-level unexpectedness) — we want to solve it but don't know how.

### K. Curiosity in Hollywood Screenplays (pp. 94-95)

- Robert McKee (*Story*): "Curiosity is the intellectual need to answer questions and close open patterns. Story plays to this universal desire by doing the opposite, posing questions and opening situations."
- Every scene is a Turning Point that hooks curiosity: "What will happen next? How will it turn out?"
- The *How will it turn out?* question is powerful enough to keep us watching bad movies just to get the answer

### L. The Gap Theory of Curiosity (pp. 95-96)

- George Loewenstein (Carnegie Mellon, 1994): curiosity arises when we feel a gap in our knowledge — "It's like having an itch that we need to scratch"
- Gaps cause pain. We sit through bad movies because it's too painful not to know how they end.
- Movies, mystery novels, sports contests, crossword puzzles, and Pokemon cards all naturally create knowledge gaps
- **Critical insight:** We need to *open* gaps before we *close* them. Our tendency is to tell people the facts first. But Loewenstein argues we must first highlight the *specific knowledge that's missing* — make people feel the gap.
- Local news teasers are sensationalist examples: "There's a new drug sweeping the teenage community — and it may be in your own medicine cabinet!"

### M. Battling Overconfidence (pp. 99-101)

- People tend to be overconfident about how much they know — in one study, participants thought they'd identified 75% of the best solutions to a parking problem when they'd actually identified only 30%
- Strategies for combating overconfidence and priming the gap:
  - Ephron's journalism teacher made students commit to their defective schemas before shattering them
  - Eric Mazur (Harvard physics) uses "concept testing" — students vote publicly on answers, creating commitment and curiosity
  - Nancy Lowry and David Johnson found that fifth/sixth graders who experienced *disagreement* were more interested than those who reached consensus — 45% of the disagreement group stayed for a film during recess vs. 18% of the consensus group

### N. Gaps Start with Knowledge (pp. 102-103)

- Loewenstein argues that as we learn *more*, we become *more* curious — not less — because gaining knowledge highlights what we still don't know
- Someone who knows 47 of 50 state capitals is more likely to focus on the 3 they *don't* know than to celebrate the 47 they do
- **Implication:** You can't create a gap if people don't have enough knowledge to recognize it. Sometimes you must provide context first to *prime* the gap.

### O. Priming the Gap: NCAA Football (pp. 102-104)

- In the 1960s, Roone Arledge at ABC needed to make viewers care about college football games that didn't involve their home team
- His solution: provide backstory. Show the campus, the fans, the rivalries, the emotions *before* the game. "Take the viewer to the game" rather than "bring the game to the viewer."
- Arledge turned an abyss of ignorance into a gap of curiosity by filling in just enough context for viewers to start caring
- This became the foundation of *Monday Night Football*, *Wide World of Sports*, and *20/20* — thirty-six Emmys worth of gap-priming
- Knowledge gaps create interest. But proving the gap exists may require providing some knowledge first. "Here's what you know. Now here's what you're missing."

### P. Walking on the Moon and Radios in Pockets (pp. 106-109)

- Masaru Ibuka (Sony, 1953) proposed a "pocketable radio" when radios were pieces of furniture built by cabinetmakers — utterly unexpected
- JFK (May 1961) proposed landing a man on the moon within the decade — the U.S. had barely put a man in orbit
- Both ideas share three properties: (1) they create *surprise* — radios don't fit in pockets; men don't walk on the moon; (2) they create *insight* — a dramatic glimpse of how the world might unfold; (3) they create *knowledge gaps* — audacious but not insurmountable goals that immediately prompt brainstorming
- Loewenstein: "If people *like* curiosity, why do they work to resolve it? Why don't they put mystery novels down before the last chapter?"
- Both goals sustained motivation across years and thousands of people. "These are big, powerful, sticky ideas."

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The chapter's emphasis on surprise and violated expectations connects to [[representativeness]] and base-rate reasoning — we form expectations based on representative schemas, and when those schemas are broken, we pay attention. This is the same mechanism that makes the [[base-rate-neglect|base-rate fallacy]] so persistent — schemas override statistical reality.
- Cialdini's finding that the best science writing uses mystery structures connects to his work on [[commitment-and-consistency]] — once readers commit to wanting to solve the mystery, consistency pressure keeps them reading.
