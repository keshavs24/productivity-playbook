# Age of Propaganda Ch.6: Words of Influence — Summary

**Authors:** Anthony Pratkanis & Elliot Aronson
**Source:** *Age of Propaganda: The Everyday Use and Abuse of Persuasion* (Revised Edition), Chapter 6
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Words pre-persuade by defining the situation** before argumentation begins. The labels we use to describe an object or event direct our thoughts and channel our cognitive responses. Cicero recognized this: his courtroom success came from redefining crimes as virtuous deeds and victims as villains.
2. **Aspirin commercials demonstrate "as" framing.** "No other pain reliever is stronger, faster, or more effective" leads consumers to infer Brand A is *the best* — when tests showed all brands were equally effective. The product description creates the illusion of superiority through careful word choice.
3. **Glittering generalities** — purr words with positive connotations but ambiguous meaning ("freedom fighters," "a kinder, gentler America," "the best that money can buy") — are a classic propaganda device identified by the Institute for Propaganda Analysis in the 1930s.
4. **Labels create self-fulfilling prophecies.** Students randomly labeled "neat and tidy" became neater; those labeled "math achievers" improved in math — while students who merely received lectures on neatness or math showed no improvement. Labels define identity, and people act to be consistent with their labeled identity.

## Detailed Notes

### The Power of Naming

Words gain persuasive power through their ability to define situations and channel cognitive responses. Examples:
- "75% lean" is rated more favorably than "25% fat" — identical product, different response (Levin & Gaeth, 1988)
- Gasoline stations call credit-card surcharges a "cash discount"
- Grocery stores label frozen fish as "fresh frozen"
- The military uses "collateral damage" instead of "civilian deaths" and "low-intensity conflict" instead of "war"
- Politicians frame issues through language: "the war on drugs," "the domino theory," "a bridge to the twenty-first century"

### Glittering Generalities

The Institute for Propaganda Analysis (1930s) identified the use of **purr words** — words with positive connotations that are ambiguous in context:
- "A kinder, gentler America"
- "The best that money can buy"
- "We must support our valiant freedom fighters"

Few would disagree that kindness, quality, and freedom are good things. But what these words mean concretely is left to the audience's imagination — which is precisely the point.

### Labeling Creates Reality

Advertisers create needs by inventing labels: the Lambert Company popularized "halitosis" (bad breath) and then sold Listerine as the cure. Arthur Kudner invented "athlete's foot" and linked it to Absorbine Jr. More recently: NyQuil (the nighttime cold medicine), 7-Up (the uncola), Miller (the "lite" beer).

A good brand name can be worth more than the factory — Philip Morris bought Kraft for 6x book value because "Kraft is a trusted name."

The power of naming extends beyond products. Vernon Howell renamed himself "David Koresh" — "David" (spiritual descendant of King David) + "Koresh" (Hebrew for Cyrus, the Persian king who defeated Babylon) — thereby establishing himself as a prophet-messiah to his followers.

### Self-Fulfilling Prophecy

The tendency for a label to evoke behavior that makes the definition come true. Key studies:

**Miller, Brickman, and Bolin (labeling study):** Fifth-graders in Chicago were either (a) lectured on the importance of being neat and tidy, or (b) repeatedly labeled "a neat and tidy class" by teachers and janitors. Results: lectured students showed *no* improvement in littering; labeled students became neater, dropping 3x more litter in trash cans. A second study found that second-graders labeled "math achievers" subsequently improved in math more than those merely exhorted to work harder.

**Snyder, Tanke, and Berscheid (attractive-partner study):** Male subjects were paired with female partners for a phone conversation. Half were shown a photo of a very attractive woman; half an unattractive one. Males who believed they were talking to an attractive woman treated their partners as more poised, humorous, and socially adept — and independent observers listening to only the woman's half of the conversation rated women whose partners believed them attractive as *actually* more attractive, confident, animated, and warm. The label had created reality.

### The Orwellian Dimension

George Orwell's *1984* dramatizes the ultimate endpoint of language control. "Newspeak" was designed not just to provide a medium of expression for the party worldview but "to make all other modes of thought impossible." When language controls thought, heresy becomes literally unthinkable.

As Gordon Allport observed, language inherently categorizes and divides — "man," "woman," "philanthropist," "athlete." Nouns that "cut slices" (we-they, black-white, male-female) serve to divide the world into neat packages and imply courses of action.
