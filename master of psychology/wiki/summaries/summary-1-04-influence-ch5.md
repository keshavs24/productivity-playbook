# Influence Ch.5: Liking — The Friendly Thief — Summary

**Author(s):** Robert B. Cialdini
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 1
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **People prefer to say yes to those they know and like.** This simple rule is exploited in hundreds of ways by compliance professionals — from Tupperware parties to car sales to police interrogations. The liking bond between friends is so powerful that compliance professionals either leverage existing friendships or engineer rapid liking through specific, identifiable factors.

2. **Five major factors reliably produce liking:** [[physical-attractiveness-bias]], [[similarity-and-liking]], compliments/flattery, [[mere-exposure-effect]] (contact and familiarity), and [[conditioning-and-association]]. Each operates largely outside conscious awareness, functioning as a [[click-whirr]] trigger that automatically increases compliance.

3. **Association is a deeply underestimated force.** People connect messengers with their messages (weathermen blamed for bad weather, Persian messengers killed for bearing bad news). We actively manage our associations — "basking in reflected glory" (BIRGing) by connecting to winners and distancing from losers. This extends to sports fandom, celebrity endorsements, credit card insignias, and the "luncheon technique."

4. **Cooperation toward shared goals is one of the most powerful liking generators.** Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment and Aronson's jigsaw classroom demonstrate that while mere contact (even pleasant contact) fails to reduce intergroup hostility, structured cooperation toward common goals reliably produces liking and friendship across group lines.

5. **The defense against the liking principle is not to prevent liking but to notice it.** Rather than trying to block the many routes to liking, Cialdini recommends monitoring for the *effect* — asking "Have I come to like this person more quickly than I would have expected?" — and then mentally separating the requester from the request.

## Detailed Notes

### The Tupperware Party — A Classic Compliance Setting

The chapter opens with the Tupperware party as the clearest illustration of the liking rule's professional exploitation. All six weapons of influence operate simultaneously:

- **[[reciprocation]]**: Games, prizes, grab bags ensure everyone receives a gift before buying begins
- **Commitment**: Participants publicly describe uses and benefits of Tupperware they already own
- **[[social-proof]]**: Each purchase builds the idea that similar people want the products
- **Liking**: The true request comes not from the Tupperware demonstrator (a stranger) but from the party hostess — a friend

The social bond between hostess and guests is *twice as likely* to determine product purchase as preference for the product itself (Frenzen & Davis, 1990). Tupperware sales exceed $2.5 million per day. A new Tupperware party starts every 2.7 seconds globally. The company's success has spread to Europe, Latin America, and Asia — where networks of friends and family are even more socially significant (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Smith, Bond, & Kagitcibasi, 2006). Less than a quarter of Tupperware sales now take place in North America.

In 2003, Tupperware severed its relationship with Target stores because retail sales were *too strong* — reducing the number of home parties that could be arranged (Latest News, 2003).

**Anecdote — frustrated Tupperware customer:** One woman described the social pressure: "It's gotten to the point now where I hate to be invited to Tupperware parties. I've got all the containers I need... But when a friend calls up, I feel like I have to go. And when I get there, I feel like I have to buy something. What can I do? It's for one of my friends."

**Reader's Report 5.1 — MCI Friends and Family Calling Circle:** A man from Chicago describes being switched to MCI phone service not because he wanted it, but because refusing would have been "a real affront" to his friend Brad, who had placed him on his Calling Circle list. The MCI salesperson confirmed the approach works "9 out of 10 times" (*Consumer Reports*).

### The Shaklee "Endless Chain" Method

The Shaklee Corporation advises door-to-door salespeople to ask satisfied customers for the names of friends who might also appreciate the product. Each new prospect is then visited by a salesperson armed with the name of a friend "who suggested I call on you." The Shaklee sales manual states: "It would be impossible to overestimate its value. Phoning or calling on a prospect and being able to say that Mr. So-and-so, a friend of his, felt he would benefit by giving you a few moments of his time is virtually as good as a sale 50 percent made before you enter."

### Joe Girard — The World's Greatest Car Salesman

Joe Girard of Detroit specialized in using the liking rule to sell Chevrolets. For twelve consecutive years he won the title of "Number One Car Salesman," averaging more than five cars and trucks sold every day he worked. Named the world's "greatest car salesman" by the *Guinness Book of World Records*.

His formula: "Finding the salesman you like, plus the price. Put them both together, and you get a deal." The crucial question his success raises — what factors cause one person to like another?

### Factor 1: Physical Attractiveness — The [[halo-effect]]

**The halo effect:** When one positive characteristic of a person dominates the way that person is viewed by others. Physical attractiveness is a powerful trigger of the [[halo-effect]]. There is a [[click-whirr]] response to attractive people (Olson & Marshuetz, 2005).

Research shows we automatically assign to good-looking individuals favorable traits such as talent, kindness, honesty, and intelligence — without being aware we are doing so (Langlois et al., 2000). This unconscious assumption that "good-looking equals good" has serious consequences:

**Elections — Efran & Patterson (1976):** In the 1974 Canadian federal elections, attractive candidates received more than two and a half times as many votes as unattractive candidates. In follow-up research, 73% of Canadian voters denied in the strongest possible terms that their votes had been influenced by physical appearance; only 14% even allowed for the possibility.

**Hiring — Mack & Rainey (1990):** In a simulated employment interview, good grooming accounted for more favorable hiring decisions than did job qualifications — even though interviewers claimed appearance played a small role.

**Pay — Hammermesh & Biddle (1994):** Economists examining U.S. and Canadian samples found that attractive individuals get paid an average of 12-14% more than their unattractive coworkers.

**Legal system — Stewart (1980):** In a Pennsylvania study, researchers rated the physical attractiveness of 74 separate male defendants at the start of their criminal trials. When they later checked court records, the handsome men had received significantly lighter sentences. Attractive defendants were twice as likely to avoid jail as unattractive defendants (reviews: Castellow, Wuensch, & Moore, 1991; Downs & Lyons, 1990).

**Negligence trials — Kulka & Kessler (1978):** When the defendant was better looking than his victim, average damages assessed were $5,623. When the victim was more attractive, average damages jumped to $10,051. Both male and female jurors exhibited this favoritism.

**Prison recidivism — Kurtzburg, Safar, & Cavior (1968):** New York City jail inmates with facial disfigurements who underwent plastic surgery while incarcerated were significantly less likely to return to jail one year after release (except heroin addicts). This held true regardless of whether they had received traditional rehabilitative services. Cialdini notes the troubling implication from Stewart (1980): making ugly criminals more attractive may not reduce crime — it may only reduce their chances of being sent to jail for it.

**Help and persuasion:** Attractive people are more likely to obtain help when in need (Benson, Karabenic, & Lerner, 1976) and are more persuasive in changing opinions (Chaiken, 1979). Both sexes respond similarly. Exception: when the attractive person is viewed as a direct competitor or romantic rival.

**Early childhood:** The social benefits of good looks begin accumulating early. Adults view aggressive acts as less naughty when performed by an attractive child (Dion, 1972). Teachers presume good-looking children to be more intelligent (Ritts, Patterson, & Tubbs, 1992).

**Attractiveness and self-concept — Major, Carrington, & Carnevale (1984):** Good-looking people are aware that others' positive evaluations may not be based on their actual traits and abilities but on their attractiveness "halo." This can leave many attractive people with an uncertain self-concept and a tenuous relationship between attractiveness and self-esteem (Adams, 1977).

### Factor 2: [[similarity-and-liking]]

We like people who are similar to us (Burger et al., 2004). This holds for opinions, personality traits, background, and lifestyle.

**Dress similarity — Emswiller, Deaux, & Willits (1971):** In the early 1970s, experimenters dressed in either "hippie" or "straight" fashion asked college students for a dime to make a phone call. When dressed the same way as the student, the request was granted more than two-thirds of the time; when dissimilarly dressed, less than half the time.

**Antiwar petition — Suedfeld, Bochner, & Matas (1971):** Marchers in an antiwar demonstration were more likely to sign the petition of a similarly dressed requester — *and* to do so without bothering to read it first. [[click-whirr]].

**Car salespeople:** Trained to look for evidence of customer interests while examining trade-ins (camping gear, golf balls, out-of-state plates) and later claim similar interests.

**Insurance sales — Burger et al. (2004); Evans (1963):** Customers were more likely to buy insurance when the salesperson was like them in age, religion, politics, and cigarette-smoking habits.

**Name similarity and surveys — Garner (2005):** Modifying a cover letter so the survey-taker's name was similar to the recipient's name nearly doubled survey compliance in two separate studies. Robert Greer sent surveys from "Bob Gregar"; Cynthia Johnston received hers from "Cindy Johanson."

**Life-and-death decisions — Furnham (1996):** When asked to rank-order kidney disorder patients for the next available treatment, people chose those whose political party preference matched their own.

**Marriage partner choice — Jones et al. (2004):** Minor commonalities affect decisions all the way to the choice of a marriage partner.

**Mirror and match:** Sales training programs now urge trainees to mirror the customer's body posture, mood, and verbal style, as similarities along each of these dimensions produce positive results (Chartrand & Bargh, 1999; Locke & Horowitz, 1990; van Baaren et al., 2003).

**Underestimation — Gonzales, Davis, Loney, Lukens, & Junghans (1983):** We typically underestimate the degree to which similarity affects our liking for another person.

### Factor 3: Compliments

**McLean Stevenson anecdote:** Actor McLean Stevenson once described how his wife tricked him into marriage: "She said she liked me." The information that someone fancies us is a "bewitchingly effective device for producing return liking and willing compliance" (Berscheid & Walster, 1978; Howard, Gengler, & Jain, 1995, 1997).

**Joe Girard's "I like you" cards:** Each month he sent every one of his more than 13,000 former customers a holiday greeting card. The message on the face of the card never varied: "I like you." As Joe explained: "There's nothing else on the card, nothin' but my name. I'm just telling 'em that I like 'em." That meant 150,000+ cards per year — an investment that paid off because "we are phenomenal suckers for flattery."

**Reader's Report 5.2 — MBA Student in Arizona:** A coworker named Chris would always compliment before requesting help. "I heard you did a fantastic job with the such-and-such project, and I have a similar one I am hoping you can help me with." Cialdini's author's note: Chris combined liking (flattery) with consistency (giving the reader a reputation to live up to).

**North Carolina experiment — Drachman, deCarufel, & Insko (1978):** Men received comments about themselves from another person who needed a favor. Three findings: (1) The evaluator who provided only praise was liked best. (2) This held true even when the men fully realized the flatterer stood to gain from their liking him. (3) Unlike other types of comments, pure praise did not have to be accurate to work — positive comments produced just as much liking for the flatterer when they were untrue as when they were true. [[click-whirr]].

### Factor 4: Contact and Cooperation — The [[mere-exposure-effect]]

**Familiarity breeds liking:** We like things that are familiar to us (Monahan, Murphy, & Zajonc, 2000).

**Mirror image experiment — Mita, Dermer, & Knight (1977):** When women in Milwaukee were shown a true photograph of their face and a mirror-reversed image, they preferred the mirror image (the version they see every day). Their close friends preferred the true photograph (the version *they* see every day).

**Elections and familiarity — Grush, 1980; Grush, McKeough, & Ahlering (1978):** Voters often choose a candidate merely because the name seems familiar. In one controversial Ohio election, a man given little chance of winning the state attorney-general race swept to victory when he changed his name to Brown — a family name of much Ohio political tradition.

**Subliminal familiarity — Bornstein, Leone, & Galley (1987):** Faces flashed on a screen so quickly subjects couldn't recall seeing them. Yet the more frequently a face was flashed, the more the subjects liked that person in subsequent interaction — *and* the more they were persuaded by that person's opinions.

**Online advertising — Fang (2007):** Banner ads for a camera were flashed five, twenty, or not at all at the top of an article. The more frequently the ad appeared, the more participants liked the camera — even though they were not aware of seeing the ads.

#### Contact Does Not Always Produce Liking

**School desegregation — Stephan (1978):** School desegregation is more likely to *increase* prejudice between blacks and whites than to decrease it. The "contact approach" to improving race relations fails because the conditions for positive contact are not met in schools.

The school setting is not a melting pot — children clot together ethnically (Dixon et al., 2005; Oskamp & Schultz, 1998). Even if interethnic interaction occurred, contact under conditions of frustration, conflict, or competition leads to *less* liking (Richeson & Shelton, 2007; Swap, 1977; Zajonc, Markus, & Wilson, 1974). The competitive American classroom fosters precisely these unpleasant conditions.

**Aronson's description of the standard classroom (Aronson, 1975, pp. 44, 47):** "The teacher stands in front of the class and asks a question. Six to ten children strain in their seats and wave their hands in the teacher's face... Several others sit quietly with eyes averted, trying to become invisible... This game is fiercely competitive and the stakes are high, because the kids are competing for the love and approval of one of the two or three most important people in their world... Children who fail in this system become jealous and resentful of the successes, putting them down as teacher's pets or even resorting to violence against them in the school yard."

**Minority academic performance — Stephan (1978):** Although white students' achievement levels remain steady, it is 10 times more likely that minority students' academic performance will *significantly increase* rather than significantly decline after desegregation.

#### Sherif's Robbers Cave Experiment

**Sherif, Harvey, White, Hood, & Sherif (1961):** Muzafer Sherif and colleagues investigated intergroup conflict in boys' summer camps over four decades. The boys never realized they were participants in an experiment.

**Phase 1 — Creating conflict:** Simply separating boys into two residence cabins ("Eagles" and "Rattlers") stimulated a "we versus they" feeling. Introducing competitive activities (treasure hunts, tugs-of-war, athletic contests) produced name-calling ("cheaters," "sneaks," "stinkers"), cabin raids, stolen and burned banners, threatening signs, and lunchroom scuffles.

**Phase 2 — Failed remedy (mere contact):** Bringing the hostile groups together for pleasant joint activities (movies, picnics) was disastrous. Picnics produced food fights, entertainment programs became shouting contests, dining-hall lines degenerated into shoving matches.

**Phase 3 — Successful remedy (cooperative goals):** The researchers constructed situations requiring cooperation for mutual benefit: (a) a truck "stuck" that needed all boys to push; (b) a camp water supply interruption requiring unified action; (c) a desirable movie the camp couldn't afford, requiring pooled resources. These cooperative ventures steadily bridged the rift. Boys began to intermix at meal tables and list best friends from the other group. In one revealing episode, boys from one group used their $5 treasury to buy milkshakes for their former adversaries.

Successful cooperation works to reduce intergroup hostility not just among children but also in college groups (Worchel, 1979) and business organizations (Blake & Mouton, 1979). Cooperation leads to both greater liking *and* greater group success (Stanne, D. W. Johnson, & R. T. Johnson, 1999).

#### Aronson's Jigsaw Classroom

**Aronson, Stephan, Sikes, Blaney, & Snapp (1978):** Developed the "jigsaw classroom" — students grouped into cooperating teams where each student holds only one piece of the information needed to pass the test. Students must take turns teaching and helping one another. Like Sherif's campers, they become allies rather than enemies.

**Results (Aronson, Bridgeman, & Geffner, 1978a, 1978b):** Compared to traditional competitive classrooms: significantly more friendship and less prejudice among ethnic groups; increased minority students' self-esteem, liking for school, and test scores; white students benefited too — their self-esteem and liking for school went up, and test performance was at least as high as traditional classes.

**Caveats:** Issues within cooperative learning remain complex (Rosenfield & Stephan, 1981; Slavin, 1983). More research is needed on optimal frequency, ages, and group types. Competition has its place as a motivator and builder of self-concept. The task is not to eliminate academic competition but to break its monopoly by introducing regular cooperative techniques.

#### Cooperation in Compliance Settings

Compliance professionals systematically use cooperation to generate liking:

**Good Cop/Bad Cop:** A detailed illustration of how police interrogators use manufactured cooperation. Bad Cop terrifies the suspect with threats and hostility. Good Cop gradually emerges as a supporter, calling the suspect by first name, pointing out positives, even buying coffee. Good Cop's big scene: "Look, man, I don't know why, but my partner doesn't like you... If you admit you robbed that place right now, before he gets back, I'll take charge of your case and put in a good word for you to the D.A."

The technique works through multiple principles simultaneously: (1) fear of long incarceration via Bad Cop's threats; (2) [[contrast-principle]] — compared to Bad Cop, Good Cop seems *especially* reasonable (Kamisar, 1980); (3) [[reciprocation]] — Good Cop has intervened on the suspect's behalf and spent money on coffee; (4) cooperation/liking — Good Cop positions himself as working *with* the suspect for mutual benefit. The suspect sees a savior, and "from savior, it is but a short step to trusted father confessor."

**Car salespeople:** Take the customer's side and "do battle" with their bosses to secure a good deal. (In reality, the salesman often has a soft drink while the boss continues working, then returns with the same deal he had in mind all along.)

### Factor 5: [[conditioning-and-association]]

**The weatherman anecdote:** A local TV weatherman called Cialdini's psychology department asking "Why do they blame *me*, Doc?" He received hate mail, death threats, and on-air abuse from colleagues when weather was bad. Cialdini explained the principle through the Persian imperial messengers — bearers of military victory were treated as heroes; bearers of defeat were summarily slain. "The nature of bad news infects the teller" — a natural human tendency to dislike a person who brings unpleasant information, even when that person did not cause it (Manis, Cornell, & Moore, 1974).

**The general principle (Lott & Lott, 1965):** *An innocent association with either bad things or good things will influence how people feel about us.*

**Guilt by association — Miller, Campbell, Twedt, & O'Connell (1966):** People assume we have the same personality traits as our friends. Parents warning children about "the bad kids down the street" were teaching guilt by association.

**Attractive models and cars — Smith & Engel (1968):** Men who saw a new-car ad that included a seductive female model rated the *car* as faster, more appealing, more expensive-looking, and better-designed than men who viewed the same ad without the model. When asked later, the men refused to believe the presence of the young woman had influenced their judgments.

**Credit cards and spending — Feinberg (1986):** In studies at West Lafayette, Indiana: (1) restaurant patrons gave larger tips when paying with credit card instead of cash; (2) college students were willing to spend an average of 29% more for mail-order catalog items when examining them in a room containing MasterCard insignias — with no awareness that the insignias were part of the experiment; (3) when asked to contribute to charity (United Way), students in a room with MasterCard insignias were markedly more likely to give money (87% vs. 33%). Subsequent research (Feinberg, 1990) confirmed the effect only works for people with a positive credit card history. Restaurant studies replicated: diners tipped significantly more in the presence of credit card insignia, even when paying with cash (McCall & Belmont, 1996).

**Product associations and cultural trends:** During the moon shot, everything was sold with allusions to the space program. In Olympiad years, products become "official" Olympic sponsors. In the 1970s, the "natural" trend was crowded to capacity. Mars Candy Bar sales jumped after the Mars Pathfinder landed on the red planet in 1997 — despite the candy being named after founder Franklin Mars, not the planet (White, 1997). SALE signs increase purchases even when there is no actual savings, partly through repeated association with good prices in the past (Naylor et al., 2006).

**Celebrity endorsements:** Athletes are paid to connect themselves to products — relevant (sports shoes) or irrelevant (soft drinks, popcorn poppers, panty hose). Politicians assemble celebrity support to sway voters. A Los Angeles woman on a California smoking referendum: "It's a real tough decision. They've got big stars speaking for it, and big stars speaking against it. You don't know how to vote."

**Political fundraising and food:** Politicians use the "luncheon technique" — speeches and appeals come during or after the meal is served, never before.

#### The Luncheon Technique — Razran (1938, 1940)

Gregory Razran — both a respected researcher and one of the earliest translators of Russian psychological literature (Pavlov) — found that subjects became fonder of people and things they experienced while eating. In the key experiment, subjects rated political statements before and after the study. Only those statements shown while food was being eaten gained in approval. The changes appeared to occur unconsciously — subjects could not remember which statements they had seen while food was served.

Razran (1940) also included a condition where putrid odors were piped into the room while slogans were shown — approval ratings declined. More recent research (Li et al., 2007) shows that subliminal pleasant or unpleasant odors influence how likable photographed faces are judged to be.

The connection to Pavlov is direct: food produces many responses beyond salivation — including a positive feeling. Through raw association, this positive feeling can be transferred to anything (political statements, products, people) closely associated with good food.

#### Communicating Good vs. Bad News — Rosen & Tesser (1970)

University of Georgia: Students waiting for an experiment were given the job of informing a fellow student that an important phone call had come in — half the time good news, half bad. Students conveyed the information differently depending on its quality: good news — "You just got a phone call with great news. Better see the experimenter for the details." Bad news — "You just got a phone call. Better see the experimenter for the details." They had learned to connect themselves to good news but not bad.

#### [[basking-in-reflected-glory]]

People try to link themselves to positive events and separate themselves from negative events — even when they did not cause them.

**The mute veteran:** A World War II soldier in the Balkans stopped speaking after the war. Medical exams found no physical cause. For 30 years in a veterans' hospital, he never broke his silence. Then a radio in his ward was tuned to a soccer match between his hometown team and a rival. When the referee called a foul against his team's player, he jumped from his chair and shouted his first words in three decades: "You dumb ass! Are you trying to *give* them the match?" He returned to his chair and never spoke again.

**Isaac Asimov (1975):** "All things being equal, you root for your own sex, your own culture, your own locality... and what you want to prove is that *you* are better than the other person."

**Sports fan pronouns — Cialdini et al. (1976):** Students at Arizona State University were phoned and asked to describe football game outcomes from a few weeks earlier. After wins, students used "we" — "We beat Houston, 17 to 14." After losses, "we" was rarely used — "They lost to Missouri, 30 to 20" or "Arizona State got beat." One student combined both desires: "Arizona State lost it, 30 to 20" — then blurted in anguish — "*They* threw away *our* chance for a national championship!"

**Belgian elections — Boen et al. (2002):** After general elections, the better the result for a party, the longer homeowners left their lawn signs up, basking in the positive connection.

**Who BIRGs most?** People with poor self-concepts. They seek prestige not from their own attainments but from their associations with others' attainments. The persistent name-dropper is a classic example. So is the rock-music groupie. The behavior reflects "the rather tragic view of accomplishment as deriving from outside the self."

**Reader's Report 5.3 — Los Angeles Movie Studio Employee:** The reader finally understood why Kevin Costner mentioned "the people I went to high school with" in his Oscar acceptance speech for *Dances with Wolves*. Everyone who went to school with Costner would be telling others about their connection — basking in reflected glory.

**Stage mothers and vicarious achievement:** Some people work the association principle in reverse — instead of inflating their connections to successful others, they strive to inflate the success of those they are connected to. Example: a Davenport, Iowa obstetrician cut off service to the wives of three school officials because his son had not been given enough playing time in school basketball. One of the wives was eight months pregnant.

### Defense

Because liking can be increased through many means, a specific countertactic for each would be pointless. Instead, Cialdini recommends a general approach based on timing:

**Key strategy:** Rather than trying to prevent the action of liking factors *before* they work, monitor for the *effect* — the feeling that you have come to like a compliance practitioner more quickly or more deeply than the circumstances warrant. Once you notice undue liking, that feeling itself becomes the signal that a tactic is being used.

**Countermaneuver:** Mentally separate the dealer from the deal. Ask: "In the 25 minutes I've known this guy, have I come to like him more than I would have expected?" If yes, it is time to focus exclusively on the merits of the deal and the product, not on the requester.

The approach is *not* to actively dislike the practitioner — that would be unfair and contrary to one's own interests, since some naturally likeable people offer the best deals. Instead, the recognition of unwarranted liking serves as a reminder to separate feelings about the requester from the request itself.

## New Concepts Introduced

- [[liking-principle]] — the overarching principle that people comply more with those they like
- [[physical-attractiveness-bias]] — the systematic advantage attractive people enjoy across domains
- [[halo-effect]] — one positive characteristic dominating perception of the whole person
- [[similarity-and-liking]] — increased compliance from perceived similarity in opinions, traits, dress, background
- [[mere-exposure-effect]] — familiarity through repeated contact breeds liking (with conditions)
- [[conditioning-and-association]] — innocent connections with good or bad things influence how people feel about us
- [[cooperation-and-liking]] — shared cooperative goals as a powerful generator of liking
- [[basking-in-reflected-glory]] — connecting oneself to winners and distancing from losers

## Cross-References

- **[[reciprocation]]** — operates alongside liking in Tupperware parties and the Good Cop/Bad Cop technique; Good Cop's coffee purchase triggers reciprocal obligation
- **[[click-whirr]]** — the meta-mechanism underlying liking responses; physical attractiveness, similarity, and flattery all trigger automatic compliance
- **[[contrast-principle]]** — in Good Cop/Bad Cop, the contrast between the two officers makes Good Cop seem *especially* kind and reasonable
- **[[social-proof]]** — operates alongside liking in Tupperware parties; each purchase signals others should buy
- **[[expensive-equals-good]]** — parallel heuristic; "good-looking equals good" is the attractiveness analogue
- **[[fundamental-attribution-error]]** — voters deny that physical appearance influences their decisions (Efran & Patterson, 1976), attributing their choices to candidate quality

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Contact and liking are not always aligned.** The [[mere-exposure-effect]] literature generally shows familiarity breeds liking, but the school desegregation research demonstrates that contact under negative conditions (competition, frustration) can *decrease* liking (Richeson & Shelton, 2007). This is an important boundary condition.
- **Flattery works even when recognized as manipulative** (Drachman et al., 1978). This challenges the naive assumption that awareness of a persuasion attempt is sufficient defense. Relates to broader questions about whether knowledge of biases actually debiases (see [[bias-blind-spot]]).
