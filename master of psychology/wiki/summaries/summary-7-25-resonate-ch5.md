# Resonate Ch.5: Create Meaningful Content — Summary

**Author:** Nancy Duarte
**Source:** *Resonate* (2010), Chapter 5
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Generate ideas through divergent thinking, then filter through convergent thinking.** Collect and create as many ideas as possible (idea collection and idea creation), then ruthlessly filter them down to support the one big idea. Use the [[big-idea]] as the filter — kill everything that does not distinctly support it. "Murder your darlings."
2. **Balance analytical and emotional content using Aristotle's three appeals.** [[aristotle]] claimed that to persuade, one must employ [[ethos]] (ethical/credibility appeal), [[pathos]] (emotional appeal), and [[logos]] (logical appeal). Facts alone are not sufficient to persuade — they need to be complemented with credibility and emotional content. Randy Olson's "four organs of communication" (head, heart, gut, groin) provides a visceral framework for the same idea.
3. **Contrast creates contour** — the gap between what is and what could be is established through contrasting perspectives. A 1986 study by Heritage and Greatbatch found that in 476 political speeches, half of all bursts of applause could be attributed to moments where a form of contrast was communicated. Contrasting the commonplace with the lofty creates the up-and-down pattern of the presentation form.
4. **Transform ideas into meaning through stories.** Stories reshape information from static and flat to dynamic and alive. The brain processes information and associates meaning to it. Features become valuable only when they impact a human. Use the Short Story Template (beginning: when/who/where; middle: context/conflict/proposed resolution/complication; end: actual resolution/most important point).

## Detailed Notes

### Everything and the Kitchen Sink
Resist the temptation to sit down with presentation software during this initial phase. Use divergent thinking to generate as many ideas as possible — idea collection (mining existing material from peers, competitors, all resources) and idea creation (inventing new ideas instinctively, taking risks, being persistent). Use sticky notes to capture everything.

### More Than Just Facts
[[aristotle]]'s three types of argument: [[ethos]] (ethical appeal — garner respect through credibility and character), [[pathos]] (emotional appeal — stir emotions and imagination), [[logos]] (logical appeal — provide evidence through words, structure, and data). Facts alone are not sufficient to persuade. Stating fact after fact in an hour-long presentation does not signal to the audience *why* these facts are important. Use emotions as a tool to bring emphasis.

### Don't Be So Cerebral
Randy Olson's "four organs of communication": the Head (analytical, rational), the Heart (emotional, sincere), the Gut (instinct, impulsive), and the Groin (passion, no logic). Most professionals communicate only from the head. Whatever your natural tendency, learn skills in the other regions to appeal to a broad audience.

### Contrast Creates Contour
People are naturally attracted to opposites. Communicating an idea juxtaposed with its polar opposite creates energy. Moving back and forth between contradictory poles encourages full engagement. Heritage and Greatbatch (1986, *American Journal of Sociology*) analyzed 476 political speeches in Britain and found that after studying over 19,000 sentences, half of all bursts of applause were attributed to moments where a form of contrast was communicated.

Create Contrast exercise: What Is vs. What Could Be pairings — alternate point of view / your point of view, past/present / future, pain / gain, problem / solution, roadblocks / clear passage, resistance / action, impossible / possible, need / fulfillment, disadvantage / advantage, information / insight, ordinary / special, question / answer.

### Transform Ideas Into Meaning
The brain processes information and associates meaning to it. Stories reshape information from static to dynamic and alive. Features become valuable when they impact a human. "Stories are the currency of human relationships." — Robert McKee

### Recall Stories
Most great presentations use personal stories. Create a personal catalog of stories associated with various emotions. Recall stories through: People (relational memories), Places (spatial recollection), Things (sentimentally significant items).

### Turn Information Into Stories
Stories strengthen presentations by adding meaning. Short Story Template based on the Hero's Journey: Beginning (When, Transition, Who/What, Where), Middle (Context, Conflict, Proposed Resolution, Complication), End (Actual Resolution, Most Important Point).

### Case Study: Cisco Systems
Technology is meaningless until you understand how humans benefit from it. Cisco's "Hop to It" story transformed a dry technology capability slide into a compelling narrative about Dave, a microbrewery president whose prize hops were delayed in customs. The story structure introduced the hero, set up the conflict, built a cliffhanger, then showed how the technology resolved the crisis.

### Move from Data to Meaning
Numbers rarely speak for themselves. Give them a clear and convincing voice through: Scale (contrasting large numbers with items of familiar size), Compare (putting numbers in context by comparing to similar values in different contexts), Context (explaining environmental factors that influence changes).

### Murder Your Darlings
Now that you have amassed all the analytical and emotional content possible, it is time to narrow it down. The ideas need to be filtered to the points that succinctly support your [[big-idea]]. Use both divergent and convergent thinking processes. Make edits on behalf of the audience — they do not want everything. The quality depends just as much on what you choose to remove as what you choose to include. "Whenever you feel an impulse to perpetrate a piece of exceptionally fine writing, obey it — whole-heartedly — and delete it before sending your manuscript to press. Murder your darlings." — Sir Arthur Quiller-Couch

### From Ideas to Messages
Cluster ideas by topic, then turn topics into discrete messages. Topics are neutral; messages are emotionally charged complete sentences. Use MECE (Mutually Exclusive and Collectively Exhaustive) from McKinsey: each idea should not overlap, and nothing important should be left out.

### Resonance Rule #5
"Use the big idea to filter out all frequencies other than the resonant frequency."
