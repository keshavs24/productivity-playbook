# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.2 -- Overconfidence

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Edition)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Overconfidence is "the mother of all biases" -- the most potent, pervasive, and pernicious bias (Griffin & Varey, 1996; DeBondt & Thaler, 1995), blamed for wars, stock bubbles, entrepreneurial bankruptcy, Chernobyl, the Challenger explosion, and the subprime crisis
2. Overconfidence has three distinct forms: [[overprecision]] (too-narrow confidence intervals), [[overestimation]] (thinking we are better than we are), and [[overplacement]] (thinking we are better than others)
3. Overprecision: When asked for 98% confidence intervals, people typically bound only 30-70% of true quantities; Alpert & Raiffa (1969/1982) found 42.6% of quantities fell outside 90% confidence ranges; experts are equally overprecise (Henrion & Fischhoff, 1986); financial officers' 80% confidence intervals captured actual returns only 33% of the time (Ben-David, Graham, & Harvey, 2010)
4. Overestimation manifests as self-enhancement, illusion of control (Langer, 1975), planning fallacy (Buehler, Griffin, & Ross, 1994), and optimistic biases; Boston's Big Dig was $12 billion over budget and 9 years late
5. Overplacement: 93% of American drivers rate themselves more skillful than the median (Svenson, 1981); 60% of students rated their ability to get along in the top 10%, 25% in the top 1% (College Board, 1976-77); "Lake Wobegon effect" (Cannell, 1989)

## Detailed Notes

### The Mother of All Biases
Overconfidence facilitates many other biases. If we were appropriately humble about our judgment quality, we could more easily double-check opinions and correct flaws. Instead, we continue believing our views are correct despite copious evidence of fallibility (Pronin, Gilovich, & Ross, 2004; Schulz, 2010). The three-part taxonomy (Larrick, Burson, & Soll, 2007) organizes the research.

### Overprecision
The tendency to be too sure our judgments and decisions are accurate, draw overly narrow confidence intervals, and be too certain we know the truth. Causes include: (1) desire to relieve internal dissonance (Herzog & Hertwig, 2009 -- asking for a second estimate from the "crowd within" improved accuracy); (2) social rewards for expressing confidence (Sniezek & Van Sol, 2001 -- confident people seen as more persuasive and elevated to leadership); (3) confirmation bias making supportive evidence more available than contradictory evidence (Klayman & Ha, 1987). Consequences: reluctance to take advice (Yaniv & Kleinberger, 2000; Minson, Liberman, & Ross, 2009); naive realism (Ross & Ward, 1996) -- believing our perspective is the only sensible one; excessive stock trading (Barber & Odean, 2000; Odean, 1998); overprecise hiring decisions despite research showing interviews poorly predict performance (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998). Interventions: thinking about why you might be wrong (Koriat, Lichtenstein, & Fischhoff, 1980); considering alternative outcomes (Haran, Moore, & Morewedge, 2010).

### Overestimation
The common tendency to think we are better, smarter, faster, more capable, more attractive, or more popular than we actually are. Manifestations:

**Self-enhancement:** Motivated to view ourselves positively (Dunning, 2005). We evaluate ourselves more positively on desirable traits (Alicke, 1985). We even prefer the letters in our own names (Nuttin, 1985, 1987). These effects are strongest with quick, automatic System 1 responses (Koole, Dijksterhuis, & van Knippenberg, 2001).

**Illusion of control:** Overestimating how much control we have over circumstances (S.C. Thompson, 1999). Superficial signs of control (picking lottery numbers) lead to belief in control over uncontrollable events (Langer, 1975). Paradoxically, when people have great control, they tend to underestimate it, leading to fatalistic attitudes.

**Planning fallacy:** Overestimating speed of project completion (Buehler, Griffin, & Ross, 1994). Major infrastructure projects systematically underestimate cost and duration (Flyvbjerg, 2003). Boston's Big Dig: budgeted $2.5B, completed 2007 at $14.5B, 9 years late. Doctoral students dramatically overestimate likelihood of finishing dissertations within a year. Interestingly, people overestimate completion times for simple tasks when complications are few (Boltz, Kupperman, & Dunne, 1998).

**Optimistic biases:** Unrealistic optimism (Sharot, Riccardi, Raio, & Phelps, 2007). People believe they are more likely to live past 70 but less likely to live past 100 than others. Not universal (Chambers, Windschitl, & Suls, 2003; Kruger & Burrus, 2004). Defensive pessimism (Norem, 2002) is a counterforce -- bracing for disappointment. The "moment of truth" effect: we become more pessimistic as feedback approaches (Gilovich, Kerr, & Medvec, 1993).

### Overplacement
The tendency to believe we are better than others in specific ways when we are not. The "better-than-average" effect: 93% of American drivers claimed to be more skillful than the median (Svenson, 1981). Key finding: overplacement occurs on easy tasks, underplacement on difficult tasks (Kruger, 1999; Chambers et al., 2003). People believe they are more likely than others to experience common events but less likely to experience rare events (Kruger & Burrus, 2004). The fundamental driver: people focus on their own abilities and neglect the reference group (Klar, Karelitz, Roziner, & Levi, 2012; Zell & Alicke, 2009; Moore & Kim, 2003). Consequences: inflated expectations in lawsuits (Neale & Bazerman, 1985), excessive entrepreneurial entry (Camerer & Lovallo, 1999), costly failed mergers (Malmendier & Tate, 2008).

### Well-Calibrated Decision Making
Taylor and Brown (1988) argued positive illusions enhance psychological resilience. Seligman (1991) advocated "learned optimism." However, Bazerman and Moore are highly dubious: no study shows overconfidence leads to better decisions. Positive illusions lead to arrogant behavior (Anderson et al., 2006; Baumeister et al., 2003), more cheating (von Hippel et al., 2005), and undermined performance in effort-dependent areas (Vancouver & Kendall, 2006). The prescription: strive for well-calibrated beliefs that match reality.

## Contradictions or Tensions
Extends [[overconfidence-in-calibration]] from the Tversky/Kahneman paper significantly. The original paper treated overconfidence primarily as a calibration issue (overprecision). Bazerman and Moore expand it into a three-part taxonomy. No contradiction -- this is a refinement and extension.
