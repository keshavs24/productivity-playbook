# Influence Ch.7: Scarcity — Summary

**Author:** Robert B. Cialdini
**Source:** *Influence: Science and Practice*, 5th Edition (2009), Chapter 7
**Phase:** 1 — Cognitive Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Opportunities seem more valuable when they are less available.** The [[scarcity-principle]] drives people to want things more when they are rare, dwindling, or restricted — whether those things are objects, information, or freedoms.
2. **The scarcity principle draws power from two sources.** First, it exploits the mental shortcut that hard-to-get things are typically better (Lynn, 1989). Second, it triggers [[psychological-reactance]] — the drive to reassert freedoms that are threatened or eliminated (J. W. Brehm, 1966).
3. **Newly experienced scarcity is more potent than constant scarcity.** The cookie jar study (Worchel, Lee, & Adewole, 1975) demonstrated that cookies reduced from abundance to scarcity were rated more desirable than cookies that were always scarce. This principle extends to revolutions, civil unrest, and parenting.
4. **Competition for scarce resources amplifies desire to a fever pitch.** When scarcity is combined with rivalry — as in auctions, bidding wars, or the presence of a competing buyer — rational evaluation collapses and emotional, impulsive decision-making takes over.
5. **Defense requires a two-stage response.** First, use the rush of arousal itself as a warning signal to pause. Second, ask whether you want the item for its *function* (use, consume) or its *possession* — because scarcity does not improve functional value. "The scarce cookies didn't taste any better."

## Detailed Notes

### The Scarcity Principle — Opening

Cialdini opens with the story of the Mormon Temple in Mesa, Arizona. Before its consecration, it was opened to the public — a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity, since non-Mormons are permanently barred after consecration. Cialdini noticed something striking: the temple, its architecture, and its art were unremarkable, yet visitors were deeply moved. The scarcity of the opportunity — the soon-to-be-lost access — made the experience feel special.

The principle: *that opportunities seem more valuable to us when they are less available*.

**Everyday examples:**
- Interrupting a face-to-face conversation to answer an unknown phone caller, because the call possesses "potential unavailability" — miss it and it's gone forever
- Collectors prize "precious mistakes" — a blurred stamp, a double-struck coin — because imperfections bring scarcity; a three-eyed George Washington stamp is anatomically incorrect but highly valued

**Loss vs. gain framing:**
- People are more motivated by the thought of *losing* something than *gaining* something of equal value (Hobfoll, 2001)
- College students experienced much stronger emotions imagining losses vs. gains in romantic relationships and GPAs (Ketelaar, 1995)
- Connects directly to [[loss-aversion]] and [[prospect-theory]] (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981; De Dreu & McCusker, 1997)
- Health messages stressing potential losses (what you stand to lose by not getting screened) are more effective than gain-framed messages (Rothman & Salovey, 1997; Meyerwitz & Chaiken, 1987)
- Business managers weigh potential losses more heavily than potential gains (Shelley, 1994)
- The brain has evolved to make it more difficult to disrupt good decision-making about loss than gain (Weller et al., 2007)
- **Evolutionary explanation** (Haselton & Nettle, 2006): if you have enough to survive, an increase in resources is helpful but a decrease could be fatal, so sensitivity to loss is adaptive

### The "Precious Mistake" Phenomenon

In the collectibles market, flawed items — a blurred stamp, a double-struck coin — are sometimes the most valued of all. Imperfections that would otherwise make for rubbish instead make for prized possessions when they carry abiding scarcity.

### Limited Numbers Tactic

The most straightforward commercial exploitation of scarcity: informing customers that a product is in short supply and "can't be guaranteed to last long."

**Examples Cialdini observed while infiltrating sales operations:**
- "There aren't more than five convertibles with this engine left in the state."
- "This is one of only two unsold corner lots in the entire development."
- "You may want to think seriously about buying more than one case today because production is backed way up."

**The appliance store version** — the logical extreme: selling something at its scarcest point, when it can no longer be had. In one store where 30-50% of stock was on sale, salespeople would watch for moderately interested couples. They would approach, express sympathy ("unfortunately, I sold it to another couple not more than 20 minutes ago... it was the last one we had"). The customers' disappointment is visible. The salesperson offers to check the back room — but only if the customer commits to buying at this price. The customer agrees. The salesperson returns with the "found" appliance, pen and sales contract in hand. By the time they learn it's actually in supply, they've already publicly committed.

### Deadline Tactic

Related to [[limited-numbers]]: placing an official time limit on the customer's opportunity. People frequently do what they wouldn't otherwise care to do simply because the time to do so is running out.

**Examples:**
- Movie advertising: "Exclusive, limited engagement ends soon!" — invoking scarcity three times in five words
- High-pressure sellers use the ultimate deadline — *right now*: "This deal is good for today only; should you leave the premises, the deal is off"
- Child-portrait photography companies: "Stocking limitations force us to burn the unsold pictures of your children within 24 hours"
- Door-to-door magazine solicitors: "Salespeople are in the customer's area for just a day"
- Vacuum cleaner companies instruct trainees to say: "I have so many other people to see that I have the time to visit a family only once" — nonsense, since any return customer would be welcomed gladly

**The sales manager's candid admission:** The true purpose of the "can't come back" claim is to "keep the prospects from taking the time to think the deal over by scaring them into believing they can't have it later, which makes them want it now."

### Psychological Reactance

The power of scarcity comes from two sources:
1. **Shortcut heuristic**: Things difficult to get are typically better, so availability serves as a quick quality cue (McKenzie & Chase, in press). The belief is so ingrained that we also believe its obverse — that what's valuable must be scarce (Dai et al., 2008).
2. **[[psychological-reactance]]**: Developed by psychologist **Jack Brehm** (1966; Burgoon et al., 2002). Whenever free choice is limited or threatened, the need to retain our freedoms makes us want them (and the goods/services associated with them) significantly more than before. We *react against* the interference.

#### The Terrible Twos

Reactance first emerges around age 2, when children begin to see themselves as individuals rather than extensions of the social milieu (Howe, 2003). They develop a concept of autonomy, which brings the concept of freedom. Their contrariness is not defiance per se — it is a *quest for information* about the limits of their freedoms.

**Virginia toy study** (S. S. Brehm & Weintraub, 1977): Two-year-old boys were placed in a room with two equally attractive toys, one next to a transparent Plexiglas barrier. When the barrier was only 1 foot high (no real obstacle), boys showed no preference. When it was 2 feet high (a real barrier), boys went directly to the obstructed toy, making contact with it **three times faster** than the unobstructed one. Classic reactance: outright defiance.

*Note:* Two-year-old girls did not show the same resistance to the physical barrier; a follow-up study suggested girls are primarily reactant to restrictions from *people* rather than *physical barriers* (S. S. Brehm, 1981).

#### The Teenage Years

The second peak of reactance. Like two-year-olds, teenagers have an emerging sense of individuality — transitioning from child to adult, focusing on new rights more than new duties. Imposing traditional parental authority at this time is often counterproductive: teenagers sneak, scheme, and fight.

#### The Romeo and Juliet Effect

Nothing illustrates the boomerang quality of parental pressure on adolescent behavior more clearly. Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet — whose passion may have been *fueled*, not merely undeterred, by family barriers.

**Colorado study** (Driscoll, Davis, & Lipetz, 1972): 140 teenage couples. Parental interference was linked to some relationship problems (more critical views of each other) but simultaneously made pairs feel *greater love and desire for marriage*. As interference intensified, love intensified. When interference weakened, romantic feelings cooled.

**Reader's Report 7.2** — A 19-year-old woman dated a 27-year-old man she wasn't really interested in. "I didn't really become interested in him until my folks expressed their concern about his age. The more they got on my case about it, the more in love I became." The relationship lasted five months — "about four months longer than it would have lasted if my parents hadn't said anything."

#### Adult Reactance: Guns, Suds, and Coupons

**Kennesaw, Georgia gun law**: The town enacted a law requiring every adult to own a gun and ammunition, under penalty of six months in jail and $200 fine. Reactance theory predicted noncompliance. Result: gun sales boomed — but almost entirely from *out-of-town visitors*, not residents. Only those whose freedom was restricted by the law had the inclination to resist it; outsiders whose freedom was not at stake actually embraced scarcity-driven purchasing (Ruback & Juieng, 1997).

**Dade County (Miami) phosphate ban**: An antiphosphate ordinance banned laundry products containing phosphates. Two parallel reactions:
1. **Smuggling**: Miamians drove to nearby counties in large "soap caravans" to hoard phosphate detergents. Families boasted of 20-year supplies.
2. **Quality inflation**: Miami residents rated phosphate detergents as gentler, more effective in cold water, better whiteners, more powerful on stains — compared to Tampa residents unaffected by the ban. After the law, they even believed phosphate detergents *poured more easily* (Mazis, 1975; Mazis, Settle, & Leslie, 1973).

This demonstrates a crucial mechanism: when something becomes less available, we rarely recognize that reactance is making us want it more. We assume we want it because it's *good*. We assign positive qualities to justify our heightened desire — a causal confusion between desire and merit.

**Procter & Gamble coupon elimination** (Reader's Report 7.3): P&G tried eliminating savings coupons in upstate New York, replacing them with lower everyday prices. Despite only 2% of coupons being used, and consumers paying the same or less, the result was boycotts, protests, and a firestorm of complaints. "Coupons, to many people, are practically an inalienable right." Established freedoms are fought most ferociously.

### Censorship

The tendency to want what's been banned extends to information, not just commodities. Almost invariably, our response to banned information is to want it more *and* to become more favorable toward it, even if we haven't received it.

**Key studies:**
- **UNC coed dorms speech** (Worchel, Arnold, & Baker, 1975): Students told a speech opposing coed dorms on campus would be banned became *more opposed to coed dorms* — sympathizing with the argument they never heard
- **Purdue book restriction** (Zellinger, Fromkin, Speller, & Kohn, 1974): Students shown ads for a novel. Half saw "restricted to those 21 years and over." Those who learned of the restriction wanted to read it more *and* believed they would like it more
- **Courtroom evidence rulings** (Lieberman & Arndt, 2000): When judges declare evidence inadmissible, jurors who feel it's their right to consider all evidence may actually weight the banned evidence *more* — psychological reactance in the jury box
- **Choteau, Montana climate speech** (Brown, 2008): A superintendent canceled a Nobel laureate's climate change speech at a high school. Students subsequently became *more favorable* to the speaker's position — without ever hearing him

**Worrisome implication**: Fringe political groups might achieve more by getting their views officially censored and then publicizing the censorship, than by publicizing the views themselves. Perhaps the authors of the First Amendment were as much sophisticated social psychologists as civil libertarians.

**Heilman (1976)**: Supermarket shoppers were most likely to sign a petition favoring federal price controls after being told a federal official had *opposed the distribution* of the petition. Reactance drove political action.

### Exclusive Information — The Scarcity of Knowledge

Information need not be *censored* to be more persuasive — it need only be *scarce*. Timothy Brock and Howard Fromkin's "commodity theory" of persuasion (Brock, 1968; Fromkin & Brock, 1971): exclusive information is more persuasive.

**Knishinsky beef study (1982)** — the strongest experimental support:
- A beef-importing company's salespeople called buyers (supermarkets, retail food outlets) in three conditions:
  1. **Standard sales pitch** only
  2. Standard pitch + **scarcity information** (imported beef supply would be scarce in coming months)
  3. Standard pitch + scarcity information + **exclusive information** ("this news came from certain exclusive contacts that the company had")
- Results: Condition 2 (scarcity) produced buyers purchasing **2x** the standard amount. Condition 3 (scarcity + exclusive info) produced buyers purchasing **6x** the standard amount
- The scarcity double-whammy: the product was scarce *and* the news about it was scarce

### Optimal Conditions — The Cookie Jar Study

**Worchel, Lee, & Adewole (1975)** — the critical experiment on *when* scarcity works best:

**Basic design**: Participants in a consumer preference study tasted a chocolate chip cookie from a jar. Half received jars with 10 cookies; half received jars with 2 cookies. Results confirmed the basic scarcity principle: cookies from the jar of 2 were rated as more desirable, more attractive as a consumer item, and more costly than identical cookies from the jar of 10.

**Finding 1 — New scarcity is stronger than constant scarcity**: Some participants started with 10 cookies, then had them replaced with a jar of only 2. Others had only 2 from the start. The drop from abundance to scarcity produced a *decidedly more positive* reaction than constant scarcity.

**Finding 2 — Social-demand scarcity is strongest**: Among those who experienced the 10-to-2 reduction, two reasons were given:
- Some were told cookies had to be given to other raters to supply demand
- Some were told the researcher made a mistake and gave the wrong jar
- Result: Cookies that became scarce through **social demand** were liked significantly more than those scarce by mistake. In fact, cookies made scarce through social demand **were rated the most desirable of any condition in the entire study**.

### New Scarcity and Political Revolution

**James C. Davies' theory of revolution** (1962, 1969): Revolutions are most likely when a period of improving conditions is followed by a short, sharp reversal. It is not the most downtrodden who revolt — it is those given a taste of a better life and then threatened with losing it.

**Historical evidence:**
- **American Revolution**: Colonists had the highest standard of living and lowest taxes in the Western World; they revolted when Britain tried to cut into that prosperity (Fleming, 1997)
- **French, Russian, and Egyptian revolutions**: All preceded by periods of improvement followed by reversal
- **American Civil Rights Movement**: Between 1940 and the mid-1950s, black family income rose from 56% to 80% of comparably educated white family income. Then progress reversed: political gains met violent backlash (530 acts of violence after the 1954 school integration decision), and by 1962, black income had slipped back to 74%. The Birmingham riots of 1963 and subsequent upheavals followed this reversal.
- **Soviet Union coup of 1991**: Gorbachev's *glasnost* and *perestroika* granted new freedoms. When KGB officials staged a coup to restore the old order, the Soviet people — expected to acquiesce — instead took to the streets, erected barricades, confronted tanks, and defeated the coup in three days. "Freedoms once granted will not be relinquished without a fight."

**Parenting implication**: Parents who enforce rules erratically — sometimes prohibiting between-meal sweets, sometimes allowing them — are more likely to produce rebellious children than consistently strict parents (Lytton, 1979; O'Leary, 1995). The problem is not strictness but *inconsistency*, which creates and then revokes freedoms.

### Competition for Scarce Resources — "Foolish Fury"

Returning to the cookie study: among the newly scarce cookies, those that became scarce due to *social demand* (given away to other raters) were rated highest. Competition magnifies scarcity's effect.

**The "goosing 'em off the fence" tactic**: Realtors tell a fence-sitting prospect that another buyer (an "out-of-state investor" or "a physician and his wife moving into town") has seen the house and is returning to discuss terms. The fabricated rival transforms hesitation into urgency.

**Physical and emotional intensity**: Shoppers at close-out sales report being caught up emotionally, swarming and struggling for merchandise they would otherwise disdain — a "feeding frenzy" parallel to commercial fishermen throwing loose bait to create indiscriminate eating among fish. Department stores use "loss leaders" (prominently advertised, deeply discounted items) as bait to create the same competitive frenzy.

### The Great Poseidon Auction

**Barry Diller / ABC** paid $3.3 million for a single television showing of *The Poseidon Adventure* in 1973 — far exceeding the previous record of $2 million for *Patton*, and resulting in an expected $1 million loss. Why? It was the **first time** a movie was offered to networks via open-bid auction, the brainchild of producer Irwin Allen and 20th Century-Fox VP William Self.

**Robert Wood, CBS president** (the losing bidder): "We were very rational at the start. We priced the movie out, in terms of what it could bring in for us... But then the bidding started. ABC opened with $2 million. I came back with $2.4. ABC went $2.8. And the fever of the thing caught us. Like a guy who had lost his mind, I kept bidding... I went to $3.2; and there came a moment when I said to myself, 'Good grief, if I get it, what the heck am I going to do with it?' When ABC finally topped me, my main feeling was *relief*. It's been very educational."

**Lesson**: When competition and scarcity combine, the smiling man is the one who *lost* the highly sought-after prize. Barry Diller vowed ABC would "never again enter into an auction situation."

### Brother Richard's Used Car Tactic

Cialdini's brother Richard sold used cars privately by engineering competition for scarce resources. He scheduled all prospective buyers for *the same appointment time*. When the first buyer was leisurely inspecting the car, the second buyer would arrive — instantly transforming the situation from relaxed evaluation into a contest over a limited resource. The first buyer would often assert priority ("I was here first"), while the second would pace anxiously. If a third arrived, the pressure became irresistible. Buyers paid full asking price and rushed to decide, never stopping to consider that their desire was to *use* the car, not merely to *possess* it.

### Defense Against Scarcity

**The core problem**: Scarcity triggers emotional arousal that suppresses cognitive processing. As CBS president Robert Wood said: "you get caught up in the mania of the thing, the acceleration of it. Logic goes right out the window" (MacKenzie, 1974).

**Two-stage defense:**

1. **Use arousal as a warning signal.** Rather than relying on cognitive analysis (which scarcity suppresses), tune into the internal visceral sweep. When you feel the rising tide of emotional arousal in a compliance situation, flag it as a signal that scarcity tactics may be at work. Calm yourself and proceed with care.

2. **Ask: Do I want it for use or for possession?** The cookie study provides the key insight: scarce cookies were rated as more *desirable* but did not taste any *better*. "The joy is not in the experiencing of a scarce commodity but in the possessing of it." If you want something for its function — to eat it, drive it, use it — remember that scarce things do not taste or feel or work any better *because* of their limited availability. If you want it for the social, economic, or psychological benefits of *possessing* something rare, then scarcity is a legitimate guide to value.

**Reader's Report 7.4** — A Polish woman who had just read *Influence* was targeted at a supermarket tasting. She recognized consistency, reciprocity, and rejection-then-retreat tactics and resisted them all. But when the saleswoman mentioned the drink was imported from Brazil and might not be available in the future, she bought a tin anyway. The flavor was "okay but not great." Author's note: "To have armed herself optimally against it, she needed to remind herself that, like the scarce cookies, the scarce beverage didn't taste any better."
