# Guide to Managerial Communication Ch.3: Writing — Macro Issues — Summary

**Author(s):** Mary Munter, Lynn Hamilton
**Source:** *Guide to Managerial Communication*, 10th Edition (Pearson, 2014), Chapter 3
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Design documents for "high skim value" (HSV).** Busy readers skim before reading. Three techniques create HSV: (1) stand-alone headings that convey the essence of your ideas, (2) strategic white space that shows organization and emphasis, and (3) consistent typography that signals importance hierarchy.
2. **Every paragraph must begin with a generalization, then support it.** The generalization-and-support structure -- like a building with a roof (generalization broad enough to cover all columns) and columns (each sentence directly supporting the roof) -- is the fundamental unit of effective business writing.
3. **Introductions serve three purposes.** Establish common context ("what exists"), explain purpose ("why write"), and make structure explicit ("how organized" -- the roadmap statement). All three can sometimes be accomplished in a single sentence.
4. **Progression and linkage hold the document together.** Back-and-forth references, section previews, and transitional words create the connective tissue that lets busy readers absorb ideas quickly and see relationships between them.
5. **Closings must reinforce the communication objective.** Three effective closing types: feedback mechanism ("I will call you next Tuesday"), action step ("please return by July 9"), or goodwill ending. Avoid introducing new topics, apologizing, or ending abruptly.

## Detailed Notes

### I. Document Design for "High Skim Value"

Macrowriting applies to all genres of business writing -- memos, letters, reports, substantive emails. Three pillars:

#### 1. Using Headings and Subheadings

**Stand-alone sense (SAS):** Headings must make sense on their own, capturing the essence of your ideas. Many busy readers will read *only* headings and subheadings.

- Ineffective topic headings: "Recommendations," "Methodology," "Conclusions"
- Effective stand-alone headings: "Four reasons to divest," "Open new office in Singapore," "Empathy: Key to building relationships"

For longer documents, both main headings and subheadings need SAS independently, since they appear on different pages.

**Limited wording:** Reserve headings for important ideas only. Limit subheadings to six or seven words, never more than three-quarters of a line.

**Parallel form:** All headings at the same hierarchical level must be grammatically parallel (same construction -- all active verbs, all -ing forms, etc.) and conceptually parallel (same kind of item).

#### 2. Using White Space

**Break text into shorter blocks:** Keep paragraphs to 150-200 words, five sentences, or 1.5 inches of single-spaced text. Break emails into units of two to three sentences.

**Lists for visual emphasis:**
- Use lists only for items you want to emphasize visually
- Always have at least two, usually no more than seven items
- Always use parallel form
- Number points only to imply importance/sequence; otherwise use bullets
- Indent the entire list for readability

**Show organization through indentation:** Indent increasingly subordinate information to the right. Set off openings and closings flush left.

**Separate paragraphs:** Double-space between paragraphs or indent first lines.

**Choose unjustified (ragged right) margins:** Variable spacing between words ("rivers of space") in justified text irritates readers and slows reading.

**Embed tables** set off with white space for easy comparison of concepts or data.

#### 3. Choosing Typography

**For emphasis and consistency:**
- Reserve emphatic typography (boldface, italics, ALL CAPS) for headings only -- not random words mid-sentence
- Use a differentiated, consistent pattern across heading levels (e.g., boldface for main headings, something else for subheadings)

**Design cascade** (most to least emphatic):
- Placement: Centered > Flush left > Indented > Run-in
- Size: 14-16pt for headings, 11-12pt for text, 10pt for tables
- Font style: ALL CAPS > **Boldface** > *Italics* > Regular text

**For readability:**
- Serif fonts (Cambria, Times, Palatino) for traditional/printed documents; sans serif (Calibri, Arial, Helvetica) for modern look and headings
- Use sentence case for extended text (avoid ALL CAPS blocks and Title Case for body text)
- Avoid italics for extended text -- harder to read than regular type

### II. Clear Progression and Linkage

#### 1. Throughout the Message

**Back-and-forth references:** At the end or beginning of each major section, reference both the previous section (backward look) and the upcoming section (forward look). Example: "If you adopt **this new marketing plan** (backward), you can expect **the following financial results** (forward)."

**Section previews:** In longer documents, open each new section with a sentence previewing its contents. This is the written equivalent of a spoken roadmap.

#### 2. In the Introduction

Three aims for every introduction:
1. **Establish common context ("what exists")** -- build reader receptivity by referring to existing situation or shared common ground
2. **Explain purpose ("why write")** -- state what you want to tell them, what you want them to do, or what your opinion is
3. **Make structure explicit ("how organized")** -- provide a roadmap statement that outlines the message structure

**Ordering the three elements:**
- High credibility or receptive audience: explain purpose first
- Lower credibility or less receptive audience: establish common context first

**Length:** A long document might have a paragraph for each aim. A short email can accomplish all three in one sentence: "As you requested last week (= what exists), I have summarized my three objections to the new marketing plan (= why write, = how organized)."

#### 3. In the Closing

**Effective closings:**
- *Feedback mechanism* -- "I will call you next Tuesday to discuss"
- *Action step* -- "If you wish to apply, please return the enclosed application by July 9"
- *Goodwill ending* -- "I look forward to working with you on this project"

**Ineffective closings:**
- Introducing a new topic or information
- Apologizing or undercutting your argument
- Ending abruptly

### III. Effective Paragraphs and Sections

#### 1. Generalization and Support

Each paragraph or section must be built around a single unifying purpose. Every paragraph should begin with a **generalization** (topic sentence or heading), and every subsequent sentence should **support** that generalization.

The building metaphor: the generalization is like a **roof** -- broad enough to cover every column. The support sentences are the **columns** -- each one directly holding up the roof.

- Topic sentences work for standard prose paragraphs
- Headings/subheadings work for HSV prose

**Development and support:** The generalization must be fully developed with sufficient evidence. Avoid one-sentence paragraphs (they lack development and don't group ideas logically).

#### 2. Paragraph and Section Signposts

Two techniques for showing connection among sentences within a paragraph:

**Document design techniques:** Use headings, subheadings, bullet points, indentation, and typography to show how ideas connect visually.

**Transitional words:** Use explicit connectors to show relationships:

| Signal | Examples |
|--------|----------|
| Addition | And, furthermore, besides, moreover, in addition, also |
| Contrast | But, or, yet, still, however, nevertheless, on the contrary, although |
| Example | For example, for instance, such as, that is |
| Sequence | First, second, third, next, then |
| Conclusion | Therefore, thus, then, in conclusion, consequently, as a result |
| Time/place | At the same time, simultaneously, above, below, so far |
