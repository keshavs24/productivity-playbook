# Contagious Ch.4: Public — Summary

**Author:** Jonah Berger
**Source:** *Contagious: Why Things Catch On* (2013), Chapter 4
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **If it's built to show, it's built to grow.** Making products, ideas, and behaviors more publicly observable makes them easier to imitate, which makes them more likely to catch on. Public visibility is a key factor in driving products to spread.
2. **Monkey see, monkey do — but you can't copy what you can't see.** People imitate others' choices to reduce uncertainty ([[social-proof]]), but imitation requires observability. Shirts are public and socks are private, which is why fashion trends spread faster for outerwear.
3. **Behavioral residue** is the physical traces or remnants that actions and behaviors leave in their wake. Livestrong wristbands, "I Voted" stickers, and Hotmail email signatures create lasting evidence of behavior that continues to advertise even after the initial action is over.
4. **Making the private public can backfire.** Anti-drug commercials increased drug use among teens by making drug use seem common and socially sanctioned. When trying to prevent a behavior, don't tell people that lots of their peers are doing it — make others' *good* behavior more visible instead.

## Detailed Notes

### Apple's Upside-Down Logo
Steve Jobs wanted the Apple logo on PowerBook laptops to face the user when closed so they could orient the laptop easily. But Ken Segall (Jobs's ad agency creative director) pointed out that when users opened the laptop in a coffee shop, the logo appeared upside down to everyone else. Jobs ultimately flipped the logo so it looked right to *observers*, not the user. The reason? **Observability.** Jobs realized that seeing others use Apple products made people more likely to adopt them. Making something more observable makes it easier to imitate.

### The Psychology of Imitation
People imitate others for two reasons:
1. **Others' choices provide information.** Like choosing a crowded restaurant over an empty one, we use others' behavior as a signal of quality. Psychologists call this [[social-proof]].
2. **Social pressure.** Television shows use canned laugh tracks because people are more likely to laugh when they hear others laughing.

**Kidney transplant refusals:** MIT professor Juanjuan Zhang found that [[social-proof]] leads people to turn down available kidneys. If a kidney has been refused by ninety-nine other people before reaching you, you infer it must be low quality — even though many refusals are based on tissue/blood type mismatches. Thousands of patients turn down kidneys they should have accepted.

**Halal Guys vs. Halal Guys:** Two identical food carts in NYC — same food, same packaging, run by the same owners. One has a massive line, the other has none. People assume the longer the line, the better the food. Social proof in action.

**MBA career herding:** MBA students enter with diverse career interests but converge dramatically. By the end of the program, more than two-thirds want investment banking or consulting. A few people switch, which causes a few more to switch, creating a social influence snowball.

### The Power of Observability
Koreen Johannessen at the University of Arizona found that most students were *uncomfortable* with the drinking culture, but they couldn't observe others' private thoughts. They saw peers drinking and assumed everyone enjoyed it. Because behavior is public and thoughts are private, students mistakenly believed they were the only ones uncomfortable with binge drinking.

This extends broadly: at the end of a confusing presentation, the speaker asks for questions and gets silence. Not because everyone understood, but because no one wants to be the one to raise their hand. Everyone keeps doubts private, creating a false consensus.

**Observability drives purchase decisions.** [[berger]] and colleagues Eric Bradlow and Blake McShane analyzed 1.5 million car sales and found that a neighbor buying a new car made you more likely to buy one too — approximately one out of every eight cars sold was because of social influence. The effect was stronger in places where cars are more observable (sunny cities like Los Angeles and Miami vs. rainy cities like Seattle).

Observable things are also more likely to be discussed. Public visibility boosts word of mouth.

### Making the Private Public — With Moustaches
**Movember Foundation:** In 2003, friends in Melbourne challenged each other to grow moustaches for November. They later attached a cause — men's cancer research. By 2007, the annual event had spread globally, raising more than $174 million. Movember succeeded because it figured out how to **make the private public** — taking support for an abstract cause and making it visible. For thirty days, moustache-wearers become walking, talking billboards.

### Advertising Itself: Hotmail
Hotmail cleverly leveraged observability. Every email sent from a Hotmail account included a footer: "Get Your Private, Free E-mail from Hotmail at www.hotmail.com." Every email was an implicit endorsement — visible [[social-proof]]. In little over a year, Hotmail signed up more than 8.5 million subscribers. Microsoft bought it for $400 million.

Apple adopted the same strategy with "Sent from my iPhone." iPod's distinctive white headphones served the same function — when most devices came with black headphones, Apple's white ones made iPod usage publicly observable.

**Products that advertise themselves:** Louboutin's red-lacquered soles, Pringles' unique tube, Lululemon's durable shopping bags. Tailors give away bags with their name. Nightclubs use sparklers for bottle service. The easier it is for people to see someone using a product, the more the product benefits from [[social-proof]].

### Livestrong Wristbands as Behavioral Residue
Scott MacEachern at Nike developed the Livestrong wristband to capitalize on Lance Armstrong's popularity. He chose yellow because it stands out against almost any clothing and is rarely seen in accessories — maximizing observability. Over 85 million wristbands were sold.

**[[behavioral-residue]]** is the physical traces that actions leave behind — trophies, T-shirts, bumper stickers, medals from 5Ks. Unlike the actions themselves (which are temporary), behavioral residue persists, creating ongoing [[social-proof]]. "The nice thing about a wristband is that it lives on. The bike ride doesn't."

Examples of behavioral residue: Tiffany bags reused for gym clothes, Lululemon shopping bags used for groceries, "I Voted" stickers, Like buttons on Facebook (250% traffic boost for ABC News after installing them), Spotify publishing listening activity to friends' feeds.

### Anti-Drug Commercials — When Making Things Public Backfires
Congress spent nearly $1 billion on the National Youth Anti-Drug Media Campaign between 1998 and 2004. Communications professor Bob Hornik studied whether the ads decreased drug use. **They didn't. The messages actually seemed to *increase* drug use.** Kids aged twelve and a half to eighteen who saw the ads were more likely to smoke marijuana.

Why? The ads made drug use **more public.** By telling kids to say no to drugs, the ads simultaneously communicated that lots of other kids were doing drugs. And as [[social-proof]] predicts, the more others seem to be doing something, the more normal it appears.

**The same principle applies to other prevention campaigns.** The music industry's warning that "only 37% of music was paid for" essentially told people that *most* people pirate music. Cialdini's Petrified Forest study found that signs saying "many past visitors have removed petrified wood" *doubled* the number of people taking wood — by providing [[social-proof]] that stealing was common.

**The solution:** Rather than making the private public, make the public private — or better yet, highlight the *positive* behavior. Johannessen reduced binge drinking at Arizona by 30% by publishing ads showing that most students actually had only one or two drinks when they party. By making the *true norm* (moderation) visible, she corrected the false inference students had been making.
