# SDT Ch.8: Organismic Integration Theory — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ryan-deci|Richard M. Ryan & Edward L. Deci]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. **OIT is the second mini-theory within SDT**, addressing the internalization and differentiation of [[extrinsic-motivation]]
2. **Extrinsic motivation is not monolithic** -- four regulatory styles: [[external-regulation]], [[introjected-regulation]], [[identified-regulation]], and [[integrated-regulation]]
3. **Internalization is a natural organismic process** dependent on [[basic-psychological-needs]] satisfaction
4. **The continuum of relative autonomy** orders regulatory styles from least to most autonomous, verified by simplex patterns (Ryan & Connell, 1989)
5. **Internalization is not a developmental stage sequence** -- people can enter the continuum at any point; transitions can be progressive or regressive
6. **Compartmentalized identifications** represent a failure of integration where a person defensively isolates an identification from other self-knowledge
7. **Self-concordance** of personal goals predicts goal attainment, need satisfaction, and well-being (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999)

## Detailed Notes

### The Concept of Internalization

[[internalization|Internalization]] is the internal psychological process corresponding to socialization. It is the active process by which individuals take in values, beliefs, and behavioral regulations from external sources and transform them into their own (Ryan, Connell, & Deci, 1985). It is a manifestation of [[organismic-integration]], serves [[basic-psychological-needs]], has evolutionary basis in group cooperation, and can produce darker outcomes (internalized prejudice, malevolence).

### OIT Proposition I

> *The process of organismic integration inclines humans naturally to internalize extrinsic motivations endorsed by significant others. However, internalization can function more versus less effectively, resulting in different degrees of internalization that differ in perceived locus of causality and autonomy.*

### Types of Internalization and Regulation

#### 1. External Regulation
- Behavior dependent upon external reward or punishment contingencies
- External perceived locus of causality (E-PLOC) -- behavior is controlled
- Poor maintenance and transfer without contingency (Deci & Ryan, 1985b; Ryan & Deci, 2008b)
- People experience behaviors as merely instrumental
- Example: girl abstains from drinking only when parents might catch her; boy helps others only when incentive program offers points
- Connects to [[social-norms-vs-market-norms]]

#### 2. Introjected Regulation
- Partial and incomplete internalization -- taking in a regulation without fully transforming it
- Introject experienced as demanding and controlling force, internal but still relatively external to self
- Associated with: guilt, shame, contingent self-esteem, pride (contingent), [[ego-involvement]], self-critical perfectionism
- More enduring than external regulation because based on affective/evaluative contingencies within the individual
- Form of internal control -- one part of person controlling other parts
- Frequently vitality draining or ego depleting even when efficacious (Nix, Ryan, Manly, & Deci, 1999)
- Produces false self-esteem that is unstable (Kernis & Paradise, 2002; Ryan & Brown, 2006)
- Strongly related to projection -- people project approval/disapproval onto others
- Relates to self-infiltration (Kuhl & Kazen, 1994) and failure of self-compatibility checking (Kazen, Baumann, & Kuhl, 2003)
- Self-control via introjection is NOT autonomy
- **Key connection: [[want-should-conflict]]** -- introjected regulation IS the internal mechanism of the want-should tension

#### 3. Identified Regulation
- Person has consciously endorsed the value and personal importance of a behavior
- More volitional, less conflicted than introjection
- More stable, persistent, associated with positive affect
- Does not necessarily imply full self-compatibility checking
- Compartmentalization possible -- identified but defensively isolated from other identifications
- Connects to [[commitment-and-consistency]] -- resembles internalized commitment

#### 4. Integrated Regulation
- Most complete form of internalization
- Regulation brought into coherence with other values, needs, identifications
- Involves self-reflection and reciprocal assimilation
- Experienced as fully authentic (Ryan & Deci, 2004a, 2006)
- Requires self-compatibility check (Kazen et al., 2003)
- Still extrinsic -- done for importance/congruence with values, not inherent enjoyment
- Neural correlates: depends on right MPFC for self-knowledge processing (Ryan, Kuhl, & Deci, 1997); Di Domenico et al. (2013, 2016) showed higher need satisfaction associated with MPFC activation during difficult decisions

### The Continuum of Relative Autonomy

**OIT Proposition II:** Internalization of extrinsic motivation spans a continuum from heteronomous/controlled to autonomous self-regulation.

**Empirical verification:**
- Ryan and Connell (1989): SRQ-A confirmed quasi-simplex pattern
- Table 8.1 correlations: Intrinsic-Integrated (.46), Integrated-Identified (.48), Identified-Introjected (.34), Introjected-External (.40), External-Amotivation (.27)
- Replicated by Vallerand and Bissonnette (1992), Yamauchi and Tanaka (1998)
- MDS confirmed ordered placement (Roth et al., 2006)
- Bifactor-ESEM models (Howard et al., 2016; Litalien et al., 2016)
- Simplex/circumplex modeling from U.S. and Russian samples (Sheldon et al., 2016)

**Assessment tools:**
- **Relative Autonomy Index (RAI):** external=-2, introjected=-1, identified=+1, intrinsic=+2. Predictive but obscures profiles.
- **Latent Profile Analyses (LPA):** Wang et al. (2016) found four-type profiling more differentiated than two higher-order factors.

### Intrinsic Motivation vs. Internalized Regulation

- Intrinsic motivation: present-focused, spontaneous, inherent enjoyment
- Internalized regulation: instrumental, done because important for one's goals/values
- Davis et al. (2016): Higher-level construal fosters more meaning and integrated motivation

### Development and the Continuum

Not a developmental stage sequence. People can enter at any point. Transitions out of sequence possible (external directly to identification). Regressive transitions occur under controlling conditions (Deci, Schwartz, Sheinman, & Ryan, 1981).

### Compartmentalization

Compartmentalized identifications: strong but defensively isolated from other self-knowledge. Weinstein et al. (2012): four studies showing controlling/homophobic parental climates produced discrepancies between explicit sexual identifications and implicit orientations, demonstrating compartmentalized identification associated with anti-gay bias.

### Need Support and Internalization

**OIT Proposition III:** Supports for competence, relatedness, and autonomy facilitate internalization and integration. Controlling contexts thwart internalization.

- Competence required at all levels
- Relatedness central for energizing internalization, but alone produces only introjection (conditional regard: Roth et al., 2009)
- Autonomy critical for moving beyond introjection to identification/integration

### Research on Internalization and Social Context

**Deci et al. (1994):** Three facilitating factors (rationale, acknowledging feelings, choice) increased internalization of boring task. Need-supportive conditions produced integrated internalization; non-supportive produced introjected.

**Legault et al. (2011):** Autonomy-supportive nonprejudice messages reduced implicit and explicit prejudice; controlling messages increased explicit prejudice. OIT relative autonomy mediated effects.

**Grolnick and Ryan (1989):** Parental autonomy support predicted children's academic autonomy. Controlling parents (often well-intentioned) undermined autonomous internalization.

**Williams et al. (2002):** Physician autonomy support predicted autonomous patient motivation for smoking cessation.

**Williams et al. (1996):** Autonomy support in weight-loss clinic predicted autonomous motivation, attendance, weight loss at 6 months, and maintenance at 2-year follow-up.

**Ng et al. (2012):** Meta-analysis of 184 datasets -- autonomy support strongly predicted autonomous health-behavior motivation.

### Consequences of Internalization

**OIT Proposition IV:** More autonomous internalization produces greater persistence, higher quality behavior, more effective performance.

**OIT Proposition V:** More integrated internalization produces more positive experiences and greater psychological health.

### Persistence and Goal Attainment Studies

- Greenstein and Koestner (1996): Identified/intrinsic reasons predicted resolution maintenance
- Pelletier et al. (2001): Identified/intrinsic motivation predicted athletic persistence over time; external regulation was negative predictor that weakened further
- Koestner et al. (1996): Identification predicted voting and information seeking; introjection predicted susceptibility to persuasion
- Koestner and Losier (1999): For non-enjoyable important activities, identification stronger predictor than intrinsic motivation

### Self-Concordance

Sheldon and Elliot (1999): Self-concordant goals led to sustained effort, attainment, need satisfaction, well-being. Upward spiral identified (Sheldon & Houser-Marko, 2001). Koestner et al. (2002): Self-concordance and implementation intentions both independently predicted and interacted positively for goal progress.

### Well-Being Studies

- Ryan et al. (1993): Identified religious regulation related to self-actualization and self-esteem; introjected religiosity related to anxiety, depression, somatization
- Vallerand and O'Connor (1989): Among elderly, autonomous motivation associated with self-esteem and meaning; nonautonomous associated with depression
- Miller et al. (2011): Cross-cultural -- among Indians, helping family expectations correlated with identification and satisfaction (not among Americans)

### Amotivation

Two sources: (1) perceived incompetence/behavior-outcome independence, (2) lack of value or interest. Has both autonomous and controlled forms (Vansteenkiste et al., 2004).
