# Predictably Irrational Ch.4-5: Social Norms and Market Norms — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ariely|Dan Ariely]]
**Type:** Book chapters
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. We live simultaneously in two worlds: one governed by **social norms** (warm, fuzzy, community-oriented) and one by **market norms** (sharp-edged, transactional, cost-benefit)
2. Introducing money into social exchanges destroys the social norm -- and once destroyed, it is very hard to restore
3. People work **harder** for a cause (social norm) than for low cash (market norm)
4. Gifts maintain social norms; mentioning the price of a gift converts it to a market norm
5. When items are FREE (Ch.5), social norms kick in -- people become **less selfish**, taking fewer items and considering others' welfare

## Detailed Notes

### Chapter 4: The Cost of Social Norms

#### Two Worlds of Exchange (Clark, Mills, Fiske)
- **Social norms:** Friendly requests, community, no instant payback required. "Could you help me move this couch?" Reciprocity is fuzzy and delayed
- **Market norms:** Sharp, transactional. Wages, prices, rents, cost-benefit. You get what you pay for
- When kept separate, both work well. When they collide, trouble sets in. Example: offering to pay your mother-in-law for Thanksgiving dinner

#### The Circle-Dragging Experiment (Ariely & James Heyman)
Participants dragged circles across a computer screen for 5 minutes:
- **$5 group:** Dragged 159 circles (market norms -- decent effort for decent pay)
- **50-cent group:** Dragged 101 circles (market norms -- low pay, low effort)
- **No payment group (social norms):** Dragged 168 circles -- **more than the $5 group**

People worked harder as volunteers under social norms than for a small wage under market norms. Once market norms entered, effort correlated with pay.

#### The AARP Lawyers Example
- AARP asked lawyers if they would offer services to needy retirees for ~$30/hour. Lawyers said **no** -- $30 was below market rate
- Then AARP asked if they would volunteer their services for **free**. Lawyers overwhelmingly said **yes**
- How could zero dollars be more attractive than $30? Because zero invoked social norms (volunteering), while $30 invoked market norms (underpaid labor)

#### The Gift Experiment
Ariely and Heyman replaced cash with gifts:
- **Snickers bar (worth ~50 cents):** 162 circles dragged
- **Box of Godiva chocolates (worth ~$5):** 169 circles
- **No gift (social norms):** 168 circles
- All three groups worked about equally hard -- gifts keep us in the social exchange world

But when they mentioned the **price** of the gift ("a 50-cent Snickers bar"), effort dropped to match the 50-cent cash condition. **Mentioning cost converts a gift into market exchange.**

#### Vohs, Mead, and Goode: Merely Thinking About Money
Kathleen Vohs et al. ran experiments where participants unscrambled salary-related vs. neutral sentences:
- "Salary" group was **more self-reliant** (waited 5.5 min before asking for help vs. 3 min)
- Also **less willing to help others** (less likely to help an experimenter, less likely to assist a confused participant, less likely to pick up dropped pencils)
- More selfish, more individualistic, chose solitary tasks over teamwork

Just **thinking** about money shifts people from social to market mode.

#### The Israeli Day Care Center (Gneezy & Rustichini)
Classic study on the long-term effects of mixing norms:
- Day care imposed a fine on late-picking-up parents
- Before the fine: social norm (guilt) kept lateness in check
- After the fine: parents treated it as a market transaction and came late more often
- **When the fine was removed**, the social norm did NOT return -- parents continued to be late, and lateness even slightly increased. Once a social norm is replaced by a market norm, it rarely returns

#### Implications for Business and Society
- Companies invest billions creating social relationships with customers ("Like a good neighbor...") but then violate them with fees, penalties, and impersonal treatment
- Open-source software thrives on social norms -- people contribute code for free because it's a community, not a job
- **Employee motivation:** Social norms (purpose, loyalty, flexibility) can be more powerful than market norms (salary). But companies that cut benefits, outsource, and reduce perks are shifting to market norms -- and then wonder why employees have no loyalty
- **Education:** Standardized testing and performance-based pay push education from social norms toward market norms, which may backfire

### Chapter 5: The Power of a Free Cookie

#### Free Activates Social Norms
This chapter extends the social/market norms framework by showing that FREE items trigger social norm behavior:

#### The Starburst Candy Experiment (Ariely, Uri Gneezy, Ernan Haruvy)
Set up a candy booth at MIT:
- **1 cent per Starburst (monetary condition):** ~58 students/hour stopped, taking an average of 3.5 candies each
- **Free Starbursts:** ~207 students/hour stopped (3x more), but took an average of only **1.1 candies each**

The second law of demand predicts that cheaper = take more. But FREE violated this: students took **fewer** per person. They politely took one, considering others. Free activated social norms; even a penny activated market norms where selfishness was acceptable.

#### The Lindt Truffle Replication
When chocolates dropped from 1 cent to free:
- More people stopped (first law of demand confirmed)
- Each person took **fewer** (second law of demand violated)
- Net total demand **decreased by 50%** when price dropped to free

This "backward sloping demand curve" only appears at the zero price point -- confirming it is the shift from market to social norms, not a standard price effect.

#### The Effort Experiment at MIT Media Lab
Three conditions for getting Lindt truffles:
- **Monetary (1 cent each):** Average of 30 truffles taken per person
- **Free:** Average of 1.5 truffles per person
- **Effort (find pairs of s's in letter sheets):** Average of 8.6 truffles per person

Effort falls between free and monetary -- it maintains social norms to a large degree but not as powerfully as free. "Effort is money" is not exactly correct.

#### Why the Last Olive Feels Like a Big Deal
When food is served communally (shared sushi plate, party appetizers), people restrain themselves. The communal plate transforms food into a shared resource governed by social norms. This is why we hesitate to take the last piece.

#### Policy Implications
- Cap-and-trade puts a **price** on pollution, moving it from social to market norms -- companies may simply "buy" the right to pollute
- Making pollution a measurable, socially visible quantity (not tradeable) keeps it in the social norm domain where people actually care
