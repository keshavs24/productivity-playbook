# Choices, Values, and Frames — Summary

**Author(s):** [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]] and [[tversky|Amos Tversky]]
**Type:** Overview paper (APA Distinguished Scientific Contributions Award address)
**Published:** *American Psychologist*, 39:4, 341-350, 1984. Reprinted as Ch. 1 in *Choices, Values, and Frames* (2000).
**Phase:** 2 — Decision Science
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Prospect theory's principles extend beyond risky choice to riskless transactions and trades.** The psychophysics of value (S-shaped value function, loss aversion) and the psychophysics of chance (nonlinear decision weights) apply to everyday consumer decisions, not just gambles.
2. **Framing effects violate the invariance principle** -- the most basic requirement of rational choice. The Asian Disease Problem demonstrates that identical options produce opposite preferences depending on whether outcomes are framed as lives saved (gains) or lives lost (losses).
3. **Mental accounting determines how transactions are framed.** People spontaneously adopt topical accounts that evaluate gains and losses relative to context-specific reference points, producing systematic deviations from economic rationality.
4. **The cost-loss distinction matters.** The same expenditure is treated differently depending on whether it is coded as a cost (exchange of money for goods) or as an uncompensated loss.
5. **Experience value and decision value diverge.** Decision values (what drives choices) often fail to correspond to experience values (actual hedonic outcomes).

## Detailed Notes

### Part 1: Risky Choice

The paper sketches [[prospect-theory]]'s approach to risky choice using psychophysical principles. Three key properties of the [[value-function]]:

1. **[[reference-dependence]]:** Value is defined on gains and losses relative to a reference point, not on final wealth states
2. **Diminishing sensitivity:** The value function is concave for gains and convex for losses (S-shaped)
3. **[[loss-aversion]]:** The function is steeper for losses than gains. Most people refuse to stake $10 on a coin toss unless the potential gain exceeds $30.

The **[[decision-weights]]** function captures the psychophysics of chance: decision weights are regressive relative to probabilities, producing the characteristic fourfold pattern of risk attitudes.

### Part 2: Framing of Outcomes

The **Asian Disease Problem** -- the most famous demonstration of [[framing-effects]]:

- **Problem 1 (gains frame):** "200 people will be saved" (72%) vs. "1/3 probability 600 saved" (28%) -- risk aversion
- **Problem 2 (losses frame):** "400 people will die" (22%) vs. "2/3 probability 600 die" (78%) -- risk seeking

Options are mathematically identical, yet produce opposite preferences. The failure of invariance is pervasive, robust, and persists even when subjects are confronted with their inconsistency.

**Concurrent decisions produce dominated portfolios** (Problems 3 and 4): 73% chose the dominated combination (A+D) over the dominant one (B+C), because they evaluated each decision separately. This demonstrates that narrow framing of concurrent decisions can violate dominance.

The **[[pseudocertainty]] effect** is demonstrated: a probabilistically uncertain outcome is weighted as if certain when embedded in a sequential structure.

### Part 3: Transactions and Trades

This section extends prospect theory to **riskless choice**. Key innovations:

**[[mental-accounting]]** (credited to [[thaler|Richard Thaler]]): People frame decisions in terms of topical accounts rather than comprehensive wealth accounts. The **calculator/jacket problem** shows that 68% would drive 20 minutes to save $5 on a $15 calculator, but only 29% would drive the same distance to save $5 on a $125 calculator -- same $5 saving, different topical accounts.

**The lost ticket problem:** Losing a $10 ticket (only 46% would buy another) vs. losing a $10 bill (88% would still buy the ticket). The lost ticket is posted to the theater account, doubling the perceived cost; the lost cash is not.

Three types of accounts: **minimal** (only differences between options), **topical** (context-determined reference level), and **comprehensive** (total wealth). People spontaneously use topical accounts.

### Part 4: Losses and Costs

The paper draws a crucial distinction between **losses** and **costs**:
- Payments for goods are coded as costs (money exchanged for value), not losses
- Insurance can be framed as either a sure loss or a cost of protection -- opposite preferences result
- 80% chose a gamble over a sure loss of $50, but only 35% refused to pay $50 for insurance against a $200 loss

The **dead-loss effect** (from Thaler): A man with tennis elbow continues playing in agony after paying the club membership. Stopping would force recognition of the fee as a dead loss rather than a cost of playing.

The cost-loss asymmetry for gains: a gamble offering 10% chance to win $95 and 90% chance to lose $5 was rejected by many who accepted the equivalent lottery ($5 cost for 10% chance to win $100). Framing the $5 as a payment rather than a loss makes the venture more acceptable.

### Part 5: Experience Value vs. Decision Value

The paper distinguishes:
- **Experience value:** actual pleasure/pain from outcomes
- **Decision value:** the contribution of anticipated outcomes to the attractiveness of options

These often diverge. Hedonic adaptation shifts reference points, so an objective improvement can be experienced as a loss if it falls short of the new reference. The framing of decisions affects not only choices but sometimes the actual experience of outcomes.

## New Concepts Introduced

- [[pseudocertainty]] — strengthened
- Cost-loss distinction — Costs are less aversive than uncompensated losses of equal magnitude
- Topical accounting — The natural tendency to evaluate outcomes relative to context-specific reference points
- Experience value vs. decision value distinction

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **With rational choice theory:** Framing effects directly violate the invariance axiom
- **Complements [[summary-1-02-prospect-theory]]:** Extends the 1979 theory from simple gambles to riskless choice, transactions, and multi-attribute options

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "The failure of invariance is both pervasive and robust. It is as common among sophisticated respondents as among naive ones, and it is not eliminated even when the same respondents answer both questions within a few minutes."

> "In their stubborn appeal, framing effects resemble perceptual illusions more than computational errors."

> "Because losses loom larger than gains, consumers are less likely to accept a surcharge than to forego a discount."
