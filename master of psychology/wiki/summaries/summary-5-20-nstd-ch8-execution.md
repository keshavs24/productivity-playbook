# Never Split the Difference Ch.8: Guarantee Execution — Summary

**Authors:** [[chris-voss]], [[tahl-raz]]
**Source:** *[[5-20 - Never Split the Difference - Voss, Raz]]*, Chapter 8
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **"Yes" is nothing without "How"** — an agreement is nice, but a contract is better and a signed check is best. You don't get your profits with the agreement; they come upon implementation. Negotiators must be decision architects.
2. **The [[rule-of-three]]**: Get your counterpart to agree to the same thing three times in the same conversation — using different methods (a commitment, a "That's Right" summary, and a calibrated "How" question about implementation). It's nearly impossible to repeatedly lie or fake conviction.
3. **The [[seven-thirty-eight-fifty-five-rule]]**: Only 7% of a message is based on words; 38% comes from tone of voice; 55% from body language (Albert Mehrabian, UCLA). Pay close attention to incongruence between words and nonverbal signals.
4. **The Pinocchio Effect**: Liars use more words, more third-person pronouns, and more complex sentences than truth-tellers (Deepak Malhotra, Harvard). People distancing from lies switch from "I" to "we/they/them."
5. **There is always a team on the other side** — if you are not influencing the people behind the table ("Level II" players), you are vulnerable. Deal killers are often more important than deal makers.
6. **Use calibrated "How" questions to say "No" four times before actually saying it** — "How am I supposed to do that?" / "Your offer is very generous, I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me" / "I'm sorry but I'm afraid I just can't do that" / "I'm sorry, no."

## Detailed Notes

### St. Martin Parish Prison Siege

The chapter opens with a prison hostage situation in Louisiana where negotiators designed an elaborate surrender ritual involving walkie-talkies. The plan failed when a SWAT officer confiscated the first prisoner's walkie-talkie, causing panic inside. The near-disaster illustrates that getting to "Yes" is only half the battle — execution requires accounting for every player, including those on your own side.

**Core principle**: Your job isn't just to get to an agreement; it's to get to one that can be implemented and making sure that happens.

### "Yes" Is Nothing Without "How" — The Ecuador Kidnapping

Jose, an American-Ecuadoran tour guide, was kidnapped by Colombian-based rebels demanding $5 million:

- Voss's team coached wife Julie to answer every demand with [[calibrated-questions]]: "How do I know Jose is alive?" / "How can we pay you anything until we know Jose is okay?"
- The calibrated questions forced the kidnappers' negotiator to walk to town each time, then return to the jungle for group deliberations — all the delays and questions caused guerrillas to peel off
- Julie drove the ransom from $5M down to $16,500 using only questions and minuscule offers
- Jose eventually escaped when only one teenager was guarding him at night during a rainstorm

**Key insight from Jose's debrief**: The calibrated questions didn't just buy time — they forced unprecedented internal coordination among the kidnappers. "Because Julie kept asking him questions he didn't really know for sure how to answer, he kept coming out to the jungle."

### "How" Questions as Gentle "No"s

The trick to "How" questions: correctly used, they are gentle and graceful ways to say "No" and guide your counterpart to develop a better solution — your solution.

- "How am I supposed to do that?" makes the other side take a good look at your situation
- This creates "forced empathy" — especially effective if you've already been empathic with your counterpart
- **Kelly the accountant**: Owed money by a corporate client. Waited until the client asked for more work, then said: "I'd love to help, but how am I supposed to do that?" The deadbeat client had no choice but to put her needs first. She got paid.

### Two Key Implementation Questions

1. "How will we know we're on track?"
2. "How will we address things if we find we're off track?"

When they answer, summarize their answers until you get a "That's right." Then you know they've bought in.

**Warning signs of fake buy-in:**
- "You're right" (not "That's right") — means they're not vested
- "I'll try" — really means "I plan to fail"

When you hear either, dive back in with calibrated "How" questions until they define successful implementation in their own words.

### Influencing Those Behind the Table

- Few hostage-takers and few business deal makers fly solo — there are almost always other players who can act as deal makers or deal killers
- You must discover how to affect these "Level II" players — ask: "How does this affect the rest of your team?" / "How on board are the people not on this call?"
- **Voss's Florida training deal failure**: Had total buy-in from the CEO and HR head, but the division head who "needed" the training felt threatened. The deal collapsed because of one unaccounted-for Level II player.
- **It only takes one bit player to screw up a deal.**

### Spotting Liars, Dealing with Jerks, and Charming Everyone Else

Truly effective negotiators are conscious of verbal, paraverbal (how it's said), and nonverbal communications. They employ subtleties to their benefit, such as using "not lose" instead of "keep."

#### The Alastair Onglingswan Kidnapping (Philippines)

- A taxi driver/kidnapper drugged, imprisoned, and ransomed Alastair
- Alastair's brother Aaron became the negotiator, coached remotely by Voss from Quantico
- Aaron peppered the kidnapper with calibrated "How" questions to absorb threats and wear him down
- "When we run out of money, what will happen?" — the kidnapper paused and said "It will be all right" — an inadvertent promise not to harm Alastair
- Over time, Aaron turned the kidnapper into almost a friend through the protracted calibrated conversations
- The kidnapper's wife was found by police; he was arrested; Alastair was freed

#### The [[seven-thirty-eight-fifty-five-rule]] (Mehrabian's Rule)

- **7% of a message** is based on words
- **38%** comes from tone of voice
- **55%** comes from body language and face
- Pay close attention to whether tone and body language match the literal meaning of the words
- When there's incongruence, use labels to discover the source: "I heard you say 'Yes,' but it seemed like there was hesitation in your voice"

#### The [[rule-of-three]]

- Three kinds of "Yes": Commitment, Confirmation, and Counterfeit
- The Rule of Three: get the other guy to agree to the same thing three times in the same conversation
- It triples the strength of whatever dynamic you're drilling into and uncovers problems before they happen
- **Vary your tactics**: First agreement is the commitment. Second might be a label or summary that gets "That's right." Third could be a calibrated "How" or "What" question about implementation.

#### The Pinocchio Effect

- **Malhotra et al. (Harvard)**: Liars use more words, more third-person pronouns, and more complex sentences
- Like Pinocchio's nose, the number of words grows with the lie
- Liars work harder at being believable — too hard

#### Pay Attention to Pronouns

- Heavy use of "I," "me," "my" = less important; the real power to decide lies elsewhere
- Heavy use of "we," "they," "them" = more likely dealing directly with a savvy decision maker keeping options open
- The Alastair kidnapper used "we," "they," "them" rigorously early on — Voss was convinced they were engaged with a leader. It was literally true.

#### The Chris Discount

- Use your own name to humanize yourself — it creates "forced empathy" and makes the other side see you as a real person
- "My name is Chris. What's the Chris discount?" — a playful introduction that makes the interaction enjoyable
- Voss used this to get 10% off shirts at a store and to defuse a potential bar fight with a Vietnam veteran in Kansas

### How to Get Your Counterparts to Bid Against Themselves

Four ways to say "No" using "How" questions before actually saying the word:

1. "How am I supposed to do that?" (deferential request for help)
2. "Your offer is very generous. I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me." (no counteroffer, empathic)
3. "I'm sorry but I'm afraid I just can't do that." (slightly more direct)
4. "I'm sorry, no." (barely sounds negative if delivered gently)

#### The Joaquin and Bruno Case Study

Jesus Bueno helped his brother Joaquin buy out business partner Bruno's shares in a Spanish cannabis grow shop:
- Used empathy label about Bruno's wife's pressure, then mirrors to keep him talking
- Discovered the bank had given Bruno an ultimatum about a loan — a critical Black Swan
- Decided on a target of EUR 23,000 with EUR 11,000 up front
- Bruno's hired advisor demanded EUR 30,812 (nonround, specific figures — the advisor was a pro)
- Joaquin and Jesus crafted a series of gentle "No" messages (no counteroffers, just empathic refusals)
- The advisor dropped from EUR 30,812 to EUR 28,346 to EUR 25,000
- Joaquin's final offer: EUR 23,567 with EUR 15,321.37 up front — brilliantly specific numbers
- The advisor accepted within one hour
