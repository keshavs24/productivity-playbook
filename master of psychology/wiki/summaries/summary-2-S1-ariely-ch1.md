# Predictably Irrational Ch.1: The Truth about Relativity — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ariely|Dan Ariely]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. Humans rarely evaluate things in absolute terms -- we always compare relative to available alternatives
2. The **[[decoy-effect]]** (asymmetric dominance) is a powerful manipulation: adding an inferior option shifts preference toward the option it resembles
3. Relativity makes us miserable through social comparison -- jealousy and envy spring from comparing our lot to others'
4. We can break the cycle of relativity by consciously narrowing our comparison circles

## Detailed Notes

### The Economist Subscription Experiment
Ariely noticed The Economist offering three subscription options:
1. Internet-only for $59
2. Print-only for $125
3. Print-and-Internet for $125

The print-only option (same price as the combo) appeared to be a decoy. When Ariely tested this with 100 MIT Sloan MBA students:
- **With all three options:** 16 chose Internet-only, 0 chose print-only, 84 chose print-and-Internet
- **With the decoy removed (only options 1 and 3):** 68 chose Internet-only, 32 chose print-and-Internet

The mere presence of the dominated option dramatically shifted preferences -- from 16% to 68% choosing Internet-only when the decoy was removed. This is not rational: the removed option was one nobody selected, yet its presence changed the outcome entirely.

### The Decoy Effect Explained
- People don't know what they want until they see it in context
- We lack an internal "value meter" -- instead we focus on the **relative advantage** of one thing over another
- The decoy (-A) creates an easy comparison with option A, making A look better not just relative to -A but also relative to the harder-to-compare option B
- When A is better on attribute 1 and B is better on attribute 2, adding -A (clearly worse than A but similar) makes people choose A

### Real-World Decoy Applications
- **Television sales:** Sam the salesman displays a 36-inch for $690, 42-inch for $850, and 50-inch for $1,480. Most people pick the middle option -- the one Sam wants to sell
- **Restaurant menus:** Gregg Rapp (restaurant consultant) learned that high-priced entrees boost revenue even if nobody buys them, because customers order the second most expensive dish
- **Bread makers:** Williams-Sonoma's $275 bread maker sold poorly until they introduced a larger, more expensive model. The original then flew off shelves as the "better deal"
- **Dating:** Ariely advises bringing a slightly less attractive version of yourself (-you) to singles events to exploit the decoy effect

### The Dating/Attractiveness Experiment
Ariely took photos of 60 MIT students and had 25 undergraduates pair them by attractiveness. He selected 6 pairs, then used Photoshop to create slightly less attractive "decoy" versions (-A, -B) of each person. He distributed 600 sheets to students, each containing:
- A regular photo (A), its decoy (-A), and another regular photo (B); OR
- A regular photo (B), its decoy (-B), and another regular photo (A)

**Result:** 75% of the time, participants chose the "regular" person whose decoy was present. The decoy systematically shifted dating preferences.

### Relativity and Misery: Social Comparison
- The Ten Commandments warns against coveting -- "the toughest commandment to follow" given our wiring for comparison
- **CEO compensation spiral:** In 1976, average CEO pay was 36x average worker. After 1993 regulations forced pay disclosure, the media ranked CEOs by pay, executives started comparing and demanding more. By now: 369x average worker
- **The investment banker anecdote:** A young man making $300K was miserable because colleagues made $310K
- **Salary and happiness:** The link between salary and happiness is weaker than expected; much dissatisfaction comes from sheer envy
- **The physician story:** A Harvard MD left cancer research for Wall Street to match his colleagues' earnings, then felt "poor" relative to the new comparison set

### Breaking the Cycle of Relativity
- Control the "circles" around us -- move toward smaller comparison sets that boost relative happiness
- Shift from narrow to broad: Tversky and Kahneman's pen/suit experiment shows that people will drive 15 minutes to save $7 on a $25 pen but not on a $455 suit, even though $7 is $7 regardless of context
- **James Hong** (Hotornot.com co-founder) sold his Porsche Boxster for a Toyota Prius to shrink his comparison circle
