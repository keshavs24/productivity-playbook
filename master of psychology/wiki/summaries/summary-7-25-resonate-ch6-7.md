# Resonate Ch.6-7: Structure & Memorable Delivery — Summary

**Author:** Nancy Duarte
**Source:** *Resonate* (2010), Chapters 6 and 7
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Structure is greater than the sum of its parts.** Solid structure strengthens thinking and helps the audience follow the thought process. Pull the presentation out of the linear slide-making environment and look at it spatially and holistically. Use organizational structures that create natural contrast: chronological, sequential, spatial, climactic (story-like), problem-solution, compare-contrast, cause-effect, advantage-disadvantage.
2. **Order messages for impact** — the same information arranged differently produces entirely different emotional outcomes. Duarte demonstrates with the same Q3 update data arranged in a demotivating order vs. a motivating order where each point builds on the previous one toward a crescendo.
3. **Create three types of contrast in every presentation:** content contrast (what is vs. what could be), emotional contrast (alternating analytical and emotional content — "beats" borrowed from cinema), and delivery contrast (varying stage use, style, visuals, interaction, content type, and involvement between traditional and nontraditional methods).
4. **Create a S.T.A.R. moment** (Something They'll Always Remember) in each presentation. Five types: Memorable Dramatization (demos, props, skits), Repeatable Sound Bites (catchy phrases), Evocative Visuals (powerful images), Emotive Storytelling (stories that package information memorably), and Shocking Statistics (surprising numbers drawn out rather than glossed over). Famous examples: Feynman's O-ring in ice water, Gates releasing mosquitoes at TED, Jobs pulling MacBook Air from an envelope.

## Detailed Notes

### Ch.6: Establish Structure
Presentations that follow rabbit trails lead nowhere and leave the audience lost. Structure is the foundation of a coherent presentation. Move out of the linear slide-creation environment to see the bigger picture. Clustering content helps you visually assess how much weight you have given to various portions.

### Make Sense
Use a logic tree and outline to visualize structure holistically. Benefits: creates a snapshot of the whole structure, ensures one clear big idea bolstered by supporting topics, filters tangential subtopics, helps reviewers give thoughtful feedback.

Eight organizational structures: Chronological, Sequential, Spatial, Climactic (story-like with natural story form), Problem-Solution, Compare-Contrast, Cause-Effect, Advantage-Disadvantage.

### Case Study: Richard Feynman
Feynman's lectures at Caltech appealed to both physics majors and non-physics majors. His communication style earned him the title "The Great Explainer." Analytical devices: Signal (organizational signals to help students understand structure), Itemize (breaking sections into chunks), Visualize (35mm slides, chalkboard, dramatic gestures). Emotional devices: Wonderment (childlike curiosity, poetic phrases), Humor (self-deprecating, interjected in almost every increment). He communicated from both his head and his heart in each lecture.

### Order Messages for Impact
Structure can be used to drive a desired outcome. The same data presented in different order changes the emotional impact entirely. Duarte demonstrates with a Q3 update: a "demotivating structure" that buries good news in bad, vs. a "motivating structure" where each point builds contextually to a crescendo of confidence and new product launch.

### Create Emotional Contrast
Moving between analytical and emotional content is another form of contrast. Presentation Content Types — Analytical: diagram, feature, data, evidence, example, case study, specimen, system, process, facts, supporting documentation. Emotional: biographical/fictitious stories, benefits, analogies/metaphors/anecdotes/parables, props or dramatization, suspenseful reveals, shocking or scary statements, evocative images, invitations to marvel, humor, surprises, offers/deals. In movies, alternating emotion is called "beats" — the smallest structural element.

### Contrast the Delivery
Varying delivery methods between traditional and nontraditional creates contrast. Categories: Stage (be the main event vs. share it), Style (serious vs. humor/enthusiasm), Visuals (read slides vs. minimize them), Interaction (minimize disruptions vs. plan them), Content (familiarity with features vs. wonderment and awe), Involvement (one-way delivery vs. polling, shout-outs, singing, drawing).

### Process Recap
The complete presentation creation process: (1) Generate ideas, (2) Filter down to the best supporting the big idea, (3) Cluster ideas by topic, (4) Turn topics into charged messages as sentences, (5) Place messages in impactful order, (6) Add supporting points as slides, (7) Strengthen turning points, (8) Verify contrast (content, emotional, delivery), (9) Visualize messages — turn words into pictures.

### Putting Your Story on the Silver Screen
Before opening presentation software: one idea per slide, keep it simple (sketch on paper first), turn words into pictures by identifying visual relationships (flow, structure, cluster, radiate, influence).

### Ch.7: Create a S.T.A.R. Moment
Create a moment where you dramatically drive the big idea home by intentionally placing Something They'll Always Remember — a [[star-moment]] — in each presentation. This moment should be so profound or dramatic that it becomes what the audience chats about at the watercooler or appears as a headline. Five types:
1. **Memorable Dramatization** — demos, props, reenactments, skits
2. **Repeatable Sound Bites** — succinct, clear, catchy phrases that feed press headlines and social media rally cries
3. **Evocative Visuals** — powerful full-screen images that leave a vivid imprint
4. **Emotive Storytelling** — stories that package information memorably and link to the big idea
5. **Shocking Statistics** — numbers that are surprising and drawn out rather than glossed over

### Repeatable Sound Bites
Rhetorical devices for creating memorable sound bites: repeat words at the beginning of a series (anaphora — "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times..."), repeat words in the middle (mesodiplosis — "We are troubled on every side, yet not distressed; we are perplexed, but not in despair..."), repeat words at the end (epistrophe — "...government of the people, by the people, for the people").

### Case Studies
- **Michael Pollan** (Memorable Dramatization): At Pop!Tech, placed a paper bag on stage, then later pulled out a burger and eight ounce glasses of oil to show that 26 oz of crude oil goes into one double cheeseburger.
- **Pastor John Ortberg** (Emotive Storytelling): Wove the master story of his sister's rag doll Pandy throughout his sermon about how people want to be loved in spite of their ragged condition, referencing it repeatedly like glue.
- **Steve Jobs** (all five types): 2007 iPhone launch used repeatable sound bites ("reinvent the phone" five times), evocative visuals (iPod with rotary dial joke), memorable dramatization (47 varied tasks in his demo), shocking statistics ("five million songs a day"), and emotive storytelling (personal Wozniak anecdote when the clicker broke). His sparkline showed the bulk of the presentation centered on *what could be*.

### Resonance Rule #7
"Memorable moments are repeated and retransmitted so they cover longer distances."
