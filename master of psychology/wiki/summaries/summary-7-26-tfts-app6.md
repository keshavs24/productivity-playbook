# Think Faster Talk Smarter App.6: The Secrets to Saying We're Sorry — Summary

**Author:** Matt Abrahams
**Source:** *Think Faster, Talk Smarter* (2023), Application #6
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Use the AAA framework for apologies:** Acknowledge the action and accept responsibility, Appreciate (understand) how your offense impacted others, Amends -- detail how you will make it right.
2. **Apologizing is not weakness -- it's courage and strength.** It shows care for relationships and willingness to set aside ego.
3. **Don't pre-apologize, don't over-apologize, and don't delay.** Pre-apologies backfire (if you knew it was a problem, why didn't you fix it?). Over-apologizing inflates the offense. Delayed apologies allow resentment to fester.
4. **Be specific, clear, and brief.** Own the specific action, show you understand the specific impact, commit to specific corrective action.

## Detailed Notes

### The AAA Framework
- **Acknowledge:** Clearly describe the action you took or failed to take. Accept full responsibility. No justifying, minimizing, or explaining away. No "I'm sorry *if* what I said offended you" -- that's a non-apology.
- **Appreciate:** Show you understand the full impact -- emotional, practical, social -- on the person you wronged. Don't minimize ("it's not a big deal"). Demonstrate you truly "get" why it was harmful.
- **Amends:** Specify what you'll do differently going forward. Vague promises to "do better" carry no weight. Lay out clear, concrete steps. This invites the other person to hold you accountable.

### Tips
1. **Don't pre-apologize** -- "I apologize in advance if I stumble over my words" signals you knew about the problem but didn't fix it
2. **Don't wait too long** -- apologize at the first appropriate moment to prevent resentment from festering
3. **Be specific, clear, and brief** -- one well-structured apology is enough; over-apologizing can inflate the offense and annoy the recipient

### Case Study: The Bank Example
While teaching, Abrahams used a banking example about revenue. A student's house had just been foreclosed by that same bank. The student visibly shut down. After class, Abrahams used the AAA framework: acknowledged mentioning the bank (not "I'm sorry if..."), appreciated that it could have brought up painful emotions, and promised to be more careful about examples in the future. The student quickly forgave him, and by the next class was engaged and enthusiastic again.
