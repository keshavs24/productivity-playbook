# Think Faster Talk Smarter Ch.5: Structure — Structure Your Spontaneity — Summary

**Author:** Matt Abrahams
**Source:** *Think Faster, Talk Smarter* (2023), Chapter 5
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Structure doesn't impede spontaneous communication -- it enables it.** Like jazz musicians improvising over chord progressions, having a structural framework frees cognitive resources for content and creativity.
2. **A structure is a narrative that logically connects ideas with one another, organized into a beginning, a middle, and an end.** A list is not a structure -- it lacks the logical connective tissue.
3. **Structure provides four benefits:** keeps audience attention, enhances memory/"stickiness" (episodic memory), makes information easier to process (processing fluency), and makes it easier for the *speaker* to organize their thoughts.
4. **[[what-so-what-now-what]] is the "Swiss Army knife" of structures** -- versatile, simple, and applicable to nearly any spontaneous speaking situation.

## Detailed Notes

### Structure Is Not a List
A list is a mere collection of bullet points. A structure is a *narrative or story that logically connects ideas*, with a beginning, middle, and end. Sue Stanley (Toastmasters): "Whether it's impromptu or not, you have to know where you're going to start and where you're going to end."

### Why Structure Works: Four Benefits

**1. Keeps Audience Attention:**
- Structures create logical transitions that draw people through content. Historian David Labaree: "There's something propelling a story... a line of interest that draws you in."
- Without bridges between ideas, audiences drift to phones, friends, or sleep.

**2. Enhances Memory ("Stickiness"):**
- Stories lodge in memory via [[episodic-memory]]. Neuroscientist David Eagleman: "Stories are crafted to plug into what matters to the brain."
- Students giving in-class presentations: 63% of students recalled stories afterward, only 5% recalled data points.
- Jennifer Aaker: "Those who tell the best stories will become the best leaders" -- because stories activate both rational and emotional brain systems.
- Raymond Nasr (Google exec): entrepreneurs should structure personal histories as narratives with tension and resolution, because "repeatability" means VCs can retell the story to their partners.

**3. Makes Information Easier to Process:**
- Processing fluency research: structured information gets encoded more easily. Neurologist Josef Parvizi: storytelling "is like driving a Porsche rather than taking a bike" for brain processing.
- Myka Carroll (For Dummies editorial director): clear format aids "way finding" -- readers/listeners orient themselves within a learning journey.

**4. Makes It Easier for the Speaker:**
- Structure solves the "how to say it" problem, freeing mental energy for "what to say."
- Having a road map reduces anxiety ("we know we're all set") and opens space for creative excursions without fear of getting lost.
- James Whittington (improv instructor) compares it to highway driving: "Take a stroll around the small towns... but remember to get back on the highway."

### Classic All-Purpose Structures

| Structure | Formula | Best For |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| **[[what-so-what-now-what]]** | What (topic) --> So What (why it matters) --> Now What (action) | General use, Swiss Army knife |
| **PREP** | Point --> Reason --> Example --> Point | Making arguments |
| **[[problem-solution-benefit]]** | Problem --> Solution --> Benefit | Pitching, persuading |
| **Comparison-Contrast-Conclusion** | Similarities --> Differences --> Conclusion | Analysis, evaluation |
| **STAR** | Situation --> Task --> Action --> Result | Interview answers, case studies |

### Sarah Zeitler's Story
Sarah had to deliver a colleague's presentation with no preparation -- just unfamiliar slides. She used [[what-so-what-now-what]] to structure an impromptu presentation about a product launch. Senior leaders were impressed; the absent colleague also received accolades. The structure kept Sarah focused and confident.

### Practicing Structure
- Practice applying structure by generating impromptu speeches using Toastmasters' online tools or random question generators
- Keep a reflection journal: analyze which structures worked and when
- Seek feedback from trusted people: "What can I do to be better?"
- Master 2-3 structures for your common situations rather than trying to learn them all
- Anticipate situations: like presidential debate prep, think about likely scenarios and have key messages/stories ready (Karen Dunn: "you can predict what topics are going to be covered")
- Raymond Nasr counsels clients to "stockpile stories" -- a catalog of anecdotes ready for deployment
