# Ch.6 Cognitive Functioning — Summary

**Author(s):** Albert Bandura
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Self-efficacy beliefs contribute independently to intellectual performance beyond actual cognitive ability.** Collins (1982) found that children of equal ability who judged themselves high vs. low in mathematical efficacy differed markedly: high-efficacy children discarded faulty strategies faster, solved more problems, chose to rework failures, and performed more accurately (Fig. 6.1).
2. **Three domains of efficacy beliefs govern academic development:** students' beliefs in their efficacy to master academic subjects, teachers' beliefs in their efficacy to motivate and promote learning, and faculties' collective sense of efficacy that their schools can accomplish significant academic progress.
3. **Proximal subgoals are far more effective than distal goals** in building self-efficacy and cognitive competencies. Children with proximal subgoals made rapid progress and developed strong mathematical efficacy; distal goals had no demonstrable effects (Bandura & Schunk, 1981).
4. **Self-efficacy cultivates intrinsic interest** through affective self-reactive mechanisms. Moderate perceived efficacy of threshold strength predicted high intrinsic interest, not supreme self-assurance that renders tasks unchallenging.
5. **Teachers' sense of instructional efficacy partly determines how they structure classrooms and influences student achievement trajectories.** High-efficacy teachers create mastery experiences, devote more time to academics, and view difficult students as reachable.
6. **Collective school efficacy predicts school-level achievement** after controlling for SES, student body composition, and prior achievement (Bandura, 1993).

## Detailed Notes

### 1. Students' Cognitive Self-Efficacy (pp.214-240)

**Efficacy beliefs contribute independently to intellectual performance (pp.214-216):**
- Collins (1982): At every ability level, high-efficacy children were quicker to discard faulty strategies, solved more problems, reworked more failures, did so more accurately (Fig. 6.1)
- Bouffard-Bouchard, Parent, & Larivee (1991): Regardless of cognitive ability level, high-efficacy children solved more conceptual problems, managed work time better, were more persistent
- Path analyses (Schunk, 1984a, Fig. 6.2): Perceived efficacy exerts more substantial impact on academic performance than acquired skill alone, both directly and through quality of thinking and persistence
- The unique contribution of efficacy beliefs to academic performance is highly replicable (Hackett, 1985; Pajares & Kranzler, 1995; Pajares & Miller, 1994a; Randhawa, Beamer, & Lundberg, 1993)

**Development of cognitive self-efficacy through aspiration (pp.217-218):**
- Proximal subgoals provide immediate incentives and feedback of progress; distal goals too remote
- Children with proximal subgoals made rapid progress, achieved substantial mastery, developed strong mathematical efficacy (Bandura & Schunk, 1981). Distal goals had no demonstrable effects
- Self-set goals produce higher expectations, stronger efficacy, higher performance than prescribed goals (Schunk, 1985)
- Learning goals (mastering strategies) more effective than performance goals for developing efficacy and proficiency (Schunk, 1996)
- Schunk & Rice (1989): Remedial reading students achieve higher efficacy and performance under goals emphasizing comprehension attainments or strategy use

**Cultivating intrinsic interest through self-efficacy (pp.218-222):**
- Intrinsic interest grows through affective self-reactive mechanisms: people display enduring interest in activities at which they feel efficacious and derive self-satisfaction
- Personal standards enhance interest by enlisting sustained involvement, fostering competence development, providing markers for verifying growth
- Temporal lag pattern: mastery experiences promote self-satisfactions conducive to growth of interest; increased interest emerges later as consequence of enhanced self-efficacy
- Threshold notion: at least moderate perceived efficacy required to generate interest, but increases above threshold do not produce further gains. Supreme self-assurance may render activities unchallenging (Bandura & Schunk, 1981)
- Positive incentives promote interest when they enhance or authenticate personal efficacy; interest declines when rewarded irrespective of performance quality
- Rewards tied to levels of competence enhance interest; indiscriminate rewards have no effect or reduce interest (Arnold, 1976; Calder & Staw, 1975)
- According to Deci and Ryan (1985), rewards diminish interest when controlling but increase it when conveying competency information. Bandura notes this distinction is easy to draw but actual incentive systems are difficult to classify cleanly

**Self-efficacy in metacognitive skills (pp.223-227):**
- Metacognition refers to cognitive appraisal and control of one's cognitive activity
- Verbal self-guidance enhances competence: children who verbalize cognitive strategies exhibit greater increases in perceived self-efficacy and higher proficiency (Schunk, 1982b, 1984, 1985)
- Improvisational self-guidance conveys more generalized sense of personal causation than externally prescribed self-instruction (Schunk, 1982b)
- Efficacy beliefs mediate the influence of task-related strategies on learning and cognitive functioning (Wood & Bandura, 1989a)
- Strategy instruction alone is insufficient; cognitive aspects cannot be divorced from motivational and self-reactive aspects
- Transferability requires repeated experiences in diverse tasks and settings; abstract rules taught with everyday examples produce greater use than abstract instruction or concrete examples alone (Nisbett, 1993; Rosenthal & Zimmerman, 1978)

**Self-regulated cognitive development (pp.227-233):**
- Zimmerman (1989, 1990): Expanded model of academic self-regulation integrating cognitive, metacognitive, and motivational mechanisms
- High achievers use all self-regulative strategies more than low achievers (Zimmerman & Martinez-Pons, 1986, 1988)
- Self-regulative strategies account for variation in achievement in language and math even controlling for gender and SES
- Self-regulatory efficacy -- ability to mobilize, direct, and sustain learning activities -- plays a pivotal but neglected role
- Path analysis (Fig. 6.3, Zimmerman, Bandura, & Martinez-Pons, 1984a): Self-efficacy for self-regulated learning (.21) and self-efficacy for academic achievement (.21) both contribute to grades, mediated through academic goals (.43)
- Parents' grade goals affect achievement only indirectly through children's self-influence
- Writing proficiency path analysis (Fig. 6.4, Zimmerman & Bandura, 1994): Writing instruction affects writing proficiency through self-regulatory efficacy (.19), academic efficacy (.36), self-evaluative standards (.44), and writing goals (.40)
- Effects of gender and writing aptitude largely mediated by efficacy beliefs

**Peer influences (pp.234-235):**
- Peers potent force in development and social validation of intellectual self-efficacy
- Coping peer models boost efficacy more than mastery models (Schunk & Hanson, 1985, 1989b)
- Model similarity increases perceived competence; large disparities lead children to view skills as beyond reach
- Social comparative information can influence performance through mediating processes: effort, perseverance, cognitive efficiency, choice, stress

**Self-efficacy and academic anxiety (pp.235-237):**
- Low mathematical efficacy accompanied by high math anxiety concurrently and longitudinally (Betz & Hackett, 1983; Krampen, 1988)
- Impact of past successes and failures on anxiety mediated entirely through efficacy beliefs (Meece, Wigfield, & Eccles, 1990)
- When perceived self-efficacy removed, anxiety-performance relation disappears or markedly diminishes (Pajares & Johnson, 1994; Pajares & Valiente, in press)
- Multifaceted treatment: situational, cognitive, affective, skill aspects (Smith, 1980, 1989)

**Developmental trajectories (pp.237-239):**
- Path analysis (Fig. 6.5, Bandura et al., 1996a): SES affects achievement only indirectly through parental aspirations and children's prosocialness
- Different facets of perceived self-efficacy contribute through different mediational paths
- Academic inefficacies foreshadow antisocial behavior adoption (Hinshaw, 1992; Rutter, 1979)
- Perceived social efficacy contributes to academic attainments by promoting aspirations and reducing vulnerability to futility and depression

**Advanced cognitive functioning (pp.239-240):**
- Creativity requires unshakable sense of efficacy to persist through prolonged uncertain investment
- Increased perceived efficacy promotes creative thinking both directly and through motivating personal challenges (Locke, Frederick, Lee, & Bobko, 1984)
- Research productivity: high investigatory efficacy affects scholarly productivity directly and through goal setting and multiple project involvement (Taylor et al., 1984)

### 2. Teachers' Perceived Efficacy (pp.240-243)

- Gibson & Dembo (1984): Teachers' instructional efficacy partly determines classroom structure and students' evaluations of their intellectual capabilities
- High-efficacy teachers: devote more time to academics, provide guidance to struggling students, praise accomplishments
- Low-efficacy teachers: more time on nonacademic pastimes, give up on students quickly, criticize failures
- Low-efficacy orientation: custodial view, strict regulations, extrinsic inducements, negative sanctions (Woolfolk, Rosoff, & Hoy, 1990)
- Melby (1995): Low-efficacy teachers distrust classroom management ability, stressed by misbehavior, pessimistic about students' improvability, would not choose teaching again
- Perceived inefficacy linked to burnout through stress and anger (Chwalisz, Altmaier, & Russell, 1992)
- Instructional efficacy was best predictor of teaching commitment; strong principal leadership also contributed
- Ashton & Webb (1986): Students learned much more from high-efficacy teachers over academic year
- Anderson, Greene, & Loewen (1988): Teacher efficacy stronger predictor for younger than older students
- Teacher efficacy should be measured domain-specifically (math vs. language instruction), not globally
- Multifaceted teacher efficacy scales (Bandura, 1990b) enable domain-specific research

### 3. Collective School Efficacy (pp.243-252)

**Attributes of efficacious schools (pp.244-246):**
- Strong academic leadership by principal who builds teachers' instructional efficacy (Coladarci, 1992)
- High expectations and standards pervade efficacious school environments
- Mastery model of learning: close monitoring, corrective feedback, reinstruction until mastery
- No tracking into homogeneous ability groups
- Behavior managed by promoting productive activities rather than punishing disruption
- Heavy involvement of parents as partners in education
- Parental efficacy determines participation level (Hoover-Dempsey, Bassler, & Brissie, 1987, 1992)

**Collective instructional efficacy (pp.247-251):**
- Perceived collective efficacy represents overriding quality affecting different aspects of social system
- Varies across grade level (Fig. 6.6, Bandura, 1993): low at entry, rises in middle grades, declines in later grades as academic demands increase
- Staff beliefs decline in later grades especially significant for student transitions (Midgley et al., 1989)
- Bandura (1993): Study of 79 elementary schools -- perceived collective efficacy measured as summed beliefs of teachers in their school's capability. Path analysis shows student body characteristics influence achievement largely by altering staff's collective efficacy beliefs. Collective efficacy contributes independently after controlling for student bodies, teacher characteristics, and prior achievement
- Schools with strong collective efficacy flourish academically regardless of student body composition
- Negative reciprocal causation: declining staff efficacy and declining student efficacy and achievement reinforce each other; positive bidirectional influences produce mutual enhancement

**Models for disadvantaged youth (pp.251-252):**
- Compensatory education programs have had limited success; gains small compared to widening gaps
- Educationally disadvantaged students need accelerated rather than remedial education (Levin, 1987)
- Comer's (1980, 1988) collaborative program for school development: enlisting teachers, administrators, parents, community to adopt shared responsibilities
- Remedial tracking undermines academic strivings by fostering affiliation with low-achieving, antipathetic peers (Dornbusch, 1994)

## Contradictions or Tensions
- Bandura argues self-efficacy mediates anxiety-performance relationship almost entirely, contradicting views treating anxiety as independent causal factor
- Threshold model of intrinsic interest (moderate efficacy optimal) challenges assumption that higher efficacy is always better
- Bandura critiques [[attribution-theory]]-based interventions that retrain attributions without building skills

## Quotes Worth Keeping
> "Perceived self-efficacy, therefore, is a better predictor of intellectual performance than skills alone." -- Bandura, 1997, p.216

> "In the pursuit of excellence, the higher the students' efficacy beliefs, the higher the academic challenges they set for themselves and the greater their intrinsic interest in scholastic matters." -- Bandura, 1997, p.220

> "Staff's collective sense of efficacy that they can promote high levels of academic progress contributes significantly to their schools' level of academic achievement." -- Bandura, 1997, p.250
