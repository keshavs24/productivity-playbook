# Resonate Ch.4: Define the Journey — Summary

**Author:** Nancy Duarte
**Source:** *Resonate* (2010), Chapter 4
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Every presentation should have a destination.** Every bit of content should propel the audience toward that destination. Like a sailboat tacking against the wind, the presentation moves back and forth (what is / what could be) to reach a specific point. Audience resistance can be harnessed as forward momentum.
2. **The Big Idea is the one key message** — the controlling idea, thesis statement, or single unifying message. It has three components: (1) your unique point of view on a topic, (2) a clear statement of what is at stake for those who do or do not adopt your perspective, and (3) it must be a complete sentence. A big idea plays on the two fundamental emotions — pleasure and pain — raising or lowering the likelihood of each depending on whether the audience accepts or rejects the idea. Connects to [[commanders-intent]].
3. **Plan the audience journey as a "move from / move to" transformation.** Identify both the inward change (manner of being) and outward change (manner of doing). Like a screenplay's character arc, the first and last pages should reveal maximum transformation.
4. **Acknowledge resistance and make the reward worth it.** Change requires sacrifice — the audience must abandon what they previously held true. Address their comfort zone, fears, vulnerabilities, misunderstandings, obstacles, and politics. Rewards should appeal to basic needs, security, savings, prize, recognition, relationship, or destiny.

## Detailed Notes

### The Big Idea
A [[big-idea]] is the one key message you want to communicate — the impetus that compels the audience to set a new course. Screenwriters call this the "controlling idea." Three components:
- **Your unique point of view** on a topic (not a generic topic like "the fate of the oceans" but a pointed view like "worldwide pollution is killing the ocean and us")
- **A clear statement of what is at stake** for those who do or do not adopt your perspective
- **It must be a complete sentence** with a noun and a verb

Emotion is a critical component. Ultimately, there are only two emotions — pleasure and pain. A truly persuasive presentation plays on these: raise the likelihood of pain if they reject the big idea, raise the likelihood of pleasure if they accept it. John P. Kotter (HBR) explained that most successful change efforts begin when individuals or groups look hard at a company's competitive situation, technological trends, and financial performance, then find ways to communicate this information broadly and dramatically with respect to crises or great opportunities.

### Plan the Audience's Journey
To plan an audience journey, identify where you want to move them from and to. Identify both:
- **Inward change** (manner of being) — how they think and feel
- **Outward change** (manner of doing) — what they do differently

This is the character arc. A story analyst determines the quality of a screenplay simply by looking at the first and last pages — how much did the hero change? The same applies to presentations.

### Tools for Mapping a Journey
Duarte provides extensive lists of "move from / move to" word pairs (e.g., Abstain to Try, Chaos to Structure, Doubt to Believe, Skeptical to Hopeful). Process segmentation helps determine where the audience is stuck: by project process, sales cycle, or adoption process (innovators, early adopters, majority, laggards).

### Acknowledge the Risk
People have an innate sense of fear when embarking on a journey with an unknown outcome. Change involves the addition of the new and the abandonment of the old. To adopt your perspective, the audience must abandon what they previously held true. Sacrifice is the surrender of something prized for a higher claim. Be cognizant of the sacrifice: What would they sacrifice? What beliefs or ideals will be let go? How much will it cost them in time or money? What is the perceived risk? Connects to [[loss-aversion]].

### Address Resistance — Refusal of the Call
Most people do not enjoy change and will resist. State opposing points as inoculants before they get a chance to refute your point. An inoculation purposefully infects a person to minimize the severity of an infection. Similarly, empathetically address refusals by stating them openly. What you perceive as resistance may be viewed completely differently in the audience's minds — they may be protecting things they hold dear. Profile the "Refusal of the Call": Comfort Zone, Fear, Vulnerabilities, Misunderstanding, Obstacles, Politics.

### Make the Reward Worth It
An audience will not act unless you describe a reward that makes it worthwhile. Rewards should appeal to physical, relational, or self-fulfillment needs:
- **Basic needs** (food, water, shelter, rest)
- **Security** (feeling safe at home, at work, at play)
- **Savings** (time and money)
- **Prize** (personal financial reward, market share)
- **Recognition** (being honored for efforts)
- **Relationship** (community with others who make a difference)
- **Destiny** (living up to their full potential)

The reward should be proportional to the sacrifice they have made.

### Case Study: General Electric — Beth Comstock
Comstock's presentations demonstrate the "move from / move to" framework brilliantly. For each theme (Foster Creativity, Navigate Ambiguity, Take Risks, Develop New World Skills, Empower Teams, Unleash Your Passion), she structured content as: Move to Being, Move to Doing, Benefit/Outcome, Personalize (with personal stories of risk, frailty, and victories).

### Resonance Rule #4
"Every audience will persist in a state of rest unless compelled to change."
