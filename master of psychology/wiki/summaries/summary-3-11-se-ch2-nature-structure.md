# Ch.2 The Nature and Structure of Self-Efficacy — Summary

**Author(s):** Albert Bandura
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Self-efficacy is a generative capability, not a fixed trait or a repertoire of behaviors** -- it is concerned not with the number of skills you have, but with what you believe you can do with whatever skills you have under a variety of circumstances.
2. **Self-efficacy beliefs vary along three dimensions: level (magnitude), generality, and strength** -- these dimensions have distinct performance implications and measurement requirements.
3. **Efficacy beliefs are active producers of performance, not passive foretellers** -- they causally contribute to motivation, thought processes, affective states, and the performances that are realized, demonstrated through diverse experimental paradigms.
4. **An optimistic sense of personal efficacy is adaptive, not a cognitive flaw** -- human accomplishments and positive well-being require an optimistic sense of efficacy, and the costs of overconfidence are generally outweighed by the benefits of resilient self-belief.
5. **Sources of discordance between efficacy judgment and action are systematic and identifiable** -- they include limited assessment scope, mismatch between efficacy and performance domains, ambiguity of task demands, faulty self-knowledge, disincentives, temporal disparities, and statistical overcontrol.

## Detailed Notes

### Perceived Self-Efficacy as a Generative Capability (pp.36-38)

Effective personal functioning is not simply a matter of knowing what to do and being motivated to do it. Nor is efficacy a fixed ability one does or does not have. Rather, [[self-efficacy]] is a [[generative-capability]] in which cognitive, social, emotional, and behavioral subskills must be organized and effectively orchestrated to serve innumerable purposes.

There is a marked difference between possessing subskills and being able to integrate them into appropriate courses of action under difficult circumstances. People often fail to perform optimally even though they know full well what to do and possess the requisite skills (Schwartz & Gottman, 1976). Self-referent thought activates cognitive, motivational, and affective processes that govern the translation of knowledge and abilities into proficient action.

Perceived self-efficacy is concerned not with the number of skills one has but a belief about what one can do under different sets of conditions with whatever skills one possesses. Some writers have misconstrued personal efficacy as judgments of motor acts in a "behavioral repertoire" or as a decontextualized quantity of perceived ability. For example, perceived efficacy to protect oneself against sexually transmitted diseases is not reducible to the isolated act of donning a condom -- it involves perceived self-regulatory efficacy to surmount the numerous interpersonal impediments to condom use.

In measuring driving efficacy, people are not asked to judge component subskills (turning ignition, shifting gears) but the strength of their perceived efficacy to navigate an automobile adequately under traffic conditions that present different levels of challenge. The generative capability of maneuvering an automobile under narrow margins of error is not captured by a summation of decontextualized efficacy for subskills.

### Active Producers versus Passive Foretellers of Performances (pp.38-39)

Self-efficacy beliefs are not simply inert predictors of future performance. Strong adherents of behavioristic doctrines regard thoughts as merely residues of conditioned responses that cannot influence human motivation or action. In such an agentless view, self-beliefs are mere foretellers of future behavior in some unspecified way.

There is a world of difference between doing and undergoing. People who doubt their capabilities in particular activity domains shy away from difficult tasks, find it hard to motivate themselves, slacken their efforts or give up quickly in the face of obstacles, have low aspirations and weak commitment, dwell on personal deficiencies, and are prone to diagnose insufficient performance as deficient aptitude. They fall easy victim to stress and depression.

In contrast, a resilient sense of efficacy enhances sociocognitive functioning in many ways. People who have strong beliefs in their capabilities approach difficult tasks as challenges to be mastered rather than threats to be avoided. They set challenging goals and maintain strong commitment to them. They invest high effort and heighten effort in the face of failures. They remain task-focused and think strategically. They attribute failure to insufficient effort, which supports a success orientation. They quickly recover their sense of efficacy after failures or setbacks.

### The Self-Efficacy Approach to Personal Causation (pp.39-42)

Efforts to discover how personal determinants contribute to psychosocial functioning have generally relied on omnibus tests of personal attributes designed to serve diverse purposes. The items in these scales are decontextualized: people are asked to judge their aggressiveness in a situational void without reference to the form of aggression, the protagonists, the social setting, and circumstances.

It is unrealistic to expect personality measures cast in generalities to shed much light on personal factors operating in different task domains and diverse circumstances. Self-efficacy theory prescribes particularized assessment: efficacy beliefs should be measured in terms of particularized judgments of capability that may vary across realms of activity, under different levels of task demands, and under different situational circumstances.

A common misconception is that global scales requiring people to judge their general capabilities measure an efficacy disposition or trait. Such a presumption is highly disputable. Global measures of personal efficacy bear little or no relation either to efficacy beliefs related to particular activity domains or to behavior (Earley & Lituchy, 1991; Eden & Zuk, 1995; McAuley & Gill, 1983; Pond & Hay, 1989).

It is sometimes assumed that a personality disposition of perceived control spawns efficacy beliefs. Multivariate analyses indicate that so-called dispositional measures, such as perceived control and optimism, derive their predictiveness largely from their redundancy with efficacy beliefs (Cozzarelli, 1993; Dzewaltowski, Noble, & Shaw, 1990). When the influence of efficacy belief is controlled, the relation of perceived control and optimism to psychological functioning essentially disappears.

### Multidimensionality of Self-Efficacy Belief Systems (pp.42-50)

Self-efficacy theory prescribes measurement along the lines already suggested. Efficacy beliefs should be measured in terms of particularized judgments of capability that may vary across realms of activity, under different levels of task demands within a given activity domain, and under different situational circumstances. Personal efficacy is not a contextless global disposition -- it is a multifaceted phenomenon.

#### Structure of Self-Efficacy Scales (pp.42-44)

[[self-efficacy-dimensions]]: Efficacy beliefs vary on several dimensions with important performance implications:

1. **Level (magnitude):** The perceived personal efficacy of different individuals may be limited to simple task demands, extend to moderately difficult demands, or include the most taxing demands within a particular domain of functioning. The range of perceived capability is measured against levels of task demands that represent varying degrees of challenge or impediment to successful performance.

2. **Strength:** Weak efficacy beliefs are easily negated by disconfirming experiences, whereas people with tenacious belief in their capabilities will persevere despite innumerable difficulties and obstacles. Strength of perceived self-efficacy is not necessarily linearly related to choice behavior (Bandura, 1977) -- a certain threshold of self-assurance is needed to attempt a course of action, but higher strengths produce greater perseverance and higher likelihood of success.

3. **Generality:** People may judge themselves efficacious across a wide range of activities or only in certain domains. Generality can vary on number of dimensions: the degree of similarity of activities, the modalities in which capabilities are expressed (behavioral, cognitive, affective), qualitative features of situations, and the characteristics of persons toward whom behavior is directed.

In the standard methodology, individuals are presented with items portraying different levels of task demands, and they rate the strength of their belief in their ability to execute the requisite activities. Items are phrased in terms of *can do* rather than *will do* -- self-efficacy is a judgment of capability, not intention. Individuals record the strength of their belief on a 100-point scale, ranging from 0 ("Cannot do") through intermediate degrees of assurance, 50 ("Moderately certain can do"), to 100 ("Certain can do"). Scales are unipolar, ranging from 0 to maximum strength.

Preliminary instructions establish the appropriate judgmental set. People are asked to judge their operative capabilities as of now, not their potential or expected future capabilities. In the case of self-regulatory efficacy, people judge their assurance that they can perform the activity regularly over designated periods of time.

#### Effects of Self-Assessment (pp.46-47)

Self-efficacy judgments are recorded privately without personal identification to reduce social evaluative concerns. People make multiple judgments of their efficacy across the full range of task demands rather than making each judgment immediately before each performance. The nonreactivity of self-efficacy assessment is corroborated for diverse activities. Contrary to consistency theory, high social demands reduced rather than increased congruence between efficacy judgment and behavior -- people became more conservative in their efficacy appraisals under social surveillance.

#### Omnibus versus Domain-Linked Measures (pp.47-49)

The evidence is relatively consistent in showing that perceived self-efficacy is a good predictor, whereas locus of control is either a weak predictor or is nonpredictive. Efficacy beliefs predict academic performance, anxiety, pain tolerance, metabolic control in diabetes, and political participation, whereas locus of control does not (Grossman, Brink, & Hauser, 1987; Manning & Wright, 1983; McCarthy, Meier, & Rinderer, 1985; Smith, 1989; Taylor & Popma, 1990; Wollman & Stouder, 1991). More circumscribed but still general locus of control measures do not fare much better.

#### Discriminative Generalization of Self-Appraisal (pp.50-53)

A multidimensional approach does not mean there is no structure or generality to efficacy beliefs. Extreme specificity and indiscriminate transfer would both be maladaptive. Adaptive functioning requires discriminative generalization of perceived efficacy.

At least five processes produce some generality in personal efficacy:
1. **Similar subskills:** When different classes of activities are governed by similar subskills, mastery in one area generalizes
2. **Codevelopment:** When competencies are socially structured to develop together, generality occurs even across dissimilar domains
3. **Self-regulatory skills:** Widely applicable metastrategies learned in one realm tend to be used in other activity domains
4. **Construing commonalities:** Generality is achieved by structuring commonalities cognitively across diverse activities
5. **Transformational restructuring of efficacy beliefs:** Powerful mastery experiences can produce a generalized boost of personal efficacy -- the belief that one can mobilize whatever effort it takes to succeed in different undertakings

Bandura's own investigations into the causal contribution of efficacy beliefs to human adaptation began as an unintended outgrowth of phobia treatment research. Severe snake phobics who were permanently freed of phobic behavior through mastery-based treatment showed generalized improvements in other realms completely unrelated to the treated dysfunction -- reduced social timidity, boosted self-expressiveness, increased venturesome self-testing.

### Self-Efficacy Causality (pp.54-60)

A central question in any theory of cognitive regulation of motivation and action is the issue of causality. Do efficacy beliefs operate as causal factors in human functioning?

#### Dual Causal Linkage (pp.54-55)

The most stringent test of a theory provides evidence of dual linkage in the causal process -- external influences are linked to changes in an independently measured indicant of the internal mediator, and it, in turn, is linked to behavior. Further tests can determine whether the effect of external influence on action is mediated completely or partially through efficacy belief.

The efficacy-action link can be corroborated by verifying microlevel relations between particular efficacy beliefs and corresponding actions (percentage of congruence between self-efficacy judgment and actual performance). Cervone (1985) provides a randomization test for evaluating the level of significance of obtained microlevel congruences.

#### Diverse Tests of Causality (pp.56-60)

Studies of the causal contribution of efficacy beliefs test each of the postulated links in the causal process using diverse experimental strategies:

1. **Vicarious efficacy induction:** Bandura, Reese, and Adams (1982) raised self-efficacy from virtually nonexistent to either low or moderately high levels solely by modeling. Phobics observed coping strategies being modeled but performed no actions themselves. Higher levels of perceived self-efficacy were accompanied by higher performance attainments. Microanalysis of efficacy-action congruences revealed a close fit on individual tasks.

2. **Arbitrary anchoring influences:** Cervone and Peake (1986) used arbitrary reference numbers to influence self-appraisals of efficacy. Judgments of personal efficacy made from an arbitrary high starting point raised perceived efficacy, whereas an arbitrary low starting point lowered it. The higher the instilled efficacy beliefs, the longer individuals persevered on difficult and unsolvable problems before they quit. Mediational analyses reveal that neither anchoring influences nor cognitive focus had any effect on motivation when perceived self-efficacy was controlled.

3. **Bogus feedback and social comparison:** Weinberg, Gould, and Jackson (1979) showed that physical stamina in competitive situations is mediated by perceived self-efficacy. Illusorily raised efficacy beliefs of physical strength heightened stamina, whereas illusorily lowered beliefs weakened it, obliterating large preexisting sex differences. Litt (1988) demonstrated similar effects for pain tolerance using bogus normative comparison.

4. **Contravening experimental design:** Holroyd et al. (1984) demonstrated that the benefits of biofeedback training for tension headaches may stem more from enhancement of perceived coping efficacy than from the muscular exercises themselves. Bogus feedback that one was exercising good control instilled strong efficacy that prevented headaches regardless of actual muscular changes.

5. **Multivariate panel designs:** Studies in which efficacy beliefs, other determinants, and performance attainments are measured on two or more occasions. The temporal ordering and systematic variation of efficacy beliefs help remove ambiguities about causality. Efficacy beliefs usually make substantial independent contribution to variations in motivation and performance attainments.

### Sources of Discordance between Efficacy Judgment and Action (pp.61-78)

Efficacy beliefs serve functional purposes -- acting on sound appraisals increases the prospect of success. But a number of factors can create discordance between efficacy belief and action.

#### Limited Scope of Self-Efficacy Assessment (pp.61-62)
Self-efficacy theory is often tested on only a partial set of contributing factors. If the factor targeted is a relatively small contributor to the given performance, perceived efficacy cannot emerge as a strong predictor because the factor carries limited weight in the causal structure.

#### Mismatch between Self-Efficacy and the Performance Domains (pp.62-63)
When capabilities measured in perceived efficacy differ significantly from those governing performance, one would not expect efficacy beliefs and performance to be highly related. Pajares and Miller (1994b) found efficacy beliefs were stronger predictors under good matches between efficacy assessment and performance domains than under partial matches.

#### Faulty Assessments of Self-Efficacy or Performance (pp.63-64)
Microanalytic measures tailored to the domain of functioning are best. Trivial measures will yield misinformative results. Performance must also be adequately assessed -- errors of measurement place an upper limit on correlations.

#### Ambiguity of Task Demands (pp.64-65)
If one does not know what demands must be fulfilled, one cannot accurately judge whether one has the requisite abilities. Even the same activity may tap different capabilities under different circumstances.

#### Temporal Disparities (pp.67-68)
The elapsed time between assessments of efficacy and performance is an important factor. Proximal measures of perceived efficacy are more predictive than prior ones. Efficacy beliefs that are firmly established remain strong regardless of whether taxing activities are far off or imminent. In contrast, weakly held efficacy beliefs are highly vulnerable to change as performance draws near.

#### Consequences of Misjudgment (p.68)
The seriousness of missteps can influence accuracy. Situations with no consequences provide little incentive for serious appraisal. When things matter, accurate self-appraisals serve as valuable guides for action.

#### Disincentives and Performance Constraints (p.68)
People may possess skills and strong efficacy but choose not to perform because they have no incentive. Also, physical or social constraints can prevent people from performing to the level of their efficacy beliefs.

#### Causal Ordering of Determinants (pp.68-69)
Self-efficacy will be shown to have a larger effect if its contribution is assessed causally prior to goals in multiple regression analyses than if the analytic order is inverted.

#### Statistical Overcontrol (p.69)
Using past performance as a proxy for ability confounds ability and nonability factors. Past performance is itself affected by beliefs of personal efficacy. Controlling for unadjusted past performance scores is controlling not only for ability but also for the prior impact of efficacy beliefs.

#### Faulty Self-Knowledge (p.70)
Self-appraisals can be inflated by selective recall of personal successes and deflated by selective remembrance of failures. The cognitive processing of efficacy information is addressed in Ch.3.

### Veridicality of Self-Appraisal: Self-Aiding or Self-Limiting? (pp.70-72)

When people err in their self-appraisals, they generally overestimate their capabilities. In nonhazardous activities, optimistic efficacy appraisals are a benefit, whereas veridical judgments can be self-limiting. If efficacy beliefs always reflected only what people can do routinely, they would remain steadfastly wedded to overly conservative judgments that would beget only habitual performances.

The difficult nature of human realities makes optimistic self-efficacy an adaptive judgmental bias rather than a cognitive failing to be eradicated. A growing body of evidence reveals that human accomplishments and positive well-being require an optimistic sense of personal efficacy (Bandura, 1986a). Life is full of difficulties. Self-doubts can set in fast after failures. The important thing is not that difficulties arouse self-doubt but the speed of recovery of perceived efficacy after encounters with difficulty.

### Affective and Motivational Benefits of Optimistic Self-Efficacy Belief (pp.72-74)

Innovations demand heavy investments of time, effort, and resources. The benefits are often realized gradually through a lengthy process. Many innovators never see the fruits of their labor during their lifetime. Bandura provides vivid examples: Saroyan accumulated a thousand rejections, Joyce's Dubliners was rejected by 22 publishers, Gertrude Stein submitted poems for 20 years, the Impressionists had to arrange their own exhibitions, a Paris art dealer refused Picasso, Stravinsky was run out of town, Decca Records rejected the Beatles, Disney was rejected by Anaheim, Bell Telephone was dismissed as "an electrical toy."

The moral: rejections should not be accepted too readily as indications of personal failings. It takes a resilient sense of efficacy to override repeated early rejections.

Depressed people usually display realistic self-appraisals of their social competencies, whereas nondepressed people show self-enhancing biases (Lewinsohn, Mischel, Chaplin, & Barton, 1980). In laboratory tasks where actions exert no control over outcomes, the depressed are realistic in judging they lack control, while nondepressed people believe they are exercising control (Alloy, Clements, & Koenig, 1993). The depressed appear as realists, the nondepressed as confident distortionists.

The distinction between resolute strivers and wishful dreamers is important. Resolute strivers believe passionately and are willing to expend extraordinary effort. Wishful optimists lack the efficacy strength and commitment to go through the uncertainties, disappointments, and drudgery.

#### Differential Function of Preparatory and Performance Efficacy (pp.75-76)
Self-efficacy theory distinguishes between the effects of strength of efficacy beliefs during the development of skills and during the use of established skills. In the skill development phase, people who perceive themselves to be highly efficacious have little incentive to invest much preparatory effort -- some self-doubt about one's efficacy provides the impetus to acquire the needed knowledge and skills. But in applying skills already developed, a strong belief in one's efficacy is essential to mobilize and sustain effort. In short, self-doubt creates the impetus for acquiring knowledge and skills, but hinders proficient use of developed skills.

#### Self-Affirmation versus Self-Deception (pp.77-78)
Illusory judgments, which involve false notions, should be distinguished from strong commitment to accomplishments that have a low probability of realization. Transfer of positive efficacy beliefs to new endeavors is more adaptive than carryover of self-limiting beliefs. People may be said to act on illusions only when their self-beliefs remain adamantly unresponsive to massive disconfirming evidence.

Self-deception as simultaneously believing and knowing something is false is logically impossible (Bok, 1980; Champlin, 1977; Haight, 1980). Bandura (1986a) argues the split-self solution fails. Different levels of awareness are sometimes proposed, but the paradox remains. In practice, people may misconstrue their performances, filter efficacy information through biases, or judge their efficacy with deficient knowledge, but being misdirected by one's biases or ignorance does not mean that one is lying to oneself.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Optimistic efficacy vs. [[overconfidence-in-calibration]]:** Bandura argues that optimistic self-efficacy beliefs are generally adaptive, not a bias to be corrected. This stands in tension with the Phase 2 calibration literature that treats overconfidence as a systematic error. Bandura would say that in nonhazardous activities, the benefits of optimistic self-appraisal (greater persistence, effort, resilience) outweigh the costs, and that "realistic" appraisal is often self-limiting. The resolution may be domain-dependent: overconfidence is costly when margins of error are narrow (surgery, piloting), but adaptive when persistence through failure is essential (entrepreneurship, innovation, skill acquisition).
- **Self-efficacy and [[deliberate-practice]]:** Self-efficacy theory provides a motivational account for why deliberate practice works and when it fails. A resilient sense of efficacy enables people to endure the toilsome self-disciplined effort that expertise requires. But Bandura notes a paradox: some self-doubt during the preparatory phase motivates deeper preparation, whereas strong efficacy during performance enables proficient execution. This maps to the distinction between preparatory and performance efficacy.
- **Self-efficacy and [[illusion-of-control]]:** Bandura explicitly argues that Langer's (1975, 1979) research on illusory control is compatible with self-efficacy theory. Trivial situational factors (like those Langer identified) can alter efficacy beliefs, and these altered beliefs then causally regulate performance. What Langer calls "illusory" beliefs about control operate functionally as efficacy beliefs. Bandura's position is that the illusory/realistic distinction is less important than the functional impact: do the beliefs enable or disable action?
