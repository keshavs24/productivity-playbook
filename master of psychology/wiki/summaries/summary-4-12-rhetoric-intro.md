# On Rhetoric: Kennedy's Introduction — Summary

**Author(s):** Aristotle (translated by George A. Kennedy)
**Type:** Book introduction
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Rhetoric is morally neutral.** Aristotle was perhaps the first to recognize clearly that rhetoric as an art of communication could be used for either good or ill. Persuasion depends on three things: the truth and logical validity of what is being argued ([[logos]]), the speaker's success in conveying trustworthy character ([[ethos]]), and the emotions awakened in the audience ([[pathos]]).
2. **Aristotle classified rhetoric as a mixed art** — partly a method or tool (like dialectic) with no special subject of its own, and partly a practical art derived from ethics and politics. It is an ability to "see" the available means of persuasion in each case.
3. **The treatise identifies three (and only three) species of rhetoric** — [[three-genres-of-rhetoric|deliberative]] (future-oriented, concerned with the advantageous), [[three-genres-of-rhetoric|judicial/forensic]] (past-oriented, concerned with justice), and [[three-genres-of-rhetoric|epideictic]] (present-oriented, concerned with praise and blame).
4. **The enthymeme is the "body" of persuasion** — Aristotle's most original contribution to rhetorical theory. The [[enthymeme]] is a rhetorical syllogism drawn from probable premises, often with one premise left unstated because the audience supplies it.
5. **On Rhetoric is the earliest systematic account of human psychology.** Book 2 provides a comprehensive catalog of emotions ([[rhetorical-emotions]]) — their definitions, causes, and the states of mind in which people feel them — making it foundational for both rhetoric and psychology.

## Detailed Notes

### A. Aristotle's Life and Works (pp. 1-7)

- Born 384 B.C.E. in Stagiros, northern Greece; father was physician to the king of Macedon
- Studied at Plato's Academy for twenty years (367-347 B.C.E.); developed early interest in rhetoric alongside dialectic
- Began teaching rhetoric at the Academy in the mid-350s, partly to counter the influence of the rival school of Isocrates
- Tutored Alexander the Great around 343-342 B.C.E.
- Returned to Athens in 335 and opened the Lyceum ("Peripatetic" school)
- The text of *On Rhetoric* was composed over multiple periods — the "early core" (Book 1, chs. 5-15; most of Book 3) dates from the 350s; later revisions occurred in the 330s
- The work was an esoteric text (lecture notes, not published), preserved in Aristotle's personal library

### B. Rhetoric Before Aristotle (pp. 7-15)

- Rhetoric in the broadest sense is a feature of all human communication, even animal communication
- The Greeks connected rhetorical development to democracy — public deliberation required persuasive speech
- Early rhetorical handbooks (beginning with Tisias/Corax, c. 460 B.C.E.) focused narrowly on judicial speaking
- The sophists (Protagoras, Gorgias, Hippias) taught verbal skills for pay, using epideixis (demonstration speeches)
- Isocrates (436-338 B.C.E.) was the most influential rhetoric teacher, emphasizing stylistic amplification over logical argument — a contrast to Aristotle's emphasis on logical argument and truth
- Plato's complex relationship with rhetoric: attacked it in *Gorgias* as flattery, then proposed a philosophical ideal of rhetoric in *Phaedrus* — Aristotle adapted this philosophical vision to realistic situations

### C. Aristotle's Classification of Rhetoric (pp. 16-17)

Aristotle divided intellectual activity into:
1. **Theoretical sciences** — mathematics, physics, theology (knowledge for its own sake)
2. **Practical arts** — politics, ethics (goal: doing something)
3. **Productive arts** — architecture, crafts, poetry (goal: making something)
4. **Methods/tools (organa)** — logic, dialectic (applicable to all subjects)

Rhetoric is a **mixture**: partly a method (like dialectic) applicable to any subject, and partly a practical art drawing from ethics and politics. In defining rhetoric (1.2.1), Aristotle says it is "an ability of seeing the available means of persuasion" — a theoretical verb (*theorein*), emphasizing observation and understanding rather than just production.

### D. Aristotle's Original Audience and His Audience Today (pp. 17-20)

- The original audience was students at Aristotle's school (335-323 B.C.E.) who had studied dialectic
- Modern audiences fall into four groups: classical philologists (textual scholars), philosophers (interested in the philosophical content), speech/communication scholars (interested in argumentation and the [[enthymeme]]), and literary scholars (interested in metaphor and style in Book 3)

### E. Strengths and Limitations of On Rhetoric (pp. 20-23)

**Strengths:**
- Clear recognition that rhetoric is a universal technique applicable to any subject
- Identification of the [[three-modes-of-persuasion]] (ethos, pathos, logos)
- Distinction of three species/genres of rhetoric (deliberative, judicial, epideictic)
- Classification of artistic vs. non-artistic proofs
- Understanding that rhetoric deals with probabilities, not certainties
- Recognition that enthymemes draw material from politics and ethics but formal structure from [[topoi]]

**Limitations:**
- Theory of [[ethos]] is limited to what is conveyed in the speech itself — ignores the speaker's pre-existing reputation and authority (contrast with [[authority-principle]])
- Failure to illustrate how political and ethical topics could be used in actual speeches
- Description of emotions (Book 2) is not connected to rhetorical practice
- Narrow concept of epideictic rhetoric
- Over-emphasis on the [[enthymeme]] as key to logical persuasion

### F. Chapter-by-Chapter Outline of On Rhetoric (pp. 23-25)

**Book 1:** Introduction, definition, divisions of rhetoric; specific topics for deliberative, epideictic, and judicial rhetoric
- Chs. 1-3: Introduction; definition of rhetoric; three species
- Chs. 4-8: Topics for deliberative rhetoric (political topics, ethical topics, the good, constitutions)
- Ch. 9: Topics for epideictic rhetoric; amplification
- Chs. 10-15: Topics for judicial rhetoric (wrongdoing, pleasure, justice, non-artistic proofs)

**Book 2:** Character, emotions, and logical argument
- Ch. 1: Introduction; character and emotions as means of persuasion
- Chs. 2-11: The emotions (anger/calmness, friendliness/enmity, fear/confidence, shame/shamelessness, kindliness, pity/indignation, envy/emulation)
- Chs. 12-17: Character types (young, old, prime of life, wellborn, wealthy, powerful)
- Chs. 18-26: Logical argument (common topics, paradigm, maxim, enthymeme, 28 topoi, fallacies, refutation)

**Book 3:** Delivery, style, and arrangement

## Contradictions or Tensions

- Aristotle limits [[ethos]] to what is conveyed by the speech itself, explicitly excluding prior reputation. This directly contrasts with [[authority-principle]] (Cialdini), where pre-existing authority symbols (titles, uniforms, trappings) are powerful persuaders regardless of speech content.
- Chapter 1 of Book 1 seems to reject emotional appeals as improper, while Chapter 2 rehabilitates them as one of three legitimate modes of persuasion — Kennedy notes this as an apparent inconsistency resulting from different composition periods.

## Quotes Worth Keeping
