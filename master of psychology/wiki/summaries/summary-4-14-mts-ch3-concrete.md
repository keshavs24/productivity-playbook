# Made to Stick: Chapter 3 (Concrete) — Summary

**Author(s):** Chip Heath and Dan Heath
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Concreteness is the easiest SUCCESs principle to apply.** The barrier to concreteness is not difficulty but *forgetfulness* — we slip into abstract language without realizing it. Experts are particularly prone to this because abstraction is the hallmark of expertise (the [[curse-of-knowledge]] at work). See [[concreteness-principle]], [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]].
2. **Concrete ideas cling to memory via the Velcro theory.** Memory is not a single filing cabinet but more like Velcro — thousands of tiny hooks and loops. The more sensory hooks an idea has (visual, auditory, emotional, kinesthetic), the more loops it snags and the stickier it becomes. Abstract ideas have one hook; your childhood home has a gazillion.
3. **Concreteness serves three functions: understanding, memory, and coordination.** Concrete language helps novices understand new concepts (Asian math teachers using tiles and stick figures), makes ideas more memorable (Aesop's fables surviving 2,500 years through concrete images, not abstract morals), and enables groups to coordinate (Boeing's "seat 131 passengers, fly nonstop from Miami to NYC, land on Runway 4-22 at LaGuardia").
4. **The brown eyes/blue eyes experiment is the ultimate concrete lesson.** Jane Elliott's simulation of racial prejudice with third-graders — dividing the class by eye color and rotating privilege — put so many hooks into memory (the collars, the sneering friends, the academic performance changes) that students still remembered it vividly twenty years later.
5. **A concrete prop can transform communication.** James Grant (UNICEF director) carried a packet of salt and sugar — the ingredients for Oral Rehydration Therapy — and would show it to prime ministers: "Do you know that this costs less than a cup of tea and it can save hundreds of thousands of children's lives in your country?" The prop invited expertise to bear on the problem.

## Detailed Notes

### A. Sour Grapes: Concreteness in Aesop (pp. 111-113)

- Aesop's "The Fox and the Grapes" has survived 2,500+ years across dozens of cultures — the phrase "sour grapes" appears in Hungarian (*savanyu a szolo*), Chinese, Swedish, and many other languages
- The fable endures because of its concrete encoding: the grapes, the fox, the dismissive comment — not because of its abstract moral ("Don't be a bitter jerk when you fail")
- Business buzzwords are the opposite: "reciprocal cost-based reengineering," "customer-oriented visionary paradigm" — a satirical "Business Buzzword Generator" combines one word from each of three columns
- "Language is often abstract, but *life* is not abstract" — life is specific people doing specific things. Even the most abstract strategy eventually shows up in tangible human actions.

### B. The Nature Conservancy: Landscapes as Eco-Celebrities (pp. 113-116)

- TNC's traditional "bucks and acres" strategy was beautifully concrete — a big gift bought a big piece of land you could walk on
- In California, 40% of the state was environmentally sensitive (too much to buy), and 9% was in "critical danger"
- Students at Stanford tried to make the abstract goal concrete by subdividing ("2 percent chunk per year" or "acreage per year") but the numbers were still too big
- TNC's solution: talk about "landscapes" — contiguous plots with unique ecological features. They named a set of brown hills east of Silicon Valley "the Mount Hamilton Wilderness"
- The name and identity made the area tangible — other organizations began referencing it in their own campaigns. "We're always laughing now because we see other people's documents and they're talking about the Mount Hamilton Wilderness. We say, 'You know we made that up.'"
- TNC realized that even as their solutions grew more ambiguous, their *messages* could not be allowed to grow more ambiguous

### C. Understanding Subtraction: Asian vs. American Math Teaching (pp. 117-119)

- "Concrete" = something you can examine with your senses. A V8 engine is concrete. "High performance" is abstract.
- Concrete language especially helps novices. "Abstraction is the luxury of the expert."
- Researchers studied ten schools each in Japan, Taiwan, and the U.S. — all used rote recall, but other techniques varied dramatically
- Asian teachers used "Computing in Context" — concrete, familiar scenarios: "You had 100 yen but bought a notebook for 70 yen" or "3 kids playing ball, 2 more came, then 1 more joined"
- A Japanese teacher placed 5 rows of 10 tiles, removed 3 rows of 10, and asked how many remained — students built the abstract concept of subtraction on a *concrete* foundation (30 tiles being yanked away from 50)
- "Conceptual Knowledge" questions appeared in 37% of lessons in Japan, 20% in Taiwan, but only 2% in the United States
- Contrary to stereotypes, Asian schools use concrete examples *more* than American schools, not less

### D. Concrete Is Memorable (pp. 120-121)

- Experiments show people are better at remembering concrete, easily visualized nouns ("bicycle," "avocado") than abstract ones ("justice," "personality")
- The Kidney Heist legend would be far less sticky if the man "woke up and found that someone had absconded with his self-esteem"
- Yale researcher Eric Havelock notes that the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are characterized by lots of concrete actions with few abstractions — as stories were passed orally through generations, concrete details survived while abstractions evaporated

### E. Soap-Opera Accounting (pp. 120-122)

- Carol Springer and Faye Borthick (Georgia State University) taught introductory accounting through a semester-long case study following two fictional college sophomores, Kris and Sandy, launching a startup called "Safe Night Out" (SNO)
- Students became Kris and Sandy's accountant — tracking GPS receiver costs, eBay commissions, and the difference between fixed and variable costs. The distinction between profitability and cash flow emerged when Kris and Sandy called in a panic: they'd been selling more than ever but had no cash (bounced check)
- Students who worked through the concrete case study scored noticeably higher on the first exam of the *next* accounting course — two years later — especially C-average students, who scored twelve points higher
- "Concreteness sticks."

### F. The Velcro Theory of Memory (pp. 122-124)

- David Rubin (Duke University) exercise: try to remember (1) the capital of Kansas, (2) the first line of "Hey Jude," (3) the Mona Lisa, (4) your childhood home, (5) the definition of "truth," (6) the definition of "watermelon"
- Each triggers a different mental activity — the capital of Kansas is an abstract retrieval exercise; the childhood home floods you with sensory memories (smells, sounds, sights); "truth" requires constructing a definition on the fly; "watermelon" evokes the striped rind, the sweet smell, the heft
- Memory is not a single filing cabinet — it's like Velcro. One side has tiny hooks, the other tiny loops. The more hooks an idea has, the more loops it snags. Your childhood home has a gazillion hooks. A new credit card number has one, if it's lucky.
- Jane Elliott's simulation put *many* hooks into a single idea (prejudice): visual (the collars), social (the sneering friends), kinesthetic (the feel of a collar around your neck), emotional (despair, anger), cognitive (the phonics card performance)

### G. Brown Eyes, Blue Eyes (pp. 124-126)

- April 4, 1968: Martin Luther King Jr. is assassinated. The next day, Jane Elliott, an elementary teacher in the all-white town of Riceville, Iowa, decides to make prejudice *tangible* for her third-graders
- She divided the class by eye color: brown-eyed children were superior; blue-eyed children were forced to sit at the back, wore special collars, were told they were less smart, and were given less recess time
- The transformation was immediate and shocking: "I watched those kids turn into nasty, vicious, discriminating third-graders... it was ghastly." Brown-eyed kids taunted former friends.
- Next day, Elliott reversed it — brown-eyed children were now inferior. Blue-eyed children ran gleefully to collar their brown-eyed peers.
- Academic performance changed: phonics card reading took 5.5 minutes when a group was on the bottom; 2.5 minutes when on top. "We had those collars on... We couldn't stop thinking about those collars."
- Studies conducted ten and twenty years later showed Elliott's students were significantly less prejudiced than peers who hadn't experienced the exercise
- Elliott turned prejudice from abstract knowledge ("the capital of Kansas") into an *experience* ("your childhood home") — so many hooks that it could not be forgotten

### H. The Path to Abstraction: Engineers vs. Manufacturers (pp. 127-129)

- Researcher Beth Bechky studied a manufacturing firm making silicon-chip machinery — engineers (abstract thinkers) and manufacturing workers (concrete thinkers) struggled to communicate
- When a part didn't fit, manufacturing brought the physical part to the engineers. The engineers instinctively reached for their *drawings* — making them more abstract, more elaborate, which further hampered communication
- "The engineers were behaving like American tourists who travel to foreign countries and try to make themselves understood by speaking English more slowly and loudly"
- The solution was for engineers to go to the factory floor — *everyone* understood the physical machine fluently. "The moral of the story is to find a 'universal language,' one that everyone speaks fluently. Inevitably, that universal language will be concrete."

### I. Concrete Allows Coordination (pp. 130-131)

- The "pocketable radio" and "man on the moon" were both pleasingly concrete — no ambiguity about "man," "moon," or "decade"
- Boeing's 727 goal: "seat 131 passengers, fly nonstop from Miami to New York City, and land on Runway 4-22 at LaGuardia" (a runway less than a mile long — too short for any existing passenger jet). This concrete goal coordinated thousands of engineering experts across disciplines.
- Imagine how much harder it would have been if the goal was "the best passenger plane in the world" — too abstract for alignment

### J. The Ferraris Go to Disney World (pp. 131-133)

- Stone Yamashita Partners (consulting firm, San Francisco) created a 6,000-square-foot exhibit for HP's pitch to Disney — a fictional family called the Ferraris visiting Disney World, with HP technology helping them buy tickets, enter the park, schedule reservations, and automatically download roller coaster photos
- Instead of a PowerPoint about e-services, the exhibit was a living, breathing simulation — concrete and sensory
- HP engineers, initially skeptical, were transformed: "It became very viral... 'Did you see that great thing that the labs team did?'"
- Concreteness helped a diverse group of experts — engineers contemplating abstract technology problems — coordinate by grappling with one family's concrete needs

### K. White Things Exercise (pp. 134-135)

- Step 1: List as many things that are white in color as you can think of in 15 seconds
- Step 2: List as many white things in your refrigerator as you can think of in 15 seconds
- Most people list about the same number — despite the refrigerator being a tiny subset of the universe
- Concreteness mobilizes and focuses the brain. Similarly: "Think of five silly things people have done in the world in the past ten years" vs. "Think of five silly things your child has done in the past ten years" — the second is far easier

### L. Kaplan and Go Computers (pp. 135-137)

- Jerry Kaplan pitched a "pen-based portable computer" to Kleiner Perkins in 1987 with no slides, no business plan, no prototype — just a maroon leather portfolio
- After describing the concept verbally to a tense room, he tossed the portfolio onto the table: "Gentlemen, here is a model of the next step in the computer revolution."
- The partners reached out to touch it, began brainstorming excitedly about memory, chip sizes, handwriting recognition — "It had been magically transformed from a stationery-store accessory into a symbol of the future of technology"
- Kleiner Perkins invested $4.5 million in the nonexistent company
- The concrete prop changed the audience's attitude from reactive and critical to active and creative — in the same way that "white things in your refrigerator" focuses the brain

### M. Clinic: Oral Rehydration Therapy (pp. 137-140)

- Over a million children die annually from dehydration caused by diarrhea — preventable with Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT), a simple mix of salt, sugar, and water
- Message 1 (PSI): scientific, credible description of dehydration mechanics and ORS — but makes the problem sound complex and unsolvable. "What would you do tomorrow to start saving children's lives?"
- Message 2 (James Grant, UNICEF director): carried a packet of salt and sugar to meetings with prime ministers. "Do you know that this costs less than a cup of tea and it can save hundreds of thousands of children's lives in your country?"
- Grant's concrete prop invited people to brainstorm solutions — getting salt/sugar packets to schools, teaching mothers the recipe. The prop works like Kaplan's portfolio: it transforms the audience from passive receivers to active problem-solvers.
- "You can't see it and *not* start brainstorming about the possibilities."

### N. Making Ideas Concrete: Hamburger Helper and Saddleback Sam (pp. 139-142)

- Melissa Studzinski (General Mills) inherited three huge binders of data about Hamburger Helper customers — "death binders" that were impossible to absorb
- The team sent members into actual customers' homes ("Fingertips" program). Studzinski saw a mother with a toddler on her hip while mixing dinner: "We know that 'convenience' is an important attribute, but it's a different thing to see the need for convenience firsthand."
- Key finding: Hamburger Helper had eleven pasta shapes (kids didn't care) and thirty flavors (moms struggled to find favorites). "Moms saw new flavors as risky." The team simplified the product line, boosting sales 11%.
- Saddleback Church (50,000+ members) created "Saddleback Sam" — a detailed concrete persona of their target audience: a late-30s, college-educated, self-satisfied, unchurched man who is skeptical of organized religion
- The persona guided hundreds of independent ministry leaders — when someone proposed a telemarketing campaign, the leaders knew immediately that Sam hates telemarketers, so the idea was scratched
- "Most of our members would have no trouble describing Sam"

### O. The Power of Concreteness (pp. 141-142)

- "Of the six traits of stickiness, concreteness is perhaps the easiest to embrace. It may also be the most effective."
- Being simple is difficult. Being unexpected requires creativity. Being concrete isn't hard — the barrier is forgetfulness. We forget that we're slipping into abstractspeak.
- "We're the engineers who keep flipping back to our drawings, not noticing that the assemblers just want us to follow them down to the factory floor."

## Contradictions or Tensions

- The Velcro theory of memory connects to dual-coding theory in cognitive psychology (Paivio) — concrete words activate both verbal and visual memory systems, creating more retrieval paths. This enriches the existing wiki page on [[abstract-vs-concrete-information]].
- The engineers-vs-manufacturers story illustrates a specific case of the [[curse-of-knowledge]] — experts instinctively communicate in their own abstract language, not realizing it fails to connect with their audience. This parallels the tapper/listener experiment from the Introduction.
- The brown eyes/blue eyes simulation connects to [[framing-effects]]: identical children were framed as superior or inferior based on a single arbitrary attribute, and behavior changed dramatically. It also connects to [[self-efficacy]] — children in the "superior" group performed better on academic tasks, consistent with Bandura's finding that efficacy expectations shape performance.
