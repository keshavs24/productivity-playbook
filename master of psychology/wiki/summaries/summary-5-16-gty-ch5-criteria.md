# Getting to Yes Ch.5: Insist on Using Objective Criteria — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 5
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Deciding on the basis of will is costly.** When negotiation becomes a contest over who can be the most stubborn, the outcome results from the interaction of two wills — almost as if the negotiators were on a desert island with no history, customs, or moral standards.
2. **The solution is to negotiate on some basis independent of the will of either side — objective criteria.** The more you bring standards of fairness, efficiency, or scientific merit to bear on your particular problem, the more likely you are to produce a final package that is wise and fair.
3. **Principled negotiation produces wise agreements amicably and efficiently.** An agreement consistent with precedent is less vulnerable to attack. A constant battle for dominance threatens a relationship; principled negotiation protects it.
4. **Two types of objective criteria exist: fair standards and fair procedures.** Fair standards include market value, precedent, scientific judgment, professional standards, efficiency, costs, reciprocity, and what a court would decide. Fair procedures include "one cuts, the other chooses," taking turns, drawing lots, and letting a third party decide.

## Detailed Notes

### Deciding on the Basis of Will Is Costly

However well you understand the other side's interests, however ingeniously you invent options for mutual gain, you will almost always face the harsh reality of interests that conflict. No talk of "win-win" strategies can conceal that fact. The question is how to resolve these differences.

Typically, negotiators try to resolve conflicts by positional bargaining — talking about what they are willing and unwilling to accept. The outcome focuses on what each side is willing to agree to. As discussed in Chapter 1, trying to reconcile differences on the basis of will has serious costs: inefficiency, damaged relationships, and unwise agreements.

### The Case for Using Objective Criteria

**Construction contract example.** You entered into a fixed-price contract for a house foundation. The contractor says "I want along with you on steel girders for the roof." Rather than horse-trading, insist on discussing the issue in terms of objective safety standards.

**Principled negotiation produces wise agreements amicably and efficiently.** The more you bring standards of fairness, efficiency, or scientific merit to bear, the more likely a wise and fair result. An agreement consistent with precedent is less vulnerable to attack.

**Independent standards are even more important when more parties are involved.** Coalitions are difficult to build with positions; objective criteria reduce the number of commitments each side must make.

**The MIT model at the Law of the Sea Conference.** India proposed no initial fee for deep-seabed mining; the U.S. rejected this. Then MIT developed an economic model that both parties accepted as objective — it provided a way to evaluate any fee proposal on its merits. The model showed that some initial fee was economically feasible. No one backed down; the parties reached a tentative agreement. The existence of an objective model able to forecast consequences convinced the parties that the tentative agreement was fair.

### Developing Objective Criteria

**Fair standards.** You will usually find more than one objective criterion available as a basis for agreement. For a demolished car:
1. Original cost minus depreciation
2. What the car could have been sold for
3. Standard "blue book" value
4. Cost to replace with a comparable car
5. What a court would award

Other sources: market value, precedent, scientific judgment, professional standards, efficiency, costs, moral standards, equal treatment, tradition, reciprocity.

Objective criteria should be independent of each side's will. Ideally, they should be both independent and legitimate/practical. The test of reciprocal application: would you accept this standard if roles were reversed?

**Fair procedures.** The age-old way to divide a piece of cake between two children: one cuts and the other chooses. "Last-best-offer arbitration" in baseball works similarly — the arbitrator must choose between the last offer made by one side and the last offer made by the other, pressuring both to be reasonable.

Letting someone else play a key role is well-established: submit to a mediator, ask an expert for advice, submit to an arbitrator for binding decision.

### Negotiating with Objective Criteria

Three basic points:
1. **Frame each issue as a joint search for objective criteria.** "Look, you want a high price and I want a low one. Let's figure out what a fair price would be."
2. **Reason and be open to reason** as to which standards are most appropriate and how they should be applied.
3. **Never yield to pressure, only to principle.**

**Ask "What's your theory?"** If the seller starts by giving a position, ask for the theory behind it. "How did you arrive at that figure?" Treat the problem as though the seller too is looking for a fair price based on objective criteria.

**Agree first on principles.** Before even considering possible terms, you may want to agree on the standard or standards to apply.

**Reason and be open to reason.** Come to the table with an open mind. One standard of legitimacy does not preclude the existence of others. You should be willing to respond to reasons for applying another standard or for applying a standard differently.

**Never yield to pressure.** Pressure can take many forms: a bribe, a threat, a manipulative appeal to trust, or a simple refusal to budge. The principled response is the same: invite them to state their reasoning, suggest objective criteria you think apply, and refuse to budge except on this basis. It is easier for you to resist making an arbitrary concession than it will be for them to resist advancing some objective standards.

### "It's Company Policy" — Extended Case Study

Tom's car was destroyed by a dump truck. His insurance company says: "We have studied your case. The policy applies. You're entitled to $3,300."

Tom uses principled negotiation:
- Asks how they reached the figure
- Requests the objective standard they used
- Finds a comparable car advertised for $6,800
- Uses blue book values, mileage adjustments, and radio/air conditioning valuations
- Walks out with a check for **$8,024** — more than double the initial offer

Tom never yielded to pressure ("Take it or leave it"), never threatened, and never dug into a position. He insisted on objective criteria throughout.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The insistence on objective criteria is a debiasing strategy against [[anchoring-and-adjustment]] — by demanding fair standards, you prevent the other side's opening offer from serving as the anchor
- Fair procedures like "one cuts, the other chooses" connect to procedural justice research in [[cognitive-barriers-to-resolution]]
- The "never yield to pressure" principle is a direct counter to many of the compliance tactics in [[six-principles-of-influence]] — particularly reciprocation pressure and commitment/consistency traps
