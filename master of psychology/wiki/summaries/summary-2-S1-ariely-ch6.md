# Predictably Irrational Ch.6: The Influence of Arousal — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ariely|Dan Ariely]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. People in a "cold" (unaroused) state systematically **fail to predict** how they will behave in a "hot" (aroused) emotional state -- the **[[hot-cold-empathy-gap]]**
2. The magnitude of this underprediction is massive: arousal increased predicted attraction to unusual sexual activities by ~72%, immoral behavior propensity by ~136%, and unsafe sex likelihood by ~25%
3. We are not one integrated self but more like an "agglomeration of multiple selves" -- Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde
4. Emotional states transform decision-making so completely that cold-state commitments may be useless during hot states

## Detailed Notes

### The Berkeley Sexual Arousal Experiment (Ariely & George Loewenstein)
25 male Berkeley undergraduates participated in a two-session study:

**Session 1 (Cold state):** Students answered questions while calm and unaroused, predicting what they would find attractive, whether they would engage in immoral acts, and whether they would use condoms -- all hypothetically during arousal.

**Session 2 (Hot state):** Same students answered the same questions while actually sexually aroused (viewing erotic images and masturbating, with a Saran-wrapped laptop).

### Results Across Three Categories
**Sexual preferences (19 questions):** Aroused predictions were nearly **twice as high** (72% higher) as cold-state predictions. Activities considered unappealing when cold became much more appealing when aroused.

**Immoral behavior (5 questions):** Propensity to engage in immoral activities was **more than twice as high** (136% higher) when aroused. For example:
- "Would you keep trying after your date says no?" -- Cold: 20, Aroused: 45 (125% increase)
- "Would you slip a woman a drug?" -- Cold: 5, Aroused: 26 (420% increase)

**Unsafe sex (3 questions):** When aroused, participants were 25% more likely to predict foregoing condoms, despite years of safe-sex education.

### The Jekyll and Hyde Insight
- Even the most brilliant, rational person, in the heat of passion, becomes "absolutely and completely divorced from the person he thought he was"
- It is not just that people make wrong predictions about themselves -- **their predictions are wrong by a large margin**
- Our inability to understand ourselves in a different emotional state does **not improve with experience** -- Berkeley students spent substantial time in aroused states yet still couldn't predict their aroused selves

### Practical Implications
- **Safe sex:** "Just say no" campaigns assume we can turn off passion at will. The research shows this is false. Better strategy: ensure condoms are always available (don't decide in a cold state whether to carry one), and teach strategies for avoiding situations where arousal takes over
- **Safe driving:** A teenager driving alone is 40% more likely to crash than an adult; with one friend, risk doubles; with a third teenager, it doubles again. Cars should have built-in precautionary devices (speed limits, parental alerts) configured during cold states
- **Better life decisions:** A cold-state commitment to refuse painkillers during childbirth may evaporate during actual labor. The gap between predicted and experienced emotion must be bridged through advance planning
- **General principle:** Avoid temptation rather than trying to overcome it. Fighting temptation after it arises is much harder than avoiding it in the first place

### Connection to Chapter 7
The inability to predict our hot-state behavior connects directly to procrastination: we make cool, rational plans but then succumb to hot-state impulses (the dessert cart, the new purchase, the skipped exercise session). Understanding the hot-cold gap is essential for designing commitment devices.
