# Schools as Contexts for Learning and Social Development -- Summary

**Author(s):** Richard M. Ryan, Edward L. Deci
**Type:** Book chapter (Ch. 14)
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Schools are not merely learning factories -- they are **contexts for human development** that shape motivation, self-concept, social skills, and psychological well-being. SDT argues that the primary goal of education should be student flourishing, not just cognitive achievement.
2. [[intrinsic-motivation]] declines steadily across the school years (grades 3-8 and ages 9-16), and this decline is substantially accounted for by school environments that fail to satisfy students' basic psychological needs for [[autonomy-need|autonomy]], [[competence-need|competence]], and relatedness.
3. [[autonomy-support-in-education|Autonomy-supportive teaching]] consistently predicts higher intrinsic motivation, deeper conceptual learning, greater persistence, and better psychological adjustment -- effects replicated across elementary through post-secondary settings and across Western and Eastern cultures.
4. Grading and evaluative pressures generally **undermine** intrinsic motivation and deep learning. The controlling aspects of grading (normative comparison, pressure to perform) outweigh any informational function and produce rote memorization over conceptual understanding.
5. The motives underlying achievement goals matter more than the goals themselves. Mastery goals pursued for autonomous reasons yield the best outcomes; performance goals pursued for controlled reasons yield the worst. The autonomous vs. controlled distinction explains more variance than the mastery vs. performance distinction alone.
6. Teachers become more controlling when pressured from above (accountability mandates, high-stakes testing) or from below (disengaged students), creating a cascading chain of need frustration throughout the school system.
7. [[internalization-in-education|Internalization of academic values]] is facilitated by autonomy support, structure, and relatedness -- the same three conditions that support intrinsic motivation. Students who more fully internalize the value of schoolwork show better engagement, deeper learning, and greater well-being.
8. High-stakes testing (HST) policies have failed to improve achievement while producing a broad range of negative consequences including teaching to the test, narrowed curricula, teacher burnout, increased cheating, and student dropout.
9. Training teachers to be more autonomy-supportive is effective (meta-analytic effect size d=0.63 from Su & Reeve, 2011), and such training leads to measurable improvements in student engagement and learning.
10. The "umbrella effect": individual educators can buffer students from systemic controlling pressures by providing need-supportive micro-environments within their own classrooms.

## Detailed Notes

### Development in the Situation of Modern Schooling

Modern schooling is a relatively new experiment in human learning. Historically, children learned by watching, imitating, playing at, and pretending to be the adults around them. Their "teachers" were adults to whom they were related or closely attached. Modern schools have replaced this with professional adults teaching large groups of children pre-selected materials on prescribed schedules. The internal basis for engaging in school learning is often unclear to students -- curricula are not packaged to be intrinsically motivating or relevant to students' daily lives.

Under top-down policy pressures, schools have become focused on narrow cognitive goals, neglecting students' varied interests and holistic psychological needs. Educators resort to grades, tests, and other external pressures to motivate learning, based on two questionable assumptions: (1) that extrinsic motivators effectively promote learning, and (2) that narrow intellectual goals are the most important products of schooling. SDT challenges both.

### Schools as Contexts for Development

Schools shape the **development of the whole child** -- motivation, self-concept, social relationships, the vitality and integrity of self-development. SDT argues schools should facilitate high-quality motivation, engagement, participation, citizenship, and social-emotional well-being, and should not do **developmental harm** by discouraging, demotivating, or leaving students feeling alienated.

SDT research focuses on **flourishing**: students becoming motivated, vital, resourceful, and fully functioning adults who feel empowered and confident in their learning, feel a sense of belonging to their schools and larger community.

Student motivation is linked to teacher motivation. When teachers lack professional autonomy and are pressured toward specific outcomes, they apply more controlling strategies, harming both students and their own work satisfaction.

### Intrinsic Motivation in the Classroom

[[intrinsic-motivation|Intrinsic motivation]] is a manifestation of natural human propensities to assimilate and integrate knowledge. Characterized by curiosity and interest, it represents an active and willing acquisition of knowledge. Certain classroom climates ignite this fuel for learning; others smother it.

**Decline of intrinsic motivation across school years:**
- **Harter (1981); Lepper, Corpus, & Iyengar (2005):** Students in grades 3-8 show steady year-to-year decline in intrinsic motivation.
- **Gillet, Vallerand, & Lafreniere (2012):** Students in Quebec showed decreased intrinsic motivation from age 9 to 15. Decreasing teacher autonomy support mediated this decline. An increase after age 15 was attributed to increased choice over school subjects.
- **Gnambs & Hanfstingl (2016):** Marked decline in intrinsic motivation between ages 11 and 16 in Austrian schools, substantially accounted for by insufficiently satisfied needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

**Intrinsic motivation is a crucial ingredient for academic achievement:**
- **Taylor et al. (2014):** Meta-analysis of cross-sectional studies plus three longitudinal studies (Canada and Sweden). Across all studies, intrinsic motivation was the only type of motivation consistently positively associated with academic achievement over a 1-year period, controlling for baseline achievement.
- **Froiland & Worrell (2016):** Intrinsic motivation robustly predicted engagement, which predicted higher GPA in an ethnically and racially diverse sample. Effect generalized to African American and Latino students.

**Early SDT classroom research:**
- **Deci, Schwartz, Sheinman, & Ryan (1981):** Teachers assessed on autonomy-supportive vs. controlling orientation before the school year. Students (grades 4-6) assessed at start and again 2 months later. Students of autonomy-supportive teachers had higher intrinsic motivation, perceived competence, and self-esteem.
- **Ryan & Grolnick (1986):** Students' perceptions of teachers' autonomy-supportive vs. controlling style predicted intrinsic motivation, self-worth, and perceived cognitive competence. Children wrote projective stories based on classroom scenes -- those in autonomy-supportive classrooms wrote stories with more positively motivated protagonists and less aggression.

**Lesson-to-lesson effects:**
- **Tsai, Kunter, Ludtke, Trautwein, & Ryan (2008):** German public school students. Perceived teacher autonomy support varied from lesson to lesson and covaried with students' interest above and beyond typical interest levels. Teacher control variations had the opposite effect. Even session-to-session fluctuations in autonomy support matter.

**Intrinsic motivation generalizes beyond the specific activity:**
- **Skinner, Chi, & Learning-Gardens Educational Association (2012):** Uninterested middle-school students in garden-based activities felt more competent, autonomous, and intrinsically motivated. These students achieved better in their regular classroom courses -- the motivation generalized.
- **Ruiz-Gallardo, Verde, & Valdes (2013):** High school students in Spain (high-risk, frequently disruptive, dropping out at high rate) introduced to garden-based learning showed substantial decreases in school failure, dropout, and disruptive behaviors, plus increases in skills and self-confidence.

**Autonomy-supportive volunteers:**
- **Generation 2 program (unpublished; Ryan, Weinstein, & Schultz):** Older adult volunteers had weekly child-centered play sessions with children from the poorest urban schools in Rochester, NY. Longitudinal evidence showed students became more positive in school-related attitudes and enthusiasm compared to controls. Even small doses of autonomy support and relatedness can significantly influence school experience.

### Internalization, Learning, and Well-Being

See [[internalization-in-education]] for full treatment.

Despite the importance of [[intrinsic-motivation]], many school tasks are not inherently interesting. Developing a personal value for schoolwork is crucial for long-term success. Through [[internalization-in-education|internalization and integration]], students can become autonomously motivated to learn material they do not find inherently interesting but in which they find value.

**The Positive Effects of Autonomous Extrinsic Motivation:**
- **Yamauchi & Tanaka (1998):** Japanese elementary students with identified and intrinsic regulation predicted greater school value, deeper approaches to learning, and less work avoidance. External regulation and introjection did not.
- **Ryan & Connell (1989):** U.S. elementary students with more autonomous motivations showed more positive effort and adjustment.
- Large literature spanning cultures, age groups, and instruments shows consistent pattern: autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation predict better engagement, learning, and adjustment than controlled forms (Burton et al., 2006; Chatzisarantis et al., 2003; Grolnick et al., 1991; Guay et al., 2010).

**Classroom Autonomy Support and Structure Facilitate Internalization:**
- **Jang, Reeve, & Deci (2010); Ryan & Connell (1989); Skinner & Belmont (1993):** Teachers' autonomy support positively predicted more autonomous (internalized) motivation, which in turn predicted a host of educational outcomes.
- **Hardre & Reeve (2003):** Rural American high schools -- students' perceptions of teacher autonomy support predicted autonomous motivation and perceived competence, which predicted school performance and intention to stay in school.
- **Jang et al. (2010):** American public high schools -- teachers' autonomy support was a very strong predictor of student engagement, more so than structure, though structure contributed additional variance for behavioral engagement.
- **Vansteenkiste, Sierens, et al. (2012):** High school students. Two configurations: (1) high autonomy support + clear structure = high autonomous motivation, deep learning, time management, low anxiety; (2) low autonomy support + vague structure = low autonomous motivation, maladaptive learning strategies.
- **Mouratidis, Vansteenkiste, Michou, & Lens (2013):** Perceptions of structure related positively to learning strategies and positive affect, negatively to negative affect.
- **Mouratidis, Vansteenkiste, Lens, & Sideridis (2008):** Positive feedback enhanced engagement, vitality, and other positive outcomes by supporting competence need satisfaction.

**Post-high-school transitions:**
- **Litalien, Ludtke, Parker, & Trautwein (2013):** Students' autonomous motivation for goals at high school graduation predicted psychological well-being 2 years later, regardless of whether they pursued university, vocational training, or noneducational paths.
- **Levesque, Zuehlke, Stanek, & Ryan (2004):** College students in America and Germany -- perceptions of high autonomy support were positively related to autonomous motivation and perceived competence, which predicted well-being in both countries.

**Autonomy-supportive rationale facilitates internalization:**
- **Reeve, Jang, Hardre, & Omura (2002):** College students studying conversational Chinese who were given autonomy-supportive rationale for its importance more fully internalized the regulation and put more effort into learning. Extended findings of Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, & Leone (1994) with college students.

**Autonomy support predicts grades and performance:**
- **Black & Deci (2000):** College students in organic chemistry. Those who found instructors more autonomy-supportive showed increases in autonomous self-regulation, perceived competence, and decreases in anxiety over the semester. Students with higher autonomous motivation received higher grades, even after controlling for ability (GPA and SAT). Students who experienced instructors as autonomy-supportive internalized course value more fully.

**Biological mediators:**
- **Reeve & Tseng (2011):** Autonomy-supportive vs. controlling teaching produced measurable biological differences in engagement.

### Students with Special Needs

- **Grolnick & Ryan (1990):** Elementary students with learning disabilities showed lower perceived competence and autonomous motivation than those without, but when compared to low-achieving students without disabilities, there were no differences -- the motivational pattern was about low achievement, not disability per se.
- **Deci, Hodges, Pierson, & Tomassone (1992):** 450+ students ages 8-21 in special education schools (LD or ED). Autonomy support and autonomous motivation were related to perceived competence, self-esteem, and more positive coping. Autonomy variables were more strongly predictive of well-being for ED students; competence variables were more predictive for LD students. ED = disability with autonomy deficiency; LD = disability with competence deficiency.
- **Shea, Millea, & Diehl (2013):** Adolescent students with high-functioning ASD. Those who perceived teachers as more autonomy-supportive reported greater autonomous motivation and higher perceived scholastic competence. Autonomous motivation mediated the relation between teacher autonomy support and perceived competence.

SDT argues that students with disabilities have the same basic psychological needs. Interventions must first and foremost respect students' autonomy. Promoting autonomy and self-determination yields more adaptive developmental outcomes than focusing on training and management.

### Gifted Students

- **Vallerand, Gagne, Senecal, & Pelletier (1994):** Gifted students in late elementary grades perceived themselves as more competent and were more intrinsically motivated than regular students.
- **Miserandino (1996):** Among elementary students with high ability, those higher in perceived competence and autonomous motivation were more positively engaged, with less boredom and anger. Both variables were significant predictors of school grades, even after controlling for standardized achievement.
- **Soenens, Vansteenkiste, & Luyten (2010):** Many parents engage in **achievement-oriented psychological control (APC)** -- coercive, manipulative methods to pressure high academic performance. **Garn & Jolly (2015):** APC was common in gifted populations and associated with higher academic amotivation and failure avoidance.

Having competence is not enough for a high-quality educational experience; autonomy must also be fostered.

### Need-Supportive Teaching Behaviors

**Elements of [[autonomy-support-in-education|Autonomy Support]]:**
Teachers who support students' needs begin by understanding and relating to students' perspectives. They provide opportunities for initiative, choices and options, rationale for requests, and encourage students to take responsibility for their learning. They provide information in an autonomy-supportive way. Controlling teachers pressure students to think, feel, or behave in particular ways, relating from the teacher's rather than the student's perspective.

Following Dewey (1938): autonomy-supportive teachers act in accordance with education as **development from within**; controlling teachers act in accordance with education as **formation from without**.

**Reeve, Bolt, & Cai (1999):** Teachers completed the Problems in Schools Questionnaire, then taught a videotaped class. Autonomy-supportive teachers listened more, made fewer directives, responded more to students' questions, attended more to students' desires, resisted giving answers/solutions, supported students' initiatives, and spoke in ways implying the students' perspectives.

**Reeve & Jang (2006):** Identified eight teacher behaviors empirically shown to be autonomy-supportive and six shown to be controlling (see Table 14.1):

**Autonomy-Supportive Behaviors:**
1. Listening to students
2. Making time for students' independent work
3. Giving students an opportunity to talk
4. Acknowledging signs of improvement and mastery
5. Encouraging students' effort
6. Offering progress-enabling hints when students seem stuck
7. Being responsive to students' comments and questions
8. Acknowledging students' experiences and perspectives

**Controlling Behaviors:**
1. Monopolizing the learning materials
2. Providing students too little time to work independently on solving problems
3. Telling students answers without giving them an opportunity to formulate them
4. Making demands and directives
5. Using controlling words such as "should" and "have to"
6. Using directed questions to control the flow of conversation

All eight autonomy-supportive behaviors positively correlated with autonomous motivation; all six controlling behaviors negatively correlated.

**Patall, Dent, Oyer, & Wynn (2012):** High school students' perceptions that teachers provided more choices, took their perspectives, and considered their interests led to greater autonomy need satisfaction, which led to perceiving the course as more valuable. Teachers' making the importance and usefulness of coursework clear directly added to perceived value.

**Haerens, Aelterman, Van den Berghe, De Meyer, Soenens, & Vansteenkiste (2013):** Extended prior work by specifying behaviors for relatedness support ("being enthusiastic and eager," "putting effort and energy into the lesson") and competence support ("giving clear instructions," "offering a rationale for tasks," "providing positive feedback") alongside autonomy support.

**Rogat, Witham, & Chinn (2014):** Videotaped seventh-grade science teachers to provide a "thick description" of autonomy-supportive practices used by real teachers.

**Wallace, Sung, & Williams (2014):** Middle school classroom management -- how skilled teachers scaffold students' autonomous self-regulatory capacities by supporting choice about strategy use and transferring responsibility for shaping the learning context.

When teachers are autonomy-supportive, they are typically also supportive of competence and relatedness needs, because understanding the student's perspective naturally leads to understanding when students need relational and competence supports.

### Effects of Grading, Evaluations, and Classroom Goals

#### Grading and Evaluations

Nearly all grading is comparative (relative to others), not criterion-based (mastery of material in an absolute sense). Grading is so pervasive it is an unquestioned feature of schools. SDT identifies two types of functional significance in feedback: **informational** (affirming success, enhancing competence) and **controlling** (pressuring toward specific behaviors/outcomes).

- **Grolnick & Ryan (1987):** Fifth-graders read a passage under three conditions: (1) **nondirected (ND)** -- read and rate interest, no test expected; (2) **noncontrolling-directed (NCD)** -- told they would be tested but not graded, to help researchers learn; (3) **controlling-directed (CD)** -- told they should read the text and would be tested and graded. Results: ND and NCD students experienced more interest and scored better on deep conceptual learning questions. CD students were just as good at rote memorization as NCD but significantly better than ND. At 1-week follow-up, the CD group had forgotten more of the rote material than the other groups and showed no advantage. **Less focus on grades facilitated intrinsic motivation and deeper conceptual learning.**

- **Kage & Namiki (1990):** Japanese middle school history classes. Students who graded their own quizzes (informational feedback) found the course more interesting and performed better on the final exam than students whose quizzes were teacher-graded and counted toward the course grade.

- **Benware & Deci (1984):** College students spent 3 hours learning neurophysiology. Half told they would be tested; half told they would teach the material to others. Those learning to teach found material more interesting, showed better conceptual learning, and scored comparably on rote questions. Having students learn to be tested is detrimental to intrinsic motivation and leads to poorer conceptual understanding.

- **Klapp (2015):** Natural experiment -- 8,000+ Swedish students. Grading in primary school had a significant negative main effect on subsequent achievement in grades 7-9. Negative effects were especially apparent for low-ability students, who had lower odds of finishing secondary education compared to ungraded students of similar ability.

**Functional analysis of grades:** Grades have two functions: (1) **competence-relevant feedback** (informational -- ideally useful) and (2) **gatekeeping** (determining eligibility). Research shows grades by themselves provide little competence-relevant feedback -- they merely let students know where they stand relative to others, which deters wanting to learn rather than facilitating greater effort or interest.

#### Performance and Mastery Goals

**Mastery goals** concern learning to enhance competence/knowledge. **Performance goals** focus on performing/doing well relative to others. Both can be differentiated into approach and avoidance types. Performance goals, especially avoidance goals, are the most detrimental in educational settings. Mastery goals are generally associated with greater well-being and sometimes enhanced performance.

SDT's key contribution: the **motives underlying** achievement goals matter more than the goals themselves.

- **Vansteenkiste, Smeets, Soenens, Lens, Matos, & Deci (2010):** The strength of performance-approach goals and the autonomous/controlled motives for pursuing them were assessed. When autonomous and controlled motives were entered into analyses, the performance-goal strength did not significantly predict outcomes (self-regulated learning, achievement, cheating). The autonomous vs. controlled motives accounted for the differential educational outcomes.

- **Benita, Roth, & Deci (2014):** When students adopted mastery goals in autonomy-supportive contexts, they had more positive emotional experiences and more interest/engagement than when mastery goals were adopted in controlling contexts.

- **Gillet, Lafreniere, Huyghebaert, & Fouquereau (2015):** Measured six types of achievement goals plus autonomous and controlled motives. The motives underlying goals were stronger predictors of subjective well-being than the goal endorsements themselves.

- **Vansteenkiste, Lens, Elliot, Soenens, & Mouratidis (2014):** Achievement goals should be limited to aims people have; the autonomous/controlled motives underlying those aims should be examined separately. The motives, not the goals, carry the explanatory weight.

Classic grading schemes (0-100%, A-F) are experienced as performance-goal structures. When applied in classrooms, they can undermine autonomy, competence, and relatedness. **Pulfrey, Buchs, & Butera (2011):** Expectations of being graded made students less autonomously motivated and more likely to adopt performance-avoidance goals.

### Influences on Autonomy-Supportive Teaching

For teachers to support students' autonomy, they must be afforded their own professional autonomy. Teachers, like students, face external pressures and performance goals that can undermine their need-supportive capacities.

- **Roth, Assor, Kanat-Maymon, & Kaplan (2006):** When teachers were autonomously motivated for teaching, their students perceived them as more autonomy-supportive, and students were more autonomously motivated for learning.

**Pressures from above -- accountability and performance standards:**
- **Deci, Spiegel, Ryan, Koestner, & Kauffman (1982):** Teachers teaching problem-solving skills. Half were told it was their responsibility to ensure students performed to high standards. Pressured teachers talked five times as much, made more directives, used more controlling words ("should," "have to"), and gave students solutions rather than allowing independent work. Their students completed more problems but solved fewer independently. Objective observers preferred to be taught by the non-pressured teachers.

- **Flink, Boggiano, & Barrett (1990):** Elementary teachers given curriculum framed as needing to raise achievement scores became more controlling and directive. Students judged by naive observers to have "better teachers" (more active teachers) actually performed worse. The more active the teacher, the less active the student.

- **Pelletier, Seguin-Levesque, & Legault (2002):** Teachers experienced pressure from above (accountability standards) and below (disengaged students). Both pressures negatively predicted autonomous motivation for teaching, which in turn negatively predicted autonomy support for students.

- **Fernet, Guay, Senecal, & Austin (2012):** Increases in overload and student disruptive behaviors led to less autonomous motivation, less perceived competence, emotional exhaustion, and less sense of personal accomplishment.

- **Bartholomew, Ntoumanis, Cuevas, & Lonsdale (2014):** More job pressures = more teacher burnout, mediated by frustration of basic psychological needs.

### High-Stakes Tests (HST)

In the U.S. and globally, governments have applied high-stakes incentives and sanctions tied to standardized test scores (PISA, TIMSS, NAEP). SDT argues this approach is misguided.

- **Hout & Elliott (2011):** U.S. National Academies task force report. Achievement tests do not provide adequate assessment of students' learning; incentives encourage teachers to focus narrowly on expected test material; based on many randomized trials (Springer et al., 2010), test-based incentive programs have had essentially no positive effects on achievement.

- **Fryer (2011):** Incentives given directly to students or families have on average no positive effect on achievement.

HST produces a cascade of controlling pressures: administrators experience tests as pressuring, which leads them to be controlling with teachers, who become controlling with students, undermining autonomous motivation and conceptual learning.

HST is associated with rewards and punishment threats, which have negative consequences for autonomous motivation (Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 2001). When people work for rewards but fail to receive them, they evidence even greater decrements than those who never expected rewards.

HST leads to **teaching to the test**, crowding out process-focused, responsive teaching. **Lonky & Reitman (1990):** Students in controlling contexts more likely to cheat on verbal reasoning tasks. Anecdotal and systematic evidence links HST to cheating (e.g., the Atlanta Public School District scandal, 2011; Chinese gaokao system stress).

SDT's criteria for judging educational policies: to what degree do they support autonomous motivation and, in turn, improve learning and adjustment?

### Making Schools Places That Promote Flourishing

#### School Reform Through Need Support

- **Every Classroom Everyday (ECED; Early et al., 2016):** Randomized control trial in challenged U.S. school districts. Intervention focused on enhancing classroom process, increasing rigor, aligning curricula, and creating more engaging classrooms from an SDT perspective. Intervention schools showed improved math and English progress. The most predictive element in positive change was **increased engagement in classes** (Early, Rogge, & Deci, 2014).

#### Professionalization and Training

Finland and Singapore -- top international performers despite massive curricular differences -- share one critical commonality: they treat teachers as professionals, invest in high-quality training, recruit the best, and help teachers internalize effective classroom practices. More competent and professional teachers benefit more from, and make better use of, professional autonomy.

Great teaching derives not from pressuring teachers toward test outcomes but from orienting educators toward creating effective, evidence-supported classroom processes. Training and evaluation should focus on the best educational strategies and their effect on the drivers of motivation: autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

#### Autonomy-Support Training Interventions

- **Reeve, Jang, Carrell, Barch, & Jeon (2004):** Trained teachers to incorporate autonomy support. Trained observers rated trained teachers as significantly more autonomy-supportive, and their students were more engaged than control-group students.

- **Edmunds, Ntoumanis, & Duda (2008):** Exercise class trainers trained in autonomy-supportive teaching showed greater increases in structure, interpersonal involvement, competence, relatedness, and positive affect.

- **Hardre & Reeve (2009):** Supervisors trained in autonomy support displayed significantly more autonomy-supportive style 5 weeks later; their supervised individuals showed more autonomous motivation and workplace engagement.

- **Su & Reeve (2011):** Meta-analytic review of 19 studies on autonomy-support training. Average effect size d=0.63 (large) for intervention groups becoming more autonomy-supportive, whether assessed by students'/employees' perceptions or trained observers.

#### Israeli School Reform (Assor, Kaplan, Feinberg, & Tal, 2009; Feinberg, Kaplan, Assor, & Kanat-Maymon, 2007)

Three Israeli elementary schools experiencing high violence. Reform based on SDT principles: didactic meetings introducing basic psychological needs to administrators and teachers, assessing need satisfaction as a first step, developing school-specific change plans with staff involvement, and change agents supporting staff members' basic needs during implementation. Bottom-up approach with high staff ownership.

**Results over 3 years:** Intervention teachers reported feeling more empathic and better about themselves as teachers, displayed increased limit setting on violence, reduced violence among students, and increased friendliness and caring in classrooms.

### The Umbrella Effect

Political and social demands often pressure educators toward suboptimal practices. However, individual educators can make a difference by creating need-supportive micro-environments. When administrators, principals, or teachers take the internal frame of reference of those they serve and empower them as much as possible, they provide a sheltered, nurturing educational environment -- an "umbrella" of need supports against the political storms surrounding education.

Central elements: taking others' internal frames of reference, encouraging self-initiation and exploration, offering choices, providing meaningful rationales, refraining from controlling language, and providing constructive feedback.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Mastery goals vs. SDT framework:** The achievement goal literature traditionally emphasized mastery vs. performance goals as the key distinction. SDT argues this distinction is secondary to the autonomous vs. controlled motives underlying any type of goal. Vansteenkiste et al. (2010) showed that once motives were accounted for, goal type did not independently predict outcomes.
- **Structure and autonomy:** Some educators assume structure and autonomy support are incompatible. SDT and evidence (Jang et al., 2010; Vansteenkiste, Sierens, et al., 2012) show they are complementary -- high autonomy support + clear structure produces the best outcomes.
- **Grades as motivators:** The near-universal assumption that grades motivate learning is contradicted by SDT research showing they primarily function as controlling mechanisms that undermine intrinsic motivation, deep learning, and long-term retention.
