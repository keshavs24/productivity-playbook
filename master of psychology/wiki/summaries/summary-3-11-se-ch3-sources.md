# Ch.3 Sources of Self-Efficacy — Summary

**Author(s):** Albert Bandura
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Self-efficacy beliefs are constructed from four principal sources of information:** [[mastery-experience]] (enactive mastery), [[vicarious-experience]] (modeling), [[verbal-persuasion]] (social persuasion), and [[physiological-and-affective-states]] (somatic and emotional indicators). These sources are not inherently enlightening -- they become instructive only through cognitive processing and reflective thought.
2. **Enactive mastery experiences are the most influential source** because they provide the most authentic evidence of whether one can muster whatever it takes to succeed. Successes build robust efficacy; failures undermine it, especially if failures occur before a sense of efficacy is firmly established.
3. **Efficacy information from all four sources is cognitively processed through two separable functions:** (a) the types of information people attend to and use as indicators of personal efficacy, and (b) the combination rules or heuristics people use to weight and integrate efficacy information from different sources.
4. **Preexisting self-schemata create interpretive biases** that contribute to the stability of efficacy beliefs -- experiences consistent with self-beliefs are noticed, given significance, and remembered; inconsistent experiences tend to be minimized, discounted, or forgotten.
5. **The four sources rarely operate in isolation** -- people simultaneously experience their own performances, observe others in similar pursuits, receive social evaluations, and read their physiological states; the relative weight of each source varies by domain and developmental level.

## Detailed Notes

### Source 1: Enactive Mastery Experience (pp.80-86)

Enactive mastery experiences are the most influential source of efficacy information because they provide the most authentic evidence of whether one can muster whatever it takes to succeed.

**Core dynamics:**
- Successes build a robust belief in personal efficacy; repeated failures undermine it, especially early in the course of events
- A resilient sense of efficacy requires experience in overcoming obstacles and setbacks through perseverant effort -- some difficulties serve a beneficial purpose in teaching that success usually requires sustained effort
- After people become convinced they have what it takes to succeed, they persevere in the face of adversity and quickly rebound from setbacks -- they emerge from adversity stronger and more able
- Elder and Liker (1982): Women who had adaptive resources during the Great Depression were left more self-assured and resourceful; women poorly equipped to cope were left intellectually astute but with a sense of ineffectualness and resignation
- Enactive mastery produces stronger and more generalized efficacy beliefs than vicarious experiences, cognitive simulations, or verbal instruction alone (Bandura et al., 1977; Biran & Wilson, 1981; Feltz, Landers, & Raeder, 1979; Gist, 1989; Gist, Schworer & Rosen, 1989)

**The inferential process:** Performance alone does not provide sufficient information to judge capability. Appraisal of personal efficacy is an inferential process in which the relative contribution of ability and nonability factors to performance successes and failures must be weighted. The impact of performance on efficacy beliefs depends on what is made of those performances, not on the performances per se.

**Cognitive processing factors that moderate the mastery-efficacy link:**

1. **Preexisting self-knowledge structures** (pp.81-82): Self-schemata of personal efficacy influence what people look for, how they interpret and organize efficacy information, and what they retrieve from memory. Efficacy beliefs are both products and constructors of experiences. Biases include:
   - Experiences consistent with self-beliefs are noticed; inconsistent ones are minimized or forgotten
   - People who doubt their efficacy view repeated successes as products of laborious effort rather than evidence of capability
   - Self-assured people believe even more highly in their capabilities following similar successes (Alden, 1987)
   - Preset efficacy beliefs created through fictitious feedback resist discreditation by the persuasory basis and even survive contradictory behavioral experiences for some time (Cervone & Palmer, 1990; Lawrence, 1988)

2. **Task difficulty and contextual factors** (pp.82-83): Mastery of difficult tasks conveys new efficacy information; succeeding at easy tasks is redundant with existing self-knowledge. Complex tasks call upon diverse subskills that vary in the level to which they have been developed. Contextual factors include:
   - Situational impediments vs. facilitators
   - Assistance provided by others
   - Adequacy of resources and equipment
   - Successes achieved with external assistance carry little efficacy value (credited to external aids)
   - Faulty performances under adverse conditions have weaker negative efficacy implications than those under optimal circumstances

3. **Effort expenditure** (pp.83-84): The amount of effort affects inferences of capability from performance. For young children, high effort means acquisition of more ability; for adults, the need to exert high effort to achieve things implies low ability. Nicholls and Miller (1984) report that effort has different ability implications for children vs. adults. Effort is also an important factor in self-appraisal of efficacy from failures -- low effort renders failures nondiagnostic. [[attribution-theory]] includes ability, effort, task difficulty, and luck as factors, but in a sociocognitive analysis these serve as conveyors of efficacy information rather than categories of causes of behavior.

4. **Selective self-monitoring and reconstruction of enactive experiences** (pp.85-86): Self-efficacy is affected by biases in self-monitoring of performances. Many factors contribute to variability in performance quality. People who selectively attend to and recall poorer performances underestimate their efficacy. Research on self-modeling shows efficacy is enhanced by selective focus on personal attainments (Dowrick, 1983; Schunk & Hanson, 1989a). After observing edited videotapes of themselves performing successfully, people display substantial gains in perceived efficacy and performance.

5. **Attainment trajectories** (pp.86): Temporal changes in attainments carry efficacy implications. Those who experience periodic failures but continue to improve over time are more apt to raise their sense of efficacy than those who succeed but see performance leveling off. If a temporary plateau is interpreted as approaching the limits of capacity, expectations for future improvement diminish.

### Source 2: Vicarious Experience (pp.86-101)

Modeling serves as an effective tool for promoting a sense of personal efficacy, especially when people have had little prior experience on which to base evaluations of their capabilities.

**Social comparison as the mechanism:** For most activities, there are no absolute measures of adequacy. People must appraise their capabilities in relation to the attainments of others. Social comparison operates as a primary factor in the self-appraisal of capabilities (Festinger, 1954; Goethals & Darley, 1977; Suls & Miller, 1977).

**Four subprocesses of observational learning** (Figure 3.1, p.89):
1. **Attentional processes:** Determine what is selectively observed -- influenced by salience, affective valence, complexity, prevalence, accessibility, and functional value of modeled events, plus observer attributes
2. **Retention processes:** Cognitive construction through symbolic coding and cognitive organization, plus rehearsal
3. **Production processes:** Representational guidance, response production, corrective adjustment through monitoring of enactments and conception matching
4. **Motivational processes:** External incentives, vicarious incentives, self-incentives, plus observer attributes

**Key moderating variables for vicarious efficacy effects:**

1. **Performance similarity** (pp.96): Persons who are similar or slightly higher in ability provide the most informative comparative information. Brown and Inouye (1978): Observers who believed themselves superior to a failing model maintained high self-efficacy despite repeated failure; those who perceived comparable ability experienced devastating drops in perceived efficacy and gave up quickly (Figure 3.2).

2. **Attribute similarity** (pp.98-99): Self-efficacy appraisals are often based on similarity to models in personal characteristics assumed to be predictive of performance (age, sex, education, race, ethnic designation). These preconceptions arise from cultural stereotyping and overgeneralization. The same physical stamina modeled by a nonathletic female raises women's perceived physical efficacy, whereas the same modeled by an athletic male does not (Gould & Weiss, 1981). When both model competence and other attributes are varied, model competence overrides age dissimilarity (Lirgg & Feltz, 1991).

3. **Multiplicity and diversity of modeling** (pp.99): Exposure to multiple skilled models produces stronger belief in one's efficacy than observing a single skilled model (Schunk, Hanson, & Cox, 1987). Diversified modeling (different people mastering different difficult tasks) is superior to exposure to the same performances by a single model (Bandura & Menlove, 1968; Kazdin, 1974a, 1975, 1976).

4. **Coping models vs. mastery models** (pp.99-100): Coping models begin timorously but gradually overcome difficulties through determined coping efforts. Mastery models perform calmly and faultlessly. Observers who are unsure of themselves may benefit more from coping models (Kazdin, 1973; Meichenbaum, 1971). However, masterly modeling is uniformly effective in raising efficacy beliefs (Bandura, 1986a; Rosenthal & Steffek, 1991). When observers' attention is explicitly focused on cognitive skills being modeled, coping and masterly modeling are equally effective.

5. **Model competence** (pp.101): Among model characteristics, level of competence carries especially heavy weight. Competent models command more attention and exert greater instructional influence.

**Modes of modeling influence** (pp.92-95):
- **Behavioral modeling:** Highly functional patterns adopted in the same form
- **Symbolic modeling:** Television, media, video technologies vastly expand the range of models
- **Cognitive modeling:** Models verbalize thought processes aloud; more effective than direct instruction (Gorrell & Capron, 1990). Cognitive modeling with skill enactment heightens efficacy more than cognitive modeling alone (Fecteau & Stoppard, 1983)
- **Self-modeling:** Videotaped replays of successful performances with missteps edited out (Dowrick, 1983, 1991). More effective than didactic instruction in raising perceived efficacy (Scraba, 1990)
- **Cognitive self-modeling:** Visualizing oneself confronting and mastering progressively more challenging situations (Bandura, 1986a; Corbin, 1972; Feltz & Landers, 1983)

### Source 3: Verbal Persuasion (pp.101-106)

Social persuasion strengthens people's beliefs that they possess the capabilities to achieve what they seek.

**Key findings:**
- **Verbal persuasion alone is limited** but can bolster self-change if within realistic bounds
- **Persuasory efficacy attributions** have greatest impact on people who have some reason to believe they can produce effects (Chambliss & Murray, 1979a, 1979b)

**Framing of performance feedback** (pp.101-103):
- Evaluative feedback highlighting personal capabilities raises efficacy beliefs (Schunk, 1982; Schunk & Cox, 1986)
- Ability feedback enhances efficacy more than effort feedback (Schunk, 1984b)
- **Effort attribution paradox:** Being told repeatedly that progress is a product of high effort conveys that one's talents must be quite limited (Schunk & Rice, 1986). Telling people they have ability and gained it by hard work produces a lower sense of efficacy than telling them their progress shows ability without reference to effort
- **[[framing-effects]] in efficacy appraisal** (Jourden, 1991): Feedback framed as performance gains enhanced efficacy beliefs, goals, and accomplishment; feedback framed as shortfalls from the standard detracted from efficacy (Figure 3.3)
- Social evaluations of limited capability are often conveyed indirectly through unchallenging tasks, excessive praise for mediocre performances, and unsolicited help

**Knowledgeability and credibility** (pp.104-105):
- Persuasory opinions are only as strong as the recipient's confidence in the persuader
- People trust evaluations by those skilled in the activity or possessing rich knowledge from observing many aspirants
- Even credible advisors may be discounted if they do not fully understand task demands

**Degree of appraisal disparity** (pp.105-106):
- Persuasory efficacy appraisals are most believable when only moderately beyond current capability
- Social persuasion serves best as part of a multifaceted strategy: structuring activities for high initial success, modeling requisite skills, providing explicit progress feedback

**Skilled efficacy builders** (pp.106): Structure activities that bring success, avoid premature placement in failure-producing situations, and encourage measurement of success in terms of self-improvement rather than triumphs over others.

### Source 4: Physiological and Affective States (pp.106-113)

People rely partly on somatic information conveyed by physiological and emotional states in judging their capabilities. Especially relevant in domains involving physical accomplishments, health functioning, and coping with stressors.

**Key principles:**
- People read physiological activation in stressful situations as signs of vulnerability to dysfunction
- Stress reactions to inefficacious control generate further stress through anticipatory self-arousal -- a vicious cycle
- Information conveyed by physiological states affects self-efficacy only through cognitive processing

**Perceived source of activation** (pp.107): Environmental factors exert strong influence on interpretation. Speakers who ascribe sweating to room discomfort read their physiology differently from those who view it as distress reflecting personal failings.

**Level of activation** (pp.108-109): It is not sheer intensity but how reactions are perceived and interpreted. High achievers view arousal as an energizing facilitator; low achievers regard it as a debilitator (Hollandsworth et al., 1979). Moderate levels heighten attentiveness; high arousal disrupts functioning. Optimum level depends on activity complexity.

**Construal biases** (pp.109): Preexisting efficacy beliefs create attentional, interpretive, and memory biases in processing somatic information. Low efficacy heightens sensitivity to bodily arousal. Panic-prone individuals misinterpret benign sensations as catastrophic (Ehlers et al., 1988). Reading arousal as challenge can boost perceived efficacy.

**Impact of mood on self-efficacy judgment** (pp.111-113):
- **Affective priming** (Bower, 1983): Mood activates mood-congruent memories -- connects to [[availability]]
- **Cognitive priming:** Successes/failures cue thoughts of other past successes/failures
- Induced positive mood enhances perceived efficacy; despondent mood diminishes it (Forgas, Bower, & Moylan, 1990; Salovey & Birnbaum, 1989)
- Impact is widely generalized across domains (Kavanagh & Bower, 1985, Figure 3.4)
- But mood cannot override the range of past experiences stored in memory
- Mood mismatches: People who fail under happy mood overestimate capabilities; those who succeed under sad mood underestimate them (Wright & Mischel, 1982)
- Bidirectional relationship: Low efficacy breeds depression; depression diminishes efficacy

### Integration of Efficacy Information (pp.113-115)

- Factors carrying efficacy information vary in informativeness and interrelatedness
- **Integration rules:** Additive, relative weighting, multiplicative-combination, or configural rules
- Under emotional states, people are poor at integrating multidimensional information (Nisbett & Ross, 1980; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974), relying on few factors and simplified heuristics -- connects to [[bounded-rationality]]
- Self-referent experiences more likely to pose threats to self-esteem; affect has different effects on personal vs. social judgment
- The ability to discern, weight, and integrate efficacy information improves with development of cognitive and metacognitive skills

## Contradictions or Tensions
- **Coping vs. mastery models:** Mixed results on which is more effective -- depends on strategy focus and model similarity
- **Effort attribution paradox:** Telling children progress is due to effort can undermine efficacy if it conveys limited ability -- relevant to [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]]
