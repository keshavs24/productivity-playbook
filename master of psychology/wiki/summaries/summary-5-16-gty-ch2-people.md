# Getting to Yes Ch.2: Separate the People from the Problem — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 2
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Negotiators are people first.** You are not dealing with abstract "representatives" but with human beings who have emotions, deeply held values, different backgrounds, and unpredictable viewpoints. The human aspect can be either helpful or disastrous.
2. **Every negotiator has two kinds of interests: substance and relationship.** The ongoing relationship is usually more important than any single transaction. Positional bargaining puts relationship and substance in conflict — trading one for the other.
3. **Separate the relationship from the substance; deal directly with the people problem.** Base the relationship on accurate perceptions, clear communication, appropriate emotions, and a forward-looking, purposive outlook. Deal with people problems directly; don't try to solve them with substantive concessions.
4. **People problems fall into three categories: perception, emotion, and communication.** Each requires distinct techniques.
5. **Prevention works best.** Build personal and organizational relationships with the other side before they become people problems. It is much easier to attribute diabolical intentions to an unknown abstraction than to someone you know personally.

## Detailed Notes

### Perception

**Put yourself in their shoes.** People tend to see what they want to see, picking out facts that confirm prior perceptions and disregarding those that call them into question. Each side sees only the merits of its case and only the faults of the other's. The ability to see the situation as the other side sees it is one of the most important skills a negotiator can possess — not just *knowing* they see things differently but *understanding empathetically* the power of their point of view.

**Tenant-landlady example:** Contrasting perceptions table shows how the same facts (rent amount, apartment condition, payment reliability) are interpreted completely differently by each party. This connects to [[self-serving-bias-in-negotiation]] and [[framing-effects]].

**Don't deduce their intentions from your fears.** We habitually assume the worst. "They met in a bar, where he offered her a ride home" — the ending is almost never what we assume. A suspicious interpretation follows naturally from existing perceptions ([[reactive-devaluation]]).

**Don't blame them for your problem.** Even if blaming is justified, it is usually counterproductive. The other side will become defensive and resist what you say. Separate the symptoms from the person — "Our rotary generator has broken down again" not "You sold us a defective generator."

**Discuss each other's perceptions.** Make them explicit. As long as you do this frankly and honestly, each side can take what the other says seriously without requiring agreement.

**Look for opportunities to act inconsistently with their perceptions.** Sadat's visit to Jerusalem in November 1977 is the paradigmatic example — by flying to the capital of his enemies, he shattered the perception that Egypt was an implacable foe.

**Give them a stake in the outcome by making sure they participate in the process.** If they are not involved in the process, they are hardly likely to approve the product. The South African white parliamentary process that excluded blacks is the negative example.

**Face-saving: Make your proposals consistent with their values.** Face-saving is not a "derogatory flavor" — it reflects a person's need to reconcile a negotiated stand with their principles and past words/deeds. A judge writes an opinion on a court ruling, saving face for both sides; a negotiator is no different.

### Emotion

**First recognize and understand emotions — theirs and yours.** Look at yourself during the negotiation. Are you feeling nervous? Is your stomach upset? Are you angry at the other side?

**Make emotions explicit and acknowledge them as legitimate.** Talk with the other side about their emotions and your own. "We're afraid an agreement will not be kept even if one is reached."

**Allow the other side to let off steam.** People obtain psychological release through the simple process of recounting their grievances. The best strategy when the other side lets off steam is to listen quietly without responding to their attacks, occasionally asking the speaker to continue.

**Don't react to emotional outbursts.** Releasing emotions can be risky if it leads to an emotional reaction. The 1950s Human Relations Committee in the steel industry adopted the rule that only one person could get angry at a time — making it legitimate for others not to respond stormily. Breaking the rule implies you have lost self-control, so you lose some face.

**Use symbolic gestures.** A note of sympathy, a statement of regret, a visit to a cemetery, a small present for a grandchild — these can defuse hostile emotional situations at small cost. An apology may be one of the least costly and most rewarding investments you can make.

### Communication

Three big problems:
1. **Negotiators may not be talking to each other** — instead playing to the gallery, talking to third parties or their own constituency
2. **Even if you are talking directly, they may not be hearing you** — people are so busy thinking about what they will say next that they forget to listen
3. **Misunderstanding** — what one says the other may misinterpret, especially across languages ("compromise" in English has a positive meaning; "mediator" in Persian suggests "meddler")

**Listen actively and acknowledge what is being said.** Standard techniques: ask the other party to spell out carefully and clearly what they mean, request that ideas be repeated if there is ambiguity. Paraphrase their position back to them — "Let me see if I follow you. From your point of view, the situation looks like this..."

**Speak to be understood.** It is easy to forget that a negotiation is not a debate. Treat your opposite number as a fellow judge with whom you are trying to work out a joint opinion.

**Speak about yourself, not about them.** "I feel let down" instead of "You broke your word." A statement about how you feel is difficult to challenge.

**Speak for a purpose.** Sometimes the problem is too much communication, not too little. Full disclosure of how flexible you are may make it harder to reach agreement rather than easier.

### Prevention Works Best
- **Build a working relationship** before the negotiation begins. Get to know them informally. Benjamin Franklin's favorite technique was to ask an adversary if he could borrow a certain book — the favor created a relationship.
- **Face the problem, not the people.** If negotiators view themselves as adversaries in a personal face-to-face confrontation, it is difficult to separate their relationship from the substantive problem. Sit side by side facing the problem. Like two shipwrecked sailors in a lifeboat quarreling over limited rations, they will want to disentangle the objective problems from the people and engage in side-by-side efforts.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The emphasis on empathetic perspective-taking directly maps to research on [[self-serving-bias-in-negotiation]] — Fisher and Ury's techniques are essentially debiasing strategies for the self-serving bias
- The "don't deduce intentions from fears" principle connects to [[reactive-devaluation]] — the tendency to devalue proposals simply because they come from the other side
- Face-saving connects to [[loss-aversion]] — people will fight harder to avoid the perception of having lost than to achieve equivalent gains
