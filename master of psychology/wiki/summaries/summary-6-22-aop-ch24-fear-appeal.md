# Age of Propaganda Ch.24: The Fear Appeal — Summary

**Authors:** Anthony Pratkanis & Elliot Aronson
**Source:** *Age of Propaganda: The Everyday Use and Abuse of Persuasion* (Revised Edition), Chapter 24
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **A fear appeal is most effective when four conditions are met:** (1) it scares the hell out of people, (2) it offers a **specific recommendation** for overcoming the fear-arousing threat, (3) the recommended action is perceived as **effective** for reducing the threat, and (4) the message recipient believes he or she **can perform** the recommended action.
2. **The fear-then-relief cycle is a powerful compliance technique.** Dolinski and Nawrat showed that people who experienced sudden fear (police whistle while jaywalking) followed by relief (no actual arrest) were significantly more likely to comply with subsequent requests — the arousal and relief distract from evaluating the request.
3. **Self-esteem moderates the effectiveness of fear appeals.** People with high self-esteem respond well to high fear — they take action. People with low self-esteem are overwhelmed by high fear and may freeze or withdraw. But given time, low-self-esteem individuals eventually act similarly to high-self-esteem ones.
4. **Fear appeals without doable solutions fail.** Anti-nuclear campaigns, "just say no" to drugs, and anti-drug-abuse PSAs often fail because they frighten without providing a specific, perceived-as-effective, easy-to-perform response. People avoid the fear by avoiding the issue.

## Detailed Notes

### Historical Examples

**Jonathan Edwards (1741):** "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" — terrified his congregation with images of hellfire and damnation, then offered a simple remedy: commit to Christ. Thousands converted during the Great Awakening.

**Adolf Hitler:** Described Jews and communists as existential threats to the German nation, then offered a simple remedy: join the Nazi party. Both Edwards and Hitler used the same fear-appeal structure — threaten dire consequences, then offer an easy, doable response.

### The Fear-Then-Relief Effect

Dolinski and Nawrat's experiments: They aroused fear by blowing a police whistle at jaywalkers or placing a fake ticket on car windshields. When the fear was quickly relieved (no arrest; the "ticket" was just an ad for hair-growth medicine), the jaywalkers and motorists were significantly more likely to comply with a subsequent request (completing a questionnaire, aiding a charity). The arousal of fear and its subsequent relief distract attention from evaluating the request, resulting in more compliance. This principle is well known to terrorists and interrogators — the Inquisition used cycles of torture followed by kindness to extract confessions.

### Leventhal's Research on Fear and Action

Howard Leventhal's experiments on smoking and chest X-rays:

**Smoking experiment:** High-fear communication (gory film of lung cancer operation) produced much greater *intention* to stop smoking. But unless accompanied by **specific behavioral instructions** (buy a magazine instead of cigarettes, drink water when urge strikes), it produced little actual behavior change. The combination of fear arousal + specific instructions produced the best results — students smoked less four months later.

**Tetanus shots:** Students urged to get tetanus shots via fear appeal. High-fear appeals were more effective than low-fear for changing *attitudes* and *stated intentions*. But specific instructions (map showing health services location, suggested times) had the biggest impact on *actual behavior*: 28% of those with instructions got shots vs. only 3% without. A control group with instructions but no fear appeal — 0% got shots. Fear was necessary to motivate; instructions were necessary to act.

### The Four Components of Effective Fear Appeals

1. **Scare them** — the more frightened, the more likely to take preventive action (all else equal)
2. **Offer a specific recommendation** for overcoming the threat
3. **The recommended action must be perceived as effective** for reducing the threat
4. **The recipient must believe they can perform the action** — it must seem doable

This explains why some fear appeals fail:
- **Nuclear disarmament:** Fear is high, but there is no simple, doable individual response
- **"Just say no" to drugs:** Teenagers don't perceive "just saying no" as an effective or doable response when friends are pressuring them
- **Fallout shelters (1950s):** Fear was high AND building a shelter was a doable response — so many people did it

### Self-Esteem and Fear

Leventhal discovered that **self-esteem moderates** the response to fear:
- **High self-esteem:** People respond well to high fear — they feel capable of coping and take action immediately
- **Low self-esteem:** High fear is overwhelming and debilitating. It makes people feel like pulling the covers over their heads. They are *more* likely to act on low or moderate fear
- **Important nuance:** After a delay, people with low self-esteem eventually catch up and act similarly to high-self-esteem individuals

This has propaganda implications: it is relatively easy to make people with high self-esteem behave like people with low self-esteem — simply overwhelm them with threats and make them feel there is nothing they can do. This is how extreme fear creates a debilitated, compliant population.

### The Propagandist's Fear Formula

The propagandist's formula: First arouse fear, channeling all thoughts toward removing the threat. Then offer a simple, doable response — one that happens to be exactly what the propagandist wanted all along. This is the structure used by fire-and-brimstone preachers, demagogues, life insurance salesmen, and political candidates alike.
