# Negotiation Genius Ch.7 — Strategies of Influence

**Author(s):** [[malhotra|Deepak Malhotra]], [[bazerman|Max Bazerman]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 5
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. Eight proven influence strategies increase the likelihood that others will accept your requests, offers, and proposals — *without* requiring you to change your position or improve the merits of your offer.
2. These strategies are grounded in the psychology of persuasion, drawing heavily on [[cialdini|Robert Cialdini's]] research on influence and [[loss-aversion|Kahneman and Tversky's]] work on prospect theory.
3. Equally important are six defense strategies for protecting yourself when you are the *target* of influence tactics.
4. Influence strategies alone are insufficient — negotiators who rely exclusively on influence without also creating value will achieve only limited success.

## Detailed Notes

### Eight Strategies of Influence

#### Strategy 1: Highlight Their Potential Losses Rather Than Their Potential Gains
- Based on [[loss-aversion]]: people weigh potential losses more heavily than equivalent gains
- University of Santa Cruz energy study: homeowners told they would *lose* X cents/day by not insulating were significantly more likely to purchase insulation than those told they would *save* X cents/day
- HIV testing study: loss-framed video (63% tested within two weeks) vs. gain-framed video (23% tested)
- **Caveat:** Overreliance on loss frames can be perceived as hostile; use strategically and sparingly

#### Strategy 2: Disaggregate Their Gains and Aggregate Their Losses
- People prefer *finding* money in installments but *losing* money in one lump sum (Thaler's mental accounting)
- **Disaggregate gains:** Break concessions into smaller pieces delivered separately; share good news in installments
- **Aggregate losses:** Make one comprehensive demand rather than serial partial demands; share bad news all at once

#### Strategy 3: Employ the "Door in the Face" Technique (DITF)
- [[cialdini|Cialdini's]] research: zoo chaperone request yielded 17% compliance alone, but 50% when preceded by a rejected extreme request (3-year counselor commitment)
- Mechanisms: perceived concession triggering reciprocity + contrast effect
- **Timing:** Make the moderate request very soon after the extreme one is rejected

#### Strategy 4: Employ the "Foot in the Door" Technique (FITD)
- Bar petition study: patrons who signed an anti-drunk-driving petition were 5.8x more likely to wait for a taxi when intoxicated (58% vs. 10%)
- Mechanism: commitment and consistency
- **Key distinction from DITF:** DITF = aim for rejection then moderate (makes key demand seem reasonable). FITD = aim for compliance then escalate (builds commitment toward key demand).

#### Strategy 5: Leverage the Power of Justification
- Langer's copy machine study: "May I use the Xerox machine?" (60%) vs. "...because I have to make some copies" (93%) — even inane justification dramatically increases compliance
- Never make a demand that cannot fit "I am asking for X because..."
- Three levels: (A) bare demand, (B) demand + reason, (C) demand + reason + supporting evidence

#### Strategy 6: Leverage the Power of Social Proof
- Infomercial case: "If operators are busy, please call again" vastly outperformed "Operators are standing by" — implied high demand
- Applications: limit open-house viewings, provide short availability windows, list prominent clients

#### Strategy 7: Make Token Unilateral Concessions
- Trade association survey: $50 promised incentive (23.3% response) vs. $1 enclosed bill (40.7% response) — the gift triggered reciprocity
- Recipients are insensitive to the *cost* incurred by the giver — even token concessions induce reciprocity
- Applications: agree to a convenient meeting time/location, bring refreshments, agree to a smaller request early

#### Strategy 8: Use Reference Points to Make Offers Seem Reasonable
- Kahneman & Tversky: people needed $20 discount to drive 20 min for a $50 calculator but $200 for a $2,000 computer — same time valued 10x differently
- Thaler's beer study: people pay more for beer from a "fancy resort hotel" vs. a "run-down grocery store" despite identical consumption
- Choose your reference points strategically — the same deal seems great or poor depending on the comparison

### Six Defense Strategies Against Influence Tactics

1. **Prepare systematically:** BATNA analysis, ZOPA evaluation, interest identification
2. **Create a scoring system:** Objectively evaluate every proposal
3. **Explicitly separate information from influence:** "Did I learn something new? Would I have done this yesterday?"
4. **Rephrase their offer in other terms:** Convert loss-frame to gain-frame and vice versa
5. **Appoint a devil's advocate:** Trustworthy, not invested in initial strategy, no conflict of interest
6. **If possible, do not negotiate under time pressure:** Influence tactics are more effective when targets must respond quickly

### The Limits of Influence
- Influence strategies do not improve your *offer* — they improve your ability to *sell* what you have
- Against a tough negotiator or in a bitter dispute, "soft" influence strategies alone will leave you outgunned
