# Getting to Yes Ch.3: Focus on Interests, Not Positions — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 3
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **The basic problem in a negotiation lies not in conflicting positions, but in the conflict between each side's needs, desires, concerns, and fears.** Positions are what you decided; interests are what caused you to so decide. Interests are the silent movers behind the hubbub of positions.
2. **The Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty at Camp David (1978) is the paradigm case.** Positions were incompatible (both wanted the Sinai Peninsula). But interests could be reconciled: Israel wanted security; Egypt wanted sovereignty. The solution: return the Sinai to complete Egyptian sovereignty with demilitarized zones ensuring Israeli security.
3. **Reconciling interests rather than positions works for two reasons.** First, for every interest there usually exist several possible positions that could satisfy it. Second, behind opposed positions lie many more interests that are compatible than conflicting.
4. **Behind opposed positions lie shared and compatible interests, as well as conflicting ones.** The landlord-tenant example reveals shared interests (stability, good maintenance, good relationship) and differing but non-conflicting interests (paint preferences, timing of payment).
5. **The most powerful interests are basic human needs:** security, economic well-being, a sense of belonging, recognition, and control over one's life.

## Detailed Notes

### The Library Window Story
Two people quarrel over an open window in a library. One wants it open, the other closed. No compromise satisfies both. The librarian asks *why* — one wants fresh air, the other wants to avoid the draft. She opens a window in the next room, bringing in fresh air without a draft. The solution was invisible at the level of positions.

### Interests Define the Problem
- "I am trying to get him to stop that real estate development next door" is a position
- "He needs the cash; I want peace and quiet" are the interests
- "He needs at least $100,000 to settle with his ex-wife. I told my family I wouldn't pay more than $95,000 for a house" — even deeper positions masking even deeper interests

### Camp David Case Study
Egypt had occupied the Sinai since the Six Day War of 1967. When Egypt and Israel sat down in 1978:
- **Israel's position:** Keep some of the Sinai
- **Egypt's position:** Return every inch of the Sinai to Egyptian sovereignty
- **Compromising on positions** (dividing the Sinai with boundary lines) was unacceptable to both
- **Israel's interest:** Security — they did not want Egyptian tanks poised on their border
- **Egypt's interest:** Sovereignty — the Sinai had been part of Egypt since the Pharaohs; Egypt had only recently regained full sovereignty and was not about to cede territory
- **Solution:** The Sinai was returned to complete Egyptian sovereignty AND demilitarized, with large areas ensuring Israeli security. The Egyptian flag would fly everywhere, but Egyptian tanks would be nowhere near Israel.

This is the central demonstration of the [[positions-vs-interests]] distinction. It connects directly to [[fixed-pie-bias]] — the assumption that interests must be zero-sum when they often are not.

### How Do You Identify Interests?

**Ask "Why?"** Put yourself in their shoes. Why does your landlord prefer to fix the rent in a five-year lease year by year? The answer reveals his interests.

**Ask "Why not?" Think about their choice.** One of the most useful ways to uncover interests is to identify the basic decision that those on the other side probably see you asking them for, and then ask yourself why they have not made that decision.

**The Iranian hostage example (1980):** Constructing the "Presently Perceived Choice" of an Iranian student leader shows why they held the hostages so long — the choice sheet was heavily weighted toward saying NO. Understanding this choice reveals what the U.S. needed to change: make saying YES more attractive by altering the consequences.

**Realize that each side has multiple interests, not just one.** During Vietnam, President Johnson lumped together all members of North Vietnam, the Vietcong, the Soviet Union, and China as "he" — failing to appreciate the differing interests of the various factions.

**The most powerful interests are basic human needs:**
- Security
- Economic well-being
- A sense of belonging
- Recognition
- Control over one's life

The U.S.-Mexico natural gas negotiation failed because the U.S. treated it as purely about price, ignoring the Mexican interest in being treated with respect and as an equal. The U.S. action ("one more attempt to bully Mexico") produced enormous anger and a political impasse.

### Talking About Interests

**Make your interests come alive.** Be specific. "Three times in the last week, a child was almost run over by one of your trucks" is better than "your trucks are dangerous."

**Acknowledge their interests as part of the problem.** People listen better if they feel you have understood their interests. "As I understand it, your interests as a construction company are basically to get the job done quickly at minimum cost."

**Put the problem before your answer.** Give your interests and reasoning first, then your conclusions or proposals. If you announce a proposal first, the other side probably will not bother to listen to the reasons.

**Look forward, not back.** If you ask two people why they are arguing, the answer will typically identify a cause, not a purpose. "Who should be blamed?" is backward-looking. "What should we do tomorrow?" is forward-looking.

**Be concrete but flexible.** "Illustrative specificity" is the key concept. Think in terms of more than one option that meets your interests. "A $5,000,000 salary would take care of your interest in receiving the salary you feel you are worth. Something on the order of a five-year contract should meet your need for job security."

**Be hard on the problem, soft on the people.** You can be just as hard in talking about your interests as any negotiator can be in talking about his position. The combination of support and attack may seem inconsistent, but psychologically (the theory of [[cognitive-dissonance]]) the inconsistency helps make it work — by attacking the problem while giving positive support to the human beings on the other side, you create cognitive dissonance that tempts them to dissociate from the problem to join you.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The Camp David case study directly challenges [[fixed-pie-bias]] — the assumption that one side's gain must be the other's loss
- The emphasis on basic human needs connects to [[self-determination-theory-framework]] and [[basic-psychological-needs]] — though Fisher and Ury's need categories differ from SDT's autonomy/competence/relatedness framework, there is significant overlap (security ~ competence, belonging ~ relatedness, control ~ autonomy)
- The "hard on the problem, soft on the people" formulation exploits [[cognitive-dissonance]] to motivate change
