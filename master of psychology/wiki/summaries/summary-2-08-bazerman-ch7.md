# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.7 — Motivational and Emotional Influences on Decision Making

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.7, pp.103-118)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Decision making is driven by the tension between two "selves" -- the impulsive "want" self (System 1) and the reflective "should" self (System 2), which activate different brain regions
2. Self-serving reasoning biases fairness perceptions unconsciously: people first determine their preferred outcome, then select fairness criteria to justify it
3. Specific emotions have distinct, predictable effects on judgment -- disgust triggers disposal, sadness triggers acquisition, anger increases risk tolerance
4. Hyperbolic discounting causes systematic overweighting of the present, explaining procrastination, undersaving, and environmental degradation
5. Regret avoidance distorts decisions: silver medalists are unhappier than bronze medalists because counterfactual "almost won gold" thoughts dominate

## Detailed Notes

### When Emotion and Cognition Collide
The chapter opens with the trolley/footbridge dilemma. Most people would flip a switch to divert a trolley (killing 1 to save 5) but refuse to push a man off a bridge for the same outcome. The inconsistency reveals that emotional responses (pushing someone = visceral revulsion) override utilitarian calculation.

**Multiple Selves:** Schelling (1984): people behave like two individuals -- "one who wants clear lungs and long life and another who adores tobacco." This explains simultaneous industries for smoking and quitting, obesity and dieting, drugs and rehab, procrastination and productivity tools.

**Neural basis:** McClure, Laibson, Loewenstein, & Cohen (2004): Different brain areas activate for immediate vs. delayed rewards. System 1 (amygdala, basal ganglia, lateral temporal cortex) handles automatic/emotional responses. System 2 (anterior cingulate cortex, prefrontal cortex, medial-temporal lobe) handles deliberative thought. The prefrontal cortex integrates both (Bechara et al., 1999). Damage to it impairs want/should tradeoffs. People with emotional brain damage sometimes make MORE rational decisions (Shiv et al., 2005) -- they take more sensible risks.

**Want/should self and time:** Milkman, Rogers, and Bazerman (2007): Movie rental study -- when ordering for days later (future), people choose "should" movies (documentaries). When movies arrive (present), they watch "want" movies (comedies). "Should" movies stayed unplayed significantly longer. Same pattern in online grocery ordering: longer lead times = more vegetables, shorter = more ice cream.

Rogers and Bazerman (2008): Support for "should" policies (like a gas tax) increases significantly when implementation is in the future rather than immediate.

### The Impact of Temporal Differences
Rational discounting is exponential (same percentage per period). Actual human discounting is **hyperbolic** (Laibson, 1994): dramatically overweight the present, with sensitivity dropping off quickly.

Example: Prefer $1 today over $0.99 tomorrow (huge subjective difference), but $1 in 365 days vs. $1 in 366 days feels identical. The near future is vivid and emotional; the distant future is abstract and cognitive.

Consequences: Homeowners underinsulate. Organizations use cheap building materials despite higher long-term costs. The 2008 housing crisis: borrowers, lenders, and politicians all failed to anticipate long-term consequences of short-term incentives.

### Reconciling Internal Conflicts
Three approaches:
1. **Economists (Schelling, 1984; Thaler, 1980):** The "should" self as planner controls the "want" self as doer. Pre-commitment devices (Antabuse for alcoholics, revocability periods for purchases, Ulysses binding himself to the mast).
2. **Decision theorists (Raiffa, 1968):** Question each self to identify which is making the error. Communication between selves until reconciliation.
3. **Negotiation framework:** Treat the two selves as negotiating parties. Impose Pareto efficiency, require agreement (no vetoes from either side), keep terms within the "want" self's bargaining zone.

### Self-Serving Reasoning
Perceptions of fairness are systematically biased by self-interest -- but unconsciously.

**Hastorf & Cantril (1954):** Princeton and Dartmouth students watching the same football film saw completely different games. Each side perceived the other as more aggressive.

**U.S. News survey:** 85% said "If someone sues you and you win, should he pay your legal costs?" but only 44% said "If you sue someone and lose, should you pay his costs?"

**Dawson, Gilovich, & Regan (2002):** People apply different evidentiary standards depending on whether information is favorable ("Can I believe this?" -- low bar) vs. unfavorable ("Must I believe this?" -- high bar).

**Ditto & Lopez (1992):** Participants searching for evidence that a friendly vs. unfriendly coworker was smarter. When evidence supported the friendly coworker, they stopped searching quickly. When evidence favored the jerk, they kept searching for contradictory information.

**Balcetis & Dunning (2006):** Motivated perception operates pre-consciously. Participants hoping to see a farm animal (to avoid drinking a veggie smoothie) literally could not perceive the alternative interpretation of an ambiguous horse/seal image.

**Pronin et al. (2004):** People believe they are less biased than others, even when presented with evidence of their own bias. Self-serving reasoning is often unconscious -- the most effective lies are those we tell ourselves (von Hippel & Trivers, 2011).

### Emotional Influences on Decision Making
**General mood effects:** Good mood increases reliance on heuristics (Bodenhausen et al., 1994). Sad people are more affected by anchors. Weather affects stock prices (Saunders, 1993) and life satisfaction reports (Schwarz, 2001).

**Specific emotions (Ekman, 1992; Lerner & Keltner, 2001):**
- **Fear:** Sensitizes to risk, prepares to flee, creates risk aversion
- **Anger:** Increases confidence, decreases risk perception, promotes risk seeking. Angry people underestimate health risks (Taylor et al., 2004) despite anger actually increasing cardiac risk (Williams et al., 2000)
- **Disgust:** Triggers desire to expel, reduces selling prices AND buying willingness
- **Sadness:** Triggers desire to change circumstances, increases willingness to pay (buy) AND decreases selling price

**Lerner et al. (2004):** Disgust vs. sadness, induced by unrelated film clips, bled into subsequent financial decisions. Disgust made sellers more eager to sell at lower prices. Sadness made buyers more willing to pay higher prices. Two different negative emotions produced opposite economic effects.

### Mood-Congruent Recall
People recall mood-consistent information more easily. Happy people recall happy times; depressed people cannot remember being happy. This parallels the [[availability]] heuristic. Stock market prices are higher on sunny days (Saunders, 1993).

### Regret Avoidance
**The missed flight problem:** Missing a flight that left on time (scenario a) vs. seeing it depart 2 minutes late (scenario b). Both outcomes are identical, but (b) is more upsetting because the counterfactual ("I almost made it") is more vivid.

**Medvec, Madey, & Gilovich (1995):** Olympic silver medalists appear less happy than bronze medalists. Silver = "I almost won gold" (upward counterfactual). Bronze = "I almost didn't medal at all" (downward counterfactual).

Larrick (1993): Regret avoidance distorts decisions in two ways: (1) choosing options that shield from feedback on foregone alternatives; (2) when feedback is inevitable, choosing options likely to compare favorably.

### Managing Emotional Bias
1. **Label emotions:** Simply identifying the emotion reduces its influence (Lieberman et al., 2007)
2. **Identify the source:** "So how's the weather where you are?" eliminates weather effects on life satisfaction (Schwarz & Strack, 1999)
3. **Accountability:** Requiring justification promotes System 2 thinking (Lerner & Tetlock, 1999)
4. **Institutionalize controls:** Sunstein (2002): cost-benefit analysis as a "cooling effect" on emotional policy-making
