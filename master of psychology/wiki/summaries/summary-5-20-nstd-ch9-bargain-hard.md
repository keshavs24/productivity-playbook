# Never Split the Difference Ch.9: Bargain Hard — Summary

**Authors:** [[chris-voss]], [[tahl-raz]]
**Source:** *[[5-20 - Never Split the Difference - Voss, Raz]]*, Chapter 9
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **The [[ackerman-model]] is a systematized offer-counteroffer method** that beats meeting in the middle: Set target price, open at 65%, raise to 85%, 95%, then 100% — using empathy and different ways of saying "No" between each raise. Use precise nonround numbers and throw in a nonmonetary item at the end.
2. **Three negotiator types exist** — Analyst, Accommodator, and Assertive — each with fundamentally different relationships to time, silence, and reciprocity. Identifying your counterpart's type (and your own) is essential before bargaining.
3. **The Black Swan Rule of negotiation**: Don't treat others the way you want to be treated; treat them the way they need to be treated. The "I am normal" paradox causes us to project our own style onto counterparts.
4. **Extreme anchors knock counterparts off their game** — prepare to take a punch (an extreme anchor from the other side) and have dodging tactics ready. Any response that is not an outright rejection means you have the edge.
5. **Strategic umbrage** — a well-timed expression of genuine anger directed at the proposal (not the person) can be effective, but fake anger backfires and destroys trust.
6. **No deal is better than a bad deal** — the ready-to-walk mindset prevents taking yourself hostage. Never be needy for a deal.

## Detailed Notes

### The Red 4Runner — Opening Anecdote

Voss bought his dream truck (Salsa Red Pearl Toyota 4Runner) using the techniques in this chapter:
- Sticker price: $36,000. His offer: $30,000 cash, upfront.
- Used empathy and calibrated questions instead of aggression
- "How am I supposed to do that?" in response to the full sticker price
- Praised the truck and expressed genuine love for it while firmly repeating he couldn't pay more
- The salesman made multiple trips to his manager: $34,000 → $32,500 → final acceptance at $30,000
- **Key insight**: Any response that is not an outright rejection means you have the edge

### Three Negotiator Types

Research by Voss's son Brandon consolidated behavioral profiles into three broad categories:

#### Analyst
- Methodical, diligent, not in a rush
- Self-image linked to minimizing mistakes; motto: "As much time as it takes to get it right"
- Prefer working alone; rarely show emotion; use FM DJ voice (slow, measured, cold)
- **Hypersensitive to reciprocity** — give a piece, expect a piece back within a timeframe or they disengage
- Skeptical by nature; hate surprises; research extensively
- **Silence = thinking** (not anger or invitation to talk)
- To deal with them: use clear data, avoid surprises, don't ad-lib, focus on facts
- If you ARE an Analyst: smile when you speak — you come across as distant otherwise

#### Accommodator
- Relationship is everything; time = relationship building
- Goal: be on great terms with counterpart; love the win-win
- Will yield a concession to appease; very easy to talk to; pleasant voices
- Most likely to build great rapport without accomplishing anything
- **Silence = anger** (for them)
- Their objections can be hidden out of fear of conflict — use calibrated questions about implementation
- May agree to things they can't actually deliver (reciprocity reflex)
- To deal with them: be sociable, listen to their ideas, use calibrated implementation questions
- If you ARE an Accommodator: don't sacrifice objections for likability; be conscious of excess chitchat

#### Assertive
- Time = money; every wasted minute is a wasted dollar
- Self-image linked to how many things get accomplished
- Direct, candid, love winning, aggressive communication style
- **Want to be heard** — they can't listen to you until they know you've heard them
- Focus on goals rather than people; tell rather than ask
- **Silence = you have nothing to say or you want them to talk**
- Reciprocity = "give an inch/take a mile" mentality
- To deal with them: focus on what they have to say, use mirrors/labels/summaries to get "That's right"
- If you ARE an Assertive: soften your tone intentionally; use calibrated questions and labels

#### How Types View Time and Silence Differently
- Analyst: time = preparation; silence = thinking
- Accommodator: time = relationship; silence = anger
- Assertive: time = money; silence = opportunity to talk or signal you're done

**The "I am normal" paradox**: The greatest obstacle to identifying someone else's style is our hypothesis that the world should look to others as it looks to us. With three types, there's a 66% chance your counterpart has a different style. A CEO who expected 9 of 10 negotiations to fail was projecting his Assertive style onto everyone.

### Taking a Punch — Handling Extreme Anchors

- Negotiation academics talk about ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement) — but in real bargaining, kick-ass negotiators lead with extreme anchors
- **Deflect the punch**: Say "No" with "How am I supposed to accept that?" or deflect with "What are we trying to accomplish here?"
- **Pivot to nonmonetary terms** when being dragged into a haggle
- **Let them go first** — you want to see their hand; sponge up information
- If pushed to name a price first, allude to an incredibly high number someone else might charge
- **Farouq's dean meeting**: Needed $600 for an alumni event. Dean jumped in with $300 max. Farouq used "How am I supposed to do that?", dropped an extreme anchor ($1,000), and through calibrated questions and specific numbers ($737.50), got $750.

### Punching Back: Using Assertion Without Getting Used By It

#### Real Anger, Threats Without Anger, and Strategic Umbrage
- Marwan Sinaceur (INSEAD) and Larissa Tiedens (Stanford): Expressions of anger increase a negotiator's advantage and final take
- But fake anger backfires — it leads to intractable demands and destroys trust
- For anger to be effective, it has to be real and under control — it reduces cognitive ability
- **Strategic umbrage**: Take offense at the proposal, not the person — "I don't see how that would ever work"
- Daniel Ames and Abbie Wazlawek (Columbia): People on the receiving end of strategic umbrage rate themselves as overassertive, even when the counterpart doesn't think so

#### "Why" Questions as a Weapon
- Normally avoid "Why" — it's accusatory
- But use it to get your counterpart to defend a position that favors you: "Why would you ever do business with me? Why would you ever change from your existing supplier? They're great!"
- The defensiveness coaxes them into working for you

#### "I" Messages
- "I'm sorry, that doesn't work for me" — the word "I" strategically focuses your counterpart's attention onto you
- Traditional format: "I feel ___ when you ___ because ___" — demands a time-out
- Be cool and level, not aggressive

#### No Neediness: The Ready-to-Walk Mindset
- If you can't say "No," you've taken yourself hostage
- Once clear on your bottom line, be willing to walk away
- Never be needy for a deal
- Maintain a collaborative relationship even when setting boundaries — response must be strong yet empathic, "tough love" not hatred
- **The person across the table is never the problem. The unsolved issue is.**
- Punching back is a last resort — first suggest de-escalating with a time-out

### The [[ackerman-model]] — Systematized Bargaining

Named after Mike Ackerman, ex-CIA type who founded a kidnap-for-ransom consulting company in Miami. Validated by Howard Raiffa of Harvard.

**The six steps:**

1. **Set your target price** (your goal)
2. **Set your first offer at 65%** of your target price — an extreme anchor that induces fight-or-flight, limiting cognitive abilities and pushing them into rash action
3. **Calculate three raises of decreasing increments** — to 85%, 95%, and 100% of target
4. **Use lots of empathy and different ways of saying "No"** to get the other side to counter before you increase your offer — try to bait them into bidding against themselves with calibrated questions
5. **When calculating the final amount, use precise, nonround numbers** — $37,893 rather than $38,000. This gives the number credibility and weight.
6. **On your final number, throw in a nonmonetary item** (that they probably don't want) to show you're at your limit

**Why it works psychologically:**

- The 65% opening sets an extreme anchor — a big slap that might bring counterpart right to their price limit
- The progressive increases to 85, 95, 100% play on **reciprocity** — each concession inspires a reciprocal concession
- The **diminishing size of the increases** convinces counterpart you're being squeezed to the breaking point — they feel they've gotten every last drop
- This **juices their self-esteem** — research shows people getting concessions feel better about the bargaining process even when they end up paying more
- **Nonround numbers** feel permanent and calculated ($1.99 vs $2.00)

### Haiti Kidnapping — Ackerman in Action

- Over eighteen months in Haiti, Voss got two or three kidnappings a week
- Market ransom was $15,000-$75,000 per victim; Voss aimed for under $5,000
- The very first kidnapping in the book used the Ackerman process: extreme anchor, calibrated questions, progressively smaller concessions, the "weird number" ($4,751), and the CD stereo as the nonmonetary item

### Mishary's Rent Negotiation — Complete Ackerman Case Study

Georgetown MBA student Mishary faced a rent increase from $1,850/month to $2,100 (10 months) or $2,000 (12 months):

1. **Research**: Comparable apartments were $1,800-$1,950. Target: $1,830.
2. **Emotional anchoring**: Emphasized how sad it would be for both sides if he left; what a good tenant he was
3. **"How am I supposed to pay $200 extra?"** — calibrated question
4. **Extreme anchor**: $1,730/month for a year (the agent laughed)
5. **Calibrated question**: "How do you price lease renewals?"
6. **Second offer**: $1,790/month for 12 months — agent asked for time
7. Agent came back at $1,950. Mishary said no without saying the word: "That is generous of you, but how am I supposed to accept it when I can move a few blocks away for $1,800?"
8. Used a mislabel: "It seems like you would rather run the risk of keeping the place unrented" — the agent had to correct him, opening up
9. **Third offer**: $1,810 (agent refused: "still lower than market")
10. **Final offer**: Asked for pen and paper, did fake calculations, came back with $1,829 — a brilliantly precise number
11. Agent: "Wow. $1,829. You seem very precise. You must be an accountant." Accepted.

**Result**: Got a rent discount when the landlord wanted to raise rent, using Ackerman offers, nonround numbers, deep research, smart labeling, and saying no without saying "No."
