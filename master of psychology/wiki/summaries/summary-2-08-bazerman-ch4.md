# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.4 -- Bounded Awareness

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Edition)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. [[bounded-awareness]] (Bazerman & Chugh, 2005) is the systematic failure to notice or attend to obvious, important, and readily available information -- distinct from the availability heuristic in that it focuses on domain-specific variables that are predictably out of focus
2. [[inattentional-blindness]]: Neisser (1979) and Simons & Chabris (1999) showed that people counting basketball passes failed to notice a woman with an umbrella (only 21% noticed) or a person in a gorilla costume -- demonstrating that focus on one task blinds us to unexpected but obvious events
3. [[change-blindness]]: People fail to notice obvious visual changes in their environment (Simons, 2000); gradual ethical degradation is harder to detect than sudden shifts (Gino & Bazerman, 2009) -- the "slippery slope" of unethical behavior
4. [[focalism]]: Gilbert, Wilson et al. (2000) coined the term for the tendency to focus too much on a focal event and too little on concurrent events; Schkade and Kahneman (1998) demonstrated the focusing illusion -- Californians and Midwesterners had similar life satisfaction but both groups rated Californians higher
5. Bounded awareness extends to groups (Stasser & Titus, 1985: groups discuss shared information more than unshared, even when pooling unique information is the purpose), strategic decisions (the winner's curse in auctions and acquisitions), and competitive assessment (reference group neglect)

## Detailed Notes

### The Madoff Case as Framing Example
Bernard Madoff's $64.8 billion Ponzi scheme illustrates bounded awareness at scale. Feeder fund managers, professional investors, government regulators, and investment bankers failed to notice readily available evidence that Madoff's returns were impossible. Rene-Theirry Magon de la Villehuchet was repeatedly warned but did not investigate; he killed himself two weeks after Madoff's arrest. The chapter is about our systematic and predictable failures to notice critical available information.

### Inattentional Blindness
Neisser (1979) pioneered the research: participants watching two superimposed groups of basketball players counting passes missed a woman walking through with an umbrella (only 21% noticed). Simons and Chabris (1999, 2010) replicated with a gorilla suit -- the person was visible for over five seconds, thumping its chest. Mack and Rock (1998) provided broader evidence that people have a tendency not to see what they are not looking for, even when looking directly at it. Mack (2003) noted this could cause pilots to overlook other aircraft on their runway. The phenomenon connects inattentional blindness to neural regions in the brain (C.M. Moore & Egeth, 1997).

### Change Blindness
Simons, Chabris, Schnur, and Levin (2002): a person holding a basketball asked a pedestrian for directions; during the interaction, a group walked between them and surreptitiously took the basketball -- most pedestrians did not notice. Angelone, Levin, and Simons (2003): people failed to notice clothing and object changes during camera cuts. Mitroff, Simons, and Franconeri (2002) confirmed that people fail to explicitly notice changes despite having implicit representations of pre- and post-change information. Gradual changes are harder to detect than sudden ones (Simons & Rensink, 2005). Application to ethics: the "slippery slope" -- people are more willing to accept ethical lapses that occur in small incremental steps than in one large step (Gino & Bazerman, 2009; Tenbrunsel & Messick, 2004). The boiling frog metaphor applies to ethical degradation in organizations.

### Focalism and the Focusing Illusion
Gilbert, Wilson et al. (2000; Wilson, Wheatley, Meyers, Gilbert, & Axsom, 2000) coined "focalism": people focus too much on a focal event and too little on concurrent events. Consequences: (1) overestimating how much future thoughts will be occupied by the focal event; (2) overestimating the duration of emotional response. We overestimate the impact of sports wins/losses and even major medical conditions on overall happiness (Ubel et al., 2001). Affective forecasting errors: Nelson, Meyvis, and Galak (2009) showed commercial breaks actually enhance TV enjoyment, contrary to viewers' predictions. Schkade and Kahneman (1998) -- the focusing illusion: Californians and Midwesterners reported similar life satisfaction, but both groups rated Californians higher because climate became salient when comparing. Fox and Tversky (1998) -- basketball tournament study: when probabilities were assessed at the team level, the eight teams' probabilities summed to 218% (vs. 100% expected); at the division level, 144%; at the conference level, 102%. Whatever is in focus gets overweighted. Tversky and Koehler (1994): doctors' probabilities for four mutually exclusive prognoses summed to far more than 100%. The Challenger disaster: NASA engineers only examined temperatures for seven launches with O-ring failures, missing the 17 launches without failure that would have shown a clear temperature-failure relationship.

### Bounded Awareness in Groups
Stasser and colleagues (Stasser, 1988; Stasser & Stewart, 1992; Stasser & Titus, 1985) showed a consistent tendency for groups to discuss shared information (known to all) more than unshared information (known to only one member). Stasser and Titus (1985) student council president study: with full information, 67% of individuals chose Candidate A (83% in groups). With unshared information distributed, only 23% of individuals chose A, and only 18% of groups -- the groups failed to pool their unique information. Strategies to overcome: forewarning the group about unique knowledge, identifying expertise before discussion (Stasser, Vaughn, & Stewart, 2000).

### Bounded Awareness in Strategic Settings
**Multiparty Ultimatum Games:** Messick, Moore, and Bazerman (1997) showed that the profit-maximizing strategy differs dramatically between "dividing the pie -- largest" (offer 10-10-10-10-10) and "dividing the pie -- smallest" (offer 55-1-1-1-1-1) conditions, but players' actual behavior was nearly identical across conditions ($8.47 vs. $8.15 to other players). Players fail to attend to the nuances of the rules and the likely decisions of others.

**The Monty Hall Problem:** In the "Monty always opens" condition, switching doubles your winning probability from 1/3 to 2/3. In the "Mean Monty" condition, keeping is correct. Friedman (1998) found substantial failure rates. Tor and Bazerman (2003): 41% traded doors, 59% kept. In Mean Monty, 79% correctly kept. Only 24% answered both versions correctly.

**Acquiring a Company:** Samuelson and Bazerman (1985): the rational offer is $0 per share (any positive offer yields negative expected return due to information asymmetry). Yet only 9 of 123 MBA students offered $0; the dominant response was $50-$75. Replications with accounting partners, CEOs, investment bankers produced similar results. The failure: people systematically exclude information about the opposing party's decisions and the rules of the game from their decision processes. However, face-to-face communication can overcome this (Valley, Moag, & Bazerman, 1998).

**The Winner's Curse:** Bazerman and Samuelson (1983): in auctions of uncertain-value commodities with many bidders, the winning bidder likely overpaid. The winning bid is from the right tail of the estimate distribution, meaning the winner overestimated value. Corporate takeovers: one-third of acquisitions fail, another third fail to live up to expectations, and merger synergy usually goes to the target, not the acquirer.

### Reference Group Neglect and Competition
Camerer and Lovallo (1999): people are insensitive to the quality of their competition. Moore, Oesch, and Zietsma (2007): entrepreneurs focus on their own strengths and weaknesses, not the competition -- entering easy industries (many competitors) too eagerly and hard industries (few competitors) too reluctantly. Radzevick and Moore (2007): people bet on their home team primarily when the home team is strong, regardless of competition strength.

### Understanding the Bounds of Others
Choice overload (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Schwartz, 2005): when faced with too many options (24 vs. 6 jams), people are more likely to sample but less likely to buy. The act of choosing depletes self-regulation resources (Vohs et al., 2008). Even adding inferior options can cause people not to act on preferences (Bernheim & Rangel, 2009).

### Discussion
Bazerman and Chugh (2005) coined "bounded awareness" to describe the narrowing of attention and focus in negotiation. The concept overlaps with [[availability]] but has a different focus: availability explains why vivid data is overweighted across contexts; bounded awareness identifies specific groups of variables that are predictably out of focus in particular domains.
