# Think Faster Talk Smarter Ch.3: Redefine — Mind Your Mindset — Summary

**Author:** Matt Abrahams
**Source:** *Think Faster, Talk Smarter* (2023), Chapter 3
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Four key mindset shifts** transform how we approach spontaneous communication: (1) Fixed to Growth, (2) Self-focused to Audience-focused, (3) "Yes, but" to "Yes, and," (4) Dwelling on the past to "Next Play."
2. **Reframing spontaneous communication as opportunity rather than threat** expands our focus, relaxes our bodies, makes our tone more confident, and invites a virtuous cycle where our positivity draws others in.
3. **"Yes, and..." is a central tenet of improvisation** that shifts us from critical evaluation to affirming and building upon what others say, opening up creative possibilities.
4. **"Next Play" mentality** (from Coach K at Duke) means quickly resetting after both successes and failures rather than dwelling. The Zen parable of the farmer's horse ("Maybe") captures the same wisdom.

## Detailed Notes

### Mindset Shift #1: From Fixed to Growth
- Carol Dweck's research: a [[growth-mindset]] sees intelligence and capabilities as fluid -- skills can be learned, performance improved. A fixed mindset regards them as unchangeable.
- People with growth mindsets about communication were less anxious about public speaking, perceived themselves as better at it, and thought more deeply about its impact (Stewart et al., 2019)
- **Practical steps:** Focus on effort over results, adopt a "not yet" attitude, use journey metaphors ("process," "adventure"), take notes and review progress

### Mindset Shift #2: From Self to Audience
- When we perceive communication as threatening, we focus on ourselves. Shifting focus to the audience reduces pressure.
- Patricia Ryan Madson (Stanford improv expert): "It's not about you. It's about the rest of the people here and how you can work with them."
- **Practical steps:** Survey the room before speaking, study audience mood/energy, ask "Who are they? What do they care about? What do they need from me?"
- Kathy Bonanno (Google exec) focuses on helping audience members "have a good moment" -- this relaxes her considerably
- Most audiences *want* speakers to succeed (Madson: audiences are "a gaggle of humans, just like you; they are there to cheer you on")

### Mindset Shift #3: From "Yes, But..." to "[[yes-and-technique|Yes, And...]]"
- The Gift-Giving Game: partners practice giving and receiving imaginary gifts, building on each other's contributions. Both the naming and justifying are spontaneous -- and joyful.
- "Yes, and..." is a foundational improv concept from Kelly Leonard's work. Instead of resisting or evaluating ideas, we affirm and add our own contribution.
- **Practical steps:** Seek areas of agreement, even in conflict. Use agreement as a starting point and build from there. Resist the impulse to judge hastily.
- Abrahams used "Yes, and..." to help two regional managers resolve a hiring conflict by reframing the role entirely

### Mindset Shift #4: From Dwelling to "Next Play"
- Patricia Ryan Madson's commencement story: she blew her cue (spoke too early before the orchestra finished), sat back down, waited, then delivered the line again with the audience's appreciation. Lesson: don't ruminate -- focus on what comes next.
- Coach K (Mike Krzyzewski): "Whatever you have just done is not nearly as important as what you are doing right now." Shane Battier called it "the most simple but eloquent lesson."
- **"New Choice" improv game:** Performers must drop their current choice and make a new one on command -- trains comfort with simply moving on.
- **The Zen Parable of the Farmer:** Horse escapes ("Maybe"), wild horses return ("Maybe"), son breaks leg ("Maybe"), son avoids military draft ("Maybe"). Suspend judgment on outcomes and proceed to the next play.

### Finding the Upside in the Downside
Abrahams tells of having to lay off a quarter of his team. By reframing the conversations as opportunities (growth mindset), focusing on employees' needs (audience focus), building on what they said ("Yes, and..."), and pushing forward (next play), these painful conversations became among the most productive he'd ever had. Several employees thanked him afterward.
