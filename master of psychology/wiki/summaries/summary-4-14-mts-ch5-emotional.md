# Made to Stick: Ch.5 Emotional -- Summary

**Author(s):** Chip Heath, Dan Heath
**Type:** Book chapter (Ch. 5, pp. 176-214)
**Phase:** 4 -- Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **The [[mother-teresa-principle]]: "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will."** In the Carnegie Mellon charity study, people who read about a single girl (Rokia) donated $2.38, while those who read statistics about millions suffering donated only $1.14. Most strikingly, people who received both the statistics and the individual story donated $1.43 -- the mere act of analytical thinking reduced charitable giving. Once we put on our "Analytical Hat," we react to emotional appeals differently.
2. **Semantic stretch erodes the emotional power of words and concepts.** When associations are overused, they lose their punch -- "unique" no longer means one of a kind, "sportsmanship" became a consolation prize for losers. To make people care, we must either find fresh associations or, like Jim Thompson's "Honoring the Game," create new language that recaptures the original emotional power.
3. **The [[wiify-principle]] (What's In It For You) should be central to every appeal, but self-interest is only part of the story.** The Tempe cable TV study showed that homeowners who imagined themselves using cable subscribed at 47% vs. 20% for those who merely heard about benefits. The tangibility of the benefit, not its magnitude, drives interest.
4. **People systematically underestimate the power of higher-level motivations.** Research shows people are personally motivated by Esteem and Learning (Maslow's upper tiers), but they predict that others are motivated by Security and Physical needs (Maslow's basement). This mismatch causes communicators to focus on the wrong appeals.
5. **Identity-based appeals can be more powerful than self-interest appeals.** James March's identity model of decision-making asks: "Who am I? What kind of situation is this? What would someone like me do?" The "Don't Mess with Texas" campaign reduced litter by 72% by appealing to Texan identity -- real Texans don't litter -- not by threatening fines or appealing to environmental guilt.

## Detailed Notes

### The [[mother-teresa-principle]] -- Feeling for the One

- Mother Teresa: "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will"
- **Carnegie Mellon Save the Children study (2004):** Two versions of a donation request letter:
  - Version 1 (statistics): Food shortages in Malawi affecting 3 million children; 4 million Angolans displaced; 11 million Ethiopians need food aid -- average donation: $1.14
  - Version 2 (individual): "Any money you donate will go to Rokia, a seven-year-old girl from Mali" -- average donation: $2.38 (more than double)
  - Version 3 (both): Statistics AND Rokia's story -- average donation: $1.43 (a dollar less than Rokia alone)
- The researchers theorized that statistics shift people into an analytical frame of mind, which suppresses emotional response
- A follow-up study confirmed this: people primed with analytical questions ("if an object travels at five feet per minute...") gave $1.26 to Rokia, while those primed with emotional questions ("describe how you feel when you hear the word 'baby'") gave $2.34
- **The mere act of calculation reduced people's charity.** Once we put on our Analytical Hat, we react to emotional appeals differently. We hinder our ability to feel
- Charities have long exploited this: you sponsor a specific child, not "African poverty." Farm Sanctuary lets donors "adopt a chicken" ($10/month), a goat ($25), or a cow ($50)
- No one wants to donate to the "General Administrative Fund" -- the abstraction kills the emotion

### Belief vs. Care -- The Gap

- Everyone believes there is tremendous human suffering in Africa -- no credibility problem. But belief does not necessarily make people care enough to act
- Everyone believes fatty food leads to health problems, but belief alone does not change behavior
- For people to take action, they have to care. Feelings inspire people to act
- This chapter is not about pushing emotional buttons like a tearjerker -- it is about making people care. The goal of making messages "emotional" is to make people care

### The Truth Campaign vs. "Think. Don't Smoke."

- The American Legacy Foundation's Truth campaign (1998) showed teens piling body bags outside tobacco company headquarters, revealing that 1,800 people die daily from tobacco
- Philip Morris's own antismoking campaign: "Think. Don't Smoke."
- Results: Truth was spontaneously recalled by 22% of teens vs. 3% for Think. Those exposed to Truth were 66% less likely to smoke in the next year. Those exposed to "Think. Don't Smoke." were 36% MORE likely to smoke
- In Florida, the Truth campaign reduced teen smoking by 18% (high school) and 40% (middle school) in two years
- **Why the difference?** "Think. Don't Smoke." put on the Analytical Hat. The Truth campaign tapped into anti-authority resentment -- the classic teenage emotion. Teens who once smoked to rebel against The Man now rebel against The Man (Big Tobacco) by NOT smoking
- The Truth campaign is not about rational decision-making; it is about rebellion

### Semantic Stretch and the Power of Association

- The most basic way to make people care is to form an association between something they do not yet care about and something they do care about
- **Semantic stretch:** When terms are overused for their emotional associations, they lose their power. "Relativity" became a color on the emotional palette. "Unique" went from meaning one-of-a-kind to merely unusual (73% increase in newspaper usage over 20 years while "unusual" declined)
- The superlatives of each generation (groovy, awesome, cool, phat) fade because they have been associated with too many things
- This arms race creates problems for communicators -- when everyone taps into the same emotional association, it loses its punch

### Fighting Semantic Stretch: "Sportsmanship" to "Honoring the Game"

- Jim Thompson, founder of the Positive Coaching Alliance, recognized that "sportsmanship" had been stretched beyond usefulness -- it evoked consolation prizes for losers, not genuine excellence
- Thompson reframed the concept as "Honoring the Game" -- a way to express sports patriotism, implying you owe your sport basic respect
- Lance Armstrong slowing down for a crashed Jan Ullrich was not just being a "good sport" -- he was Honoring the Game
- Results: Technical fouls dropped from every 15 games to every 52 games. A baseball league saw a 90% reduction in ejections after Positive Coaching training
- **Lesson:** When an emotional association is worn out, shift to new language rather than trying to resurrect the old term

### Appealing to Self-Interest -- The [[wiify-principle]]

- John Caples, the greatest direct-mail copywriter: "First and foremost, try to get self-interest into every headline you write"
- Famous headline: "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano... But When I Started to Play!" -- a classic underdog story in fifteen words
- Caples's key insight: companies emphasize features when they should emphasize benefits. People do not buy quarter-inch drill bits; they buy quarter-inch holes so they can hang their children's pictures
- Jerry Weissman (CEO speech coach): WIIFY -- "what's in it for you" -- should be central to every speech. Do not bury it or dance around it
- Keep the "self" in self-interest: "Don't say, 'People will enjoy a sense of security when they use Goodyear Tires.' Say, 'You enjoy a sense of security when you use Goodyear Tires.'"

### Cable TV in Tempe -- Imagining the Self

- **1982 Arizona study:** Homeowners received two versions of cable TV benefits:
  - Group 1: Abstract third-person description ("a person can plan in advance...")
  - Group 2: Imaginative second-person scenario ("Take a moment and imagine how CATV will provide you with...")
- Group 1 subscribed at 20% (same as the general neighborhood). Group 2 subscribed at 47%
- The research paper was subtitled "Does Imagining Make It So?" Yes -- the tangibility of the benefit, not its magnitude, drives interest
- People must be able to easily imagine themselves enjoying the benefit

### Maslow's Hierarchy Applied

- Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs: Physical, Security, Belonging, Esteem, Learning, Aesthetic, Self-actualization, Transcendence
- The hierarchical aspect is largely bogus -- people pursue multiple needs simultaneously (starving artists exist)
- When people talk about "self-interest," they typically invoke Physical, Security, and Esteem layers. But research shows people are personally most motivated by Esteem (No. 3) and Learning (No. 3 for job appeals), while they predict others are motivated by Security (No. 1) and Physical needs (No. 2)
- **We all think we live in the penthouse of Maslow's pyramid, but everyone else lives in the basement.** This mismatch causes us to overlook opportunities to motivate people
- **Floyd Lee and the Pegasus mess hall:** Lee ran a mess hall near Baghdad airport that became legendary. Same Army menu, same suppliers. But Lee defined his mission as "I am not just in charge of food service; I am in charge of morale." He was aiming for Transcendence on Maslow's hierarchy. A soldier said: "The time you are in here, you forget you're in Iraq"

### The Popcorn Popper and Political Science -- Self-Interest vs. Identity

- A marketer offered firefighters free popcorn poppers for reviewing an educational fire safety film. The firefighters were furious: "Do you think we'd use a fire safety program because of some popcorn popper?!" The offer violated their sense of identity
- **Donald Kinder (University of Michigan):** The effect of self-interest on political views is "trifling." People support policies based on group interest and identity, not personal gain. The unemployed are no more likely to support government health insurance than the fully insured
- **James March's two decision-making models:**
  - Model 1 (consequences): Weigh alternatives, choose what yields most value -- the economist's model
  - Model 2 (identity): Ask "Who am I? What kind of situation is this? What do people like me do in this kind of situation?" -- no calculations, only norms and principles
- **"Don't Mess with Texas" campaign:** Dan Syrek identified that typical Texas litterers were 18-35-year-old pickup-driving males ("Bubba"). Appeals to guilt or fear would not work. Instead, the campaign showed Texas celebrities (Dallas Cowboys, Willie Nelson) picking up trash -- establishing that "Texans don't litter." Within a year, litter declined 29%. Within five years, visible roadside litter decreased 72% and cans dropped 81%
- The campaign succeeded because it offered Bubba a compelling identity message, not a rational self-interest appeal

### The Music of Duo Piano -- Fighting the Curse of Knowledge

- The Murray Dranoff Duo Piano Foundation struggled to articulate why their art form mattered: "We exist to protect, preserve, and promote the music of duo piano"
- After being pushed with "Why?" three times, they arrived at: "When you put two of these magnificent instruments in the same room, and the performers can respond to each other and build on each other, it's like having the sound of the orchestra but the intimacy of chamber music"
- This breakthrough -- the "Three Whys" technique (derived from Toyota's "Five Whys") -- moved from abstract what to emotional why
- The Curse of Knowledge prevented the experts from expressing what they inherently knew to be true

### Creating Empathy -- IDEO's Hospital Video

- IDEO created a video from the patient's perspective: ceiling tiles, disembodied voices, long waits. When hospital staff saw it, the reaction was always "Oh, I never realized..."
- Before the video, the problem was not quite real. Afterward: "There's an immediate motivation to fix things"
- IDEO also created role-playing exercises: "Imagine you are French and trying to locate your father in the hospital, but you don't speak any English"
- The world of business tends to emphasize the pattern over the particular. The intellectual aspects of the pattern prevent people from caring

### Chapter Summary

- How to make people care: (1) Take off Analytical Hats -- avoid triggering analytical thinking that suppresses emotional response, (2) Create empathy for specific individuals, not abstractions, (3) Show how ideas are associated with things people already care about, (4) Appeal to self-interest -- but also to identity, (5) Get out of Maslow's Basement -- appeal to Esteem, Learning, Self-actualization, and Transcendence, not just physical and security needs
