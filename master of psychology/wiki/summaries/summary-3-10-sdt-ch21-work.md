# Work and Organizations: Promoting Wellness and Productivity -- Summary

**Author(s):** Richard M. Ryan, Edward L. Deci
**Type:** Book chapter (Ch. 21)
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Work is the dominant domain of adult life, yet most employees experience lower well-being during work hours than during non-work hours** -- explained by lower satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs at work (Ryan, Bernstein, & Brown, 2010)
2. **Autonomous motivation (intrinsic + well-internalized extrinsic) is necessary and essential for high-quality engagement and performance**, even when controlling motives are also present (Moran, Diefendorff, Kim, & Liu, 2012)
3. **Managers can be trained to become more autonomy-supportive**, and this training radiates positive effects to their subordinates' motivation, satisfaction, and trust in the organization (Deci, Connell, & Ryan, 1989; Hardre & Reeve, 2009)
4. **Pay level does not predict basic need satisfaction, intrinsic motivation, or procedural justice** -- managerial autonomy support does (Olafsen, Halvari, Forest, & Deci, 2015)
5. **Performance-contingent pay undermines autonomous motivation and increases turnover** compared to base-pay plans (Kuvaas et al., 2016; Harrison, Virick, & William, 1996)
6. **Need satisfaction is the universal mediating mechanism** linking managerial and organizational practices to employee outcomes -- demonstrated across shoe factories, psychiatric workshops, UN agencies, banks, business schools, volunteers, and post-communist economies
7. **Organizations with higher "Total Motivation" (ToMo) scores** -- essentially SDT's relative autonomy index in corporate language -- outperform competitors (Doshi & McGregor, 2015: Starbucks, Southwest Airlines, Whole Foods, Nordstrom)

## Detailed Notes

### The Problem: Work and Well-Being

Ryan, Bernstein, and Brown (2010) used experience-sampling with US employees across varied jobs (construction workers, secretaries, lawyers, nurses) assessed three times daily for 21 consecutive days. Findings:
- Employees reported **lower well-being during working hours** than non-working hours (higher negative affect, lower positive affect, lower vitality, higher physical symptoms)
- On weekdays vs. weekends: more negative affect, less positive affect on workdays
- Results explained by **lower satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness** during work hours
- Most people at work feel less empowered and less connected than in the rest of their lives

### Autonomy and Need Satisfaction in Modern Organizations

The nature of information-age work **requires** committed, engaged, flexible, proactive employees. Having workers be more self-motivated turns out to be better for organizational profitability and effectiveness (Deci, Olafsen, & Ryan, 2017).

**ToMo (Total Motivation) -- Doshi & McGregor (2015):**
- ToMo scores weight intrinsic motivation and identification positively, introjection and external regulation negatively -- essentially SDT's [[relative-autonomy-index]]
- Companies like Starbucks, Southwest Airlines, Whole Foods, and Nordstrom show substantially higher ToMo than competitors
- These "magical" corporate cultures have greater relative autonomy as their distinguishing feature

**The vicious cycle of control:** When managers rely on carrots and sticks, employees focus only on those incentives, requiring even more top-down monitoring and control. SDT points to pathways out of this cycle.

### Historical Roots: Not All New

- **Argyris (1957) and McGregor (1960):** Recognized that autonomous motivation and self-actualization were essential to management. McGregor's "Theory Y" resonates with SDT principles
- **Vroom (1964) -- Expectancy-Valence Theory:** People are motivated by behaviors they expect will yield high-valence outcomes. But the concept of valence is undifferentiated; intrinsic and extrinsic satisfactions are considered additive (SDT says they interact, not add)
- **Porter and Lawler (1968):** Combined Theory Y with performance-contingent rewards. Proposed (1) make work interesting through job enlargement and (2) tie extrinsic rewards to performance. Still assumed additivity
- **CET's entrance:** Deci (1971, 1972a) showed contingent monetary rewards decreased intrinsic motivation. CET became one of seven classic theories of work motivation (Ambrose & Kulik, 1999). But CET alone was insufficient -- [[organismic-integration-theory]] was needed to address the full spectrum of extrinsic motivation at work

### The SDT Approach to Work

CET, OIT, and [[basic-psychological-needs-theory]] together form the SDT approach to work motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2014b; Gagne, Deci, & Ryan, 2017):
- **Optimal motivation = autonomous motivation** = [[intrinsic-motivation]] + well-internalized [[extrinsic-motivation]]
- This depends on satisfaction of basic psychological needs
- Social-contextual conditions that facilitate both types of autonomous motivation are highly similar: **choice, voice, meaningful competence feedback, perceived equity and inclusion**
- These conditions promote satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs, which enhance engagement, improve performance, promote psychological wellness, and reduce ill-being

### Empirical Explorations of Motivation at Work

#### The Deci, Connell, & Ryan (1989) Fortune 500 Intervention

The first large-scale SDT field study. A Fortune 500 company with nationwide offices, each with its own branch manager. Employee motivation was low.

**Intervention design -- Three core concepts of [[autonomy-supportive-management]]:**
1. **Understanding others' perspectives** -- managers trained in perspective-taking during team meetings
2. **Informational feedback** -- trained to give positive, supportive feedback instead of negative/critical; learned to "hear" feedback from subordinates
3. **Supporting initiative, choice, and participation** -- supporting subordinates' initiative in decisions

**Results across four findings:**
1. Manager autonomy support correlated with subordinates' positive perceptions and satisfactions, growing stronger over time
2. Experimental-group managers became more autonomy-supportive relative to controls during the intervention
3. Over 13 months, positive changes in subordinates' perceptions and satisfactions in intervention branches; most autonomy-supportive managers had subordinates with the most positive changes
4. Company attitude surveys showed significant positive change in intervention branches vs. control branches

**ROI analysis (Forest, Gilbert, Beaulieu, Le Brock, & Gagne, 2014):** Calculated the mental health savings from the Deci et al. (1989) intervention. **Return on investment would be more than 3:1** -- the intervention not only improves well-being but provides substantial organizational savings.

#### Hardre & Reeve (2009) -- Manager Training Replication

Randomly assigned Fortune 500 managers to autonomy-support training vs. control. Five weeks post-training:
- Trained managers were significantly more autonomy-supportive
- Five weeks later, their employees were **significantly more autonomous and engaged** than employees of untrained managers

### Autonomous Motivation at Work

SDT predicts more autonomous motivation = higher quality engagement + less exhaustion/burnout. When work requires energy, care, thought, or creativity, **autonomy is expected to be associated with higher performance.**

**Key studies:**

- **Fernet, Austin, & Vallerand (2012):** Over 9 months, autonomously motivated employees showed greater job commitment and less emotional exhaustion; controlled motivation predicted great emotional exhaustion. Those with greater job resources reported increases in autonomous motivation over time
- **Otis & Pelletier (2005):** French-speaking Canadian police officers who perceived supervisors as highly autonomy-supportive displayed more autonomous work motivation, fewer daily hassles, lower physical symptoms, and stronger intentions to remain in their jobs
- **Levesque, Blais, & Hess (2004):** Teachers in the African state of Gambia whose supervisors were more supportive of basic psychological needs were more autonomously motivated, reported greater job satisfaction and life satisfaction
- **Nie, Chua, Yeung, Ryan, & Chan (2015):** Similar results with Chinese government-sponsored school teachers -- autonomy is beneficial across diverse cultural settings
- **Fernet, Guay, Senecal, & Austin (2012):** 800+ public school teachers -- greater job pressure diminished autonomous motivation, leading to greater emotional exhaustion
- **Trepanier, Fernet, & Austin (2013b):** Nurses who experienced psychological harassment (being shouted at, aggressively monitored) showed less autonomous and more controlled motivation, lower psychological wellness, and higher turnover intentions
- **Trepanier, Fernet, & Austin (2013a):** Workplace bullying led to greater burnout and less engagement, with need frustration as the mediator
- **Preenen, Oeij, Dhondt, Kraan, & Jansen (2016):** 3,000+ Netherlands companies -- job autonomy related to revenue growth was moderated by company maturity; the relation was stronger and significant for young companies

### Coping with Change in Organizations

- **Gagne, Koestner, & Zuckerman (2000):** If organizational change is introduced autonomy-supportively (clear rationale, some choice, acknowledgment of feelings), employees are less likely to resist (Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, & Leone, 1994)
- **Gagne et al. (2000) -- Telecommunications study:** During disruptive organizational change (including downsizing), employees who experienced greater autonomy support from managers at Time 1 were more accepting of the change at Time 2, controlling for Time 1 acceptance scores
- **Mitchel, Gagne, Beaudry, & Dyer (2012):** When employees perceived managers as more responsive/supportive and perceived organizational rewards as just and fair, they were more autonomously motivated to use technology. Managerial support accounted for enjoyment and acceptance; intrinsic motivation mediated the relation
- **Lynch, Plant, & Ryan (2005):** Psychiatric hospital undergoing major change. Administrators introduced changes in ways that supported staff basic psychological needs. Staff who experienced greater need support internalized the value of the new program, were more autonomously motivated, reported higher job satisfaction, more positive attitudes, and higher well-being. **As staff became less controlling, patients perceived staff as more autonomy-supportive, and patients displayed more autonomous motivation for treatment**
- **Fernet, Guay, & Senecal (2004):** Decisional control on the job made a positive difference only for people high in autonomous motivation; those people also showed low levels of burnout

### Relations to Other Organizational Concepts

#### Proactivity
- Proactive behavior (self-directed, innovative, initiating) is one of SDT's basic meta-theoretical assumptions; intrinsic motivation and engagement are manifestations of people's inherent proactive nature
- **Parker, Williams, & Turner (2006):** Autonomy, trust, and support are important antecedents of proactive work behavior
- **Grant, Nurmohamed, Ashford, & Dekas (2011):** High personal initiative combined with high autonomous and low controlled motivation fostered proactive behaviors. Job applicants received more offers and call center employees generated more revenue with these characteristics
- **White (2015):** Customer satisfaction in a service industry was substantially accounted for by employees' autonomous motivation -- accounting for more than half the variance in positive emotions and quality of service

#### Organizational Commitment
- **Gagne, Chemolli, Forest, & Koestner (2008):** Affective commitment correlated strongly with autonomous types of motivation across three organizational samples
- **Gagne & Koestner (2002):** Identified and internalized motivation correlated with autonomous motivation, less with introjection, not at all with external regulation. Cross-lag: Time 1 RAI predicted Time 2 commitment, but Time 1 commitment did not predict Time 2 RAI -- **autonomous motivation is a base for commitment, not the other way around**
- **Becker, Kernan, Clark, & Klein (2015):** Commitment to profession was positively related to intrinsic motivation for research; commitment to university was negatively related to intrinsic motivation and positively related to extrinsic motivation. Intrinsic motivation predicted high research goals, strong goal commitment, and more hours spent on research

#### Job Characteristics
- **Hackman & Oldham (1980):** Job characteristics model -- autonomy, feedback, and task significance enhance self-motivation and performance
- **Gagne, Senecal, & Koestner (1997):** Facilitative job characteristics predicted feeling empowered, leading to enhanced intrinsic motivation
- **Millette & Gagne (2008):** Volunteer workers with high levels of facilitative job characteristics showed high autonomous motivation (high RAI), which related to job satisfaction and work performance
- **Kuvaas (2009):** Job characteristics of work autonomy and work interdependence both predicted work performance; intrinsic motivation partially mediated these relations. Supervisors' support for autonomy, competence, and development predicted performance; intrinsic motivation fully mediated this
- **Job demands research:** Satisfaction of three basic needs mediated the link from job demands to exhaustion/depersonalization and from job resources to engagement (Fernet, Austin, Trepanier, & Dussault, 2013). Van den Broeck, Vansteenkiste, De Witte, and Lens (2008) found need satisfaction partially explained links from demands to exhaustion and fully mediated the relation between low resources and exhaustion
- **Goodboy, Martin, Knight, & Long (2017):** High job demands + low worker control + poor managerial need support = workplace bullying

### Extrinsic and Intrinsic Values, Goals, and Aspirations

Drawing on [[goal-contents-theory]]:

- **Vansteenkiste, Neyrinck, Niemiec, Soenens, De Witte, & Van den Broeck (2007) -- Study 1:** Nearly 900 Belgian workers -- those with high extrinsic relative to intrinsic work values were less satisfied with jobs and less happy. Higher salary made people more satisfied, but income did **not** moderate the negative relation of extrinsic goals to satisfaction/happiness -- even well-paid extrinsically oriented people were less satisfied than intrinsically oriented ones
- **Vansteenkiste, Neyrinck, et al. (2007) -- Study 2:** Belgian employees -- higher extrinsic relative to intrinsic life goals strongly predicted poor outcomes (work-family conflict, emotional exhaustion, turnover intentions). Basic psychological need satisfaction mediated each relation
- **Roche & Haar (2013):** Employees higher on intrinsic goals engaged in more organizational citizenship behaviors
- **Van den Broeck, Vansteenkiste, Lens, & De Witte (2010):** Extrinsic work goals negatively associated with job flexibility; intrinsic work goals positively associated
- **Schreurs, van Emmerik, Van den Broeck, & Guenter (2014):** SDT-based work values -- when work teams endorsed intrinsic values, employees were more engaged; partially mediated by need satisfaction
- **Deckop, Jurkiewicz, & Giacalone (2010):** Materialism negatively related to personal well-being and to job satisfaction
- **Grant (2008):** Task significance as a strong motivator of performance; it facilitates satisfaction of relatedness and competence needs. Enhancements to task significance are facilitated by **direct contact with beneficiaries** of one's work (Grant, 2007; Grant, Campbell, Chen, Cottone, Lapedis, & Lee, 2007)

### The Role of Pay in Work Organizations

#### The Core Problem
Pay has a **functional significance** that affects need satisfaction in ways that determine work quality, not just quantity. The old idea that more pay = more effort is too simplistic, especially for work beyond easily measured outputs. Principal-agent theory (Petersen, 1993) assumes employees will maximize incentive extraction -- but extrinsic incentives do not always foster high-quality motivation and may undermine it.

#### Workplace Undermining Evidence
- **Kuvaas, Dysvik, & Buch (2014):** In gas stations and financial organizations, employees' extrinsic motivation (working for money) and intrinsic motivation (working for interest/enjoyment) were **negatively correlated** -- field support for the [[undermining-effect]]
- **Frey (1993):** Rewards affect intrinsic motivation negatively if they undermine autonomy, fairness, or recognition; undermining in one domain may "spill over" to generalize an extrinsic focus and lower autonomy across other work tasks
- **Gubler, Larkin, & Pierce (2016) -- "Motivational spillovers":** An attendance award program at an industrial laundry plant had direct positive effects on attendance of targeted workers, but:
  - Was strategically gamed by workers
  - Did not lead to internalization or lasting attendance improvement
  - **Negatively affected internal motivation of previously excellent attenders** -- those with good records reduced punctuality during ineligibility periods
  - **"Crowded out" motivation and performance in tasks not targeted by the program** -- previously above-average employees lost efficiency in daily laundry tasks once the program began

#### The SDT Perspective on Pay: Functional Significance

Not all rewards undermine. Key distinctions from [[cognitive-evaluation-theory]]:
- **Task-noncontingent rewards** (Deci, 1972a), **unexpected rewards** (Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett, 1973), **non-salient rewards** (Ross, 1975): not detrimental to intrinsic motivation
- **Performance-contingent rewards in autonomy-supportive context** (Ryan, Mims, & Koestner, 1983): tended to **enhance** intrinsic motivation (conveyed competence informationally)
- **Same performance-contingent rewards given controllingly:** undermined intrinsic motivation
- The concept of **functional significance** -- events are meaningful as either informational or controlling inputs. Compensation takes on functional significance for each individual; it is that significance (not the dollar amount) that determines motivational impact
- **Positive feedback** can enhance intrinsic motivation by affirming competence (Deci et al., 1999 meta-analysis). In workplaces, people often experience pay as reflective of competence. But when employers "dangle the carrot," controlling significance is heightened, detracting from the informational value

#### Motivation and Performance
- **Cerasoli, Nicklin, & Ford (2014) meta-analysis:** Intrinsic motivation was a moderate to strong predictor of performance. With performance-contingent rewards, there was evidence of the undermining effect. Intrinsic motivation was a stronger predictor for heuristic tasks (quality matters); extrinsic rewards were better predictors for algorithmic tasks (quantity matters)
- **Weibel, Rost, & Osterloh (2010) meta-analysis (public sector):** Pay-for-performance improved performance on simple tasks but **impaired performance on more complex tasks**
- **Kuvaas, Buch, Gagne, Dysvik, & Forest (2017):** Internalization is most likely when pay is **not directly linked to performance**

#### Reward Choice
- **Caza, McCarter, & Northcraft (2015):** Giving employees significant choice about their compensation package ("reward choice") enhanced productivity -- but only when the options were attractive to employees

#### Use of Rewards in Organizations
- **Olafsen, Halvari, Forest, & Deci (2015):** Level of pay related to distributive justice, but **pay level did not relate to procedural justice, need satisfaction, or intrinsic motivation**. In contrast, **autonomy support was strongly related to need satisfaction, intrinsic motivation, and procedural justice**
- **Kuvaas et al. (2016) -- Norwegian insurance companies:** Base-pay (not contingent on performance) positively predicted autonomous motivation and was unrelated to controlled motivation. Pay-for-performance plans **negatively predicted autonomous motivation and positively predicted controlled motivation**. Autonomous motivation strongly predicted effort and negatively predicted desire to leave; controlled motivation weakly predicted effort and positively predicted desire to leave. Performance-contingent pay led to **controlled rather than autonomous motivation**, resulting in less work effort and greater turnover intentions
- **Harrison, Virick, & William (1996):** Sales employees working entirely on commission -- three-quarters left within a year. Lower performers left sooner, but turnover was extremely high for all

#### Summary of Pay and Motivation (SDT's Four Principles for Compensation)

1. **Pay should support rather than undermine psychological incentives.** Strategies of pay should be informed by functional significance. Making controlling aspects salient (emphasizing contingency between behavior and pay) is likely to undermine autonomous motivation
2. **Performance-contingent compensation has high risk of being experienced as controlling.** Meta-analyses confirm performance-contingent is the most detrimental reward contingency (Deci et al., 1999). Field studies confirm (Kuvaas, 2006; Kuvaas et al., 2016). Fixed pay has been argued to be more effective (Frey & Osterloh, 2005)
3. **Pay needs to be equitable** -- high in distributive and procedural justice (Colquitt & Greenberg, 2003). Performance is not the only input to equity; education, training, tenure, quality, and being a team player all matter. Effective managers determine equity and communicate it need-supportively
4. **Managers supporting basic needs is more important and effective than using pay as a motivator.** Pay as a tool of control creates a vicious cycle

#### A Note on CEO Pay

- CEO stock-option bonuses are **outcome-focused rewards** -- they reinforce any and all routes to the target (stock price), often fueling the shortest route with "considerable collateral damage" (Ryan & Brown, 2005; Deci & Ryan, 2013b)
- Results of studies on CEO outcome-focused pay are negative or mixed (Cooper, Gulen, & Rau, 2014; Hou, Priem, & Goranova, 2017)
- **Enron** is the paradigmatic case of abused contingency-based rewards leading to global financial consequences (Ryan & Brown, 2005; Schilit & Perler, 2002)
- A focus only on stockholder interest leads to short-term profit maximization via outcome-focused rewards; long-term organizational health requires thinking about the "whole psychology" of rewards, not just their economics

### The SDT Approach to Management

Autonomy-supportive management training aims include (Hardre & Reeve, 2009):
1. Take employees' perspectives; understand their feelings and beliefs without becoming controlling
2. Provide greater choice and inputs to decision making
3. Understand that turning to rewards and sanctions is often maladaptive
4. Learn to give more informational positive feedback and support competence experiences
5. Conceptualize "negative feedback" as supporting employees to find meaningful solutions for ineffective behaviors
6. Encourage exploration and self-initiation
7. Understand how communications and language affect motivation, performance, and well-being (providing rationale; minimizing controlling language)

**Facilitative external events (implemented whenever reasonable):** Informational feedback, supplying rationales, making work optimally challenging, acknowledging feelings and opinions, encouraging choice and participative decision making.

#### Goal Setting and Performance Evaluations
- Participative goal setting facilitates internalization and commitment
- When employees have been involved in setting goals, performance evaluations become easier -- they can reflect on shared goals
- A problem-solving approach to barriers is both effectance-relevant and positively motivating
- The supervisor's autonomy-supportiveness and responsiveness in the evaluation conversation matters

#### Transformational Leadership
- **Burns (1978):** Transformational leaders use charisma, inspiration, stimulation, problem solving, and individual consideration; contrasted with transactional leaders who use contingent rewards, norms, and problem detection
- **Gozukara & Simsek (2015a):** Transformational leaders would in theory facilitate basic need satisfaction; Burns (1978) portrayed them as seeking "to satisfy higher needs and engage the full potential of the follower"
- **Gozukara & Simsek (2015b):** Job autonomy fully mediated the effect of transformational leadership on work engagement (Turkish academic personnel)
- **Bono & Judge (2003):** Followers of transformational leaders adopted more autonomous work goals, were more affectively committed, and displayed higher job satisfaction
- **Kovjanic, Schuh, Jonas, Van Quaquebeke, & Van Dick (2012):** Transformational leadership associated with greater job satisfaction and commitment; mediated by basic psychological need satisfactions
- **Kovjanic, Schuh, & Jonas (2013):** Experimental setting -- brainstorming task led by transformational vs. nontransformational leader. All three basic needs associated with more positive outcomes; competence and relatedness most strongly mediated between leadership type and performance
- Transformational leaders themselves require support: **Trepanier, Fernet, & Austin (2012):** School principals who were more transformational also experienced more interpersonal support, more autonomous motivation, and perceived themselves as more competent
- **Hetland, Hetland, Andreassen, Pallesen, & Notelaers (2011):** Transformational leader behaviors associated with higher satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs

### Basic Need Supports in Organizations

Highly effective organizations are those where basic psychological needs are satisfied, workers autonomously engage, feel respected, and fairly compensated (Meyer & Gagne, 2008; Shuck, Zigarmi, & Owen, 2015). Effects are apparent across diverse companies, job types, and cultures.

#### Work in a Shoe Factory
- **Ilardi, Leone, Kasser, & Ryan (1993):** Manufacturing facility -- need satisfaction predicted general job satisfaction, satisfaction with specific work tasks, self-esteem, and general health symptoms, **even after controlling for level of pay and job status**. Confirmed by both self-reports and supervisor ratings

#### Adults in a Sheltered Workshop
- **Kasser, Davey, & Ryan (1992):** Psychiatric outpatients with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder in a sheltered workshop -- satisfaction of competence, autonomy, and relatedness needs predicted work adjustment indicators (hours worked, pay earned, readiness for competitive employment), whether rated by patients or supervisors. Demonstrates generalizability beyond conventional work settings

#### Work for the United Nations
- **Roca & Gagne (2008):** UN international agency employees in e-learning -- perceived competence predicted usefulness and ease; perceived autonomy support and relatedness predicted playfulness and enjoyment; usefulness and enjoyment predicted intentions to continue learning

#### Business School Alumni
- **Richer, Blanchard, & Vallerand (2002):** Nearly 500 Canadian business school alumni -- perceived competence and autonomy were associated with perceived relatedness; perceived autonomy positively predicted job satisfaction and negatively predicted emotional exhaustion. Job satisfaction negatively predicted turnover intentions; emotional exhaustion positively predicted turnover. One year later, turnover intentions at Time 1 significantly predicted actual turnover -- **full SEM from motivation through satisfaction/exhaustion through turnover intentions to actual turnover was supported**

#### American Bankers
- **Baard, Deci, & Ryan (2004):** Banking employees -- both autonomy orientation (trait) and perceived autonomy support (from managers) predicted basic need satisfaction; need satisfaction predicted both work performance and well-being (composite of low anxiety, low physical symptoms, high vitality). **Even in banking**, basic need satisfaction is central
- Women in this banking sample perceived managers as less autonomy-supportive, felt marginally less relatedness satisfaction, received poorer evaluations, and displayed poorer adjustment -- consistent with Sandberg (2014) on underrepresentation of women's voices at work

#### Volunteers in a Not-for-Profit Organization
- **Gagne (2003):** Autonomy orientation + perceived autonomy support predicted need satisfaction and work engagement (hours volunteered, self-reported engagement). A direct path from autonomy orientation to work engagement suggests autonomy-oriented individuals are most likely to be engaged in volunteer work

#### Need Satisfaction in the Eastern Bloc
- **Deci, Ryan, Gagne, Leone, Usunov, & Kornazheva (2001):** Cross-cultural study in Bulgaria and the US in 10 state-owned companies (during central-planning economy). The model -- perceived autonomy support from managers predicts need satisfaction, which predicts work engagement and self-esteem positively and anxiety negatively -- **was largely invariant across these two very different countries**

### Autonomous Unemployment? Searching or Not Searching for Work

- **Vansteenkiste, Lens, De Witte, & Feather (2005):** 400+ unemployed Belgians -- the value of having a job predicted job search intensity but also predicted feeling worthless, isolated, and less satisfied with life. When motivation to find a job was more autonomous, individuals showed greater search intensity and were more energized. Controlled motivation to seek employment predicted stronger negative feelings and lower well-being
- **Vansteenkiste, Lens, Dewitte, De Witte, & Deci (2004):** Some passive job-search behavior may be purposively autonomous (genuine desire to live simply) or controlled (felt pressure from others). In two studies, autonomous motivation not to search was a significant negative predictor of commitment to having a job, expectations of finding one, optimism, and extrinsic aspirations; but a positive predictor of pleasant search experiences and life satisfaction

### Career Indecision

- **Guay, Senecal, Gauthier, & Fernet (2003):** When parents/peers were autonomy-supportive regarding career choices, participants felt more autonomous in finding career information, perceived themselves as more competent, and showed less career indecision
- **Guay, Ratelle, Senecal, Larose, & Deschenes (2006):** Chronically undecided students were low in autonomous motivation; developmentally appropriate undecided students were higher in autonomous motivation

### Thriving People, Thriving Workplaces

The closing argument: Most people **want** to contribute, **want** to experience competence, and **want** to feel part of a collaborative organization. The "economic" view that work is merely labor exchanged for money is insufficient. Work is optimally an opportunity to express one's capacities, to feel autonomy, competence, and connection. When these satisfactions are supported, higher quality performance and wellness result. When neglected or thwarted, alienation results, with many costs to organizations.

## New Concepts Introduced

- [[autonomous-work-motivation]] -- intrinsic + identified/integrated regulation applied to work; contrasted with controlled work motivation (external + introjected regulation)
- [[autonomy-supportive-management]] -- specific managerial orientation and trainable skill set based on SDT principles
- [[need-satisfaction-at-work]] -- the three basic psychological needs as universal mediators of workplace outcomes
- [[sdt-compensation-design]] -- SDT's specific framework for how pay and rewards should be structured
- [[work-engagement-sdt]] -- engagement as an outcome of need satisfaction, distinct from engagement in the colloquial sense
- **ToMo (Total Motivation)** -- Doshi & McGregor's corporate translation of SDT's [[relative-autonomy-index]]
- **Functional significance of pay** -- compensation interpreted as either informational or controlling
- **Motivational spillovers** (Gubler, Larkin, & Pierce, 2016) -- collateral negative effects of reward programs on non-targeted tasks
- **Reward choice** -- giving employees choice over their compensation package
