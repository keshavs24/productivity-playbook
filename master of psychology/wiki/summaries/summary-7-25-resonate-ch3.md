# Resonate Ch.3: Get to Know the Hero — Summary

**Author:** Nancy Duarte
**Source:** *Resonate* (2010), Chapter 3
**Phase:** 7 — Leadership Communication
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Designing a presentation without an audience in mind is like writing a love letter and addressing it "to whom it may concern."** The audience must be your focus while you create the content. Think of them not as a unified clump but as individuals waiting to have face-to-face conversations with you.
2. **Segment the audience meaningfully** — demographics alone are insufficient. Research the audience deeply: buy their publications, review their blogs, read their press releases, understand their challenges. Target the segment that will bring the most additional supporters.
3. **Meet the Hero by profiling who they are** across six dimensions: Lifestyle, Knowledge, Motivation and Desire, Values, Influence, and Respect. Then meet the Mentor (you) by profiling what you give them: Guidance, Confidence, and Tools.
4. **Create common ground** by identifying shared experiences, common goals, and your qualifications to guide them. Communicate from the overlap — where your facts/emotions and theirs intersect. The goal is to increase the amount of common ground they have with you. Personal values ultimately drive behavior, so align with existing values.

## Detailed Notes

### Segment the Audience
One way to get to know the audience is through segmentation. Target the segment that will bring the most additional supporters. Demographics (gender, title, geography) are only part of the story. Truly communicating effectively takes research — subscribe to their publications, search for articles, review blogs, read press releases and annual reports. The research creates empathy even if you only use a portion in the actual presentation.

### Case Study: Ronald Reagan — Challenger Address
Reagan's 1986 Challenger address demonstrates masterful audience segmentation. He addressed five distinct sub-audiences: collective mourners (the nation), families of the fallen (personal tone of empathy), schoolchildren (adopted tone of an empathizing parent), the Soviet Union (attacked their secrecy), and NASA employees (offered encouragement). His ability to credibly move in and out of different roles for different segments was a large part of what made him The Great Communicator.

### Meet the Hero
After splitting the audience into segments, connect personally with what makes them human. Blake Snyder coined the "save the cat" scene — where we meet the hero and he does something that makes the audience like him. Similarly, liking your audience is the first step to being genuine with them. Study them. Profile "Who They Are" across: Lifestyle, Knowledge, Motivation and Desire, Values, Influence, Respect. Your goal is to figure out what your audience cares about and link it to your idea.

### Meet the Mentor
Mentors are selfless and think of themselves in the context of others. The mentor's role is to influence the hero at critical junctures, moving them past blockades of doubt and fear. Two major responsibilities: teaching and gift-giving. The audience should leave knowing something they did not know before and with the ability to apply that knowledge to help them succeed. Profile "What You Give Them": Guidance, Confidence, Tools.

### Create Common Ground
By identifying and articulating shared experiences and goals, you build a path of trust so strong that they feel safe crossing to your side. Even magnificent qualifications should be revealed in a humble and selfless way. Duarte shares a personal story about failing to create common ground with her employees during the 2007 economic downturn — her urgent "danger is eminent" message was perceived as manipulative because she did not use symbols or experiences her audience could connect with.

### Communicate from the Overlap
People come to a presentation with their own facts and emotions. They absorb information and transform it into personal meaning. The presenter's job is to tune into the audience's frequency. If you send a message tuned to their needs and desires, they will change, and might even quiver with enthusiasm. The Venn diagram of Source (Presenter) and Receiver (Audience) shows the goal: increase the overlap of facts and emotions. Connects to [[common-ground]].

### Resonance Rule #3
"If a presenter knows the audience's resonant frequency and tunes to that, the audience will move."
