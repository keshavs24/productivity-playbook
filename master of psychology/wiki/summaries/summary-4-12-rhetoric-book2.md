# On Rhetoric: Book 2 — Summary

**Author(s):** Aristotle (translated by George A. Kennedy)
**Type:** Book (pp. 111-192)
**Phase:** 4 — Communication Foundations
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Emotions are defined as temporary states of mind that alter judgment.** For each emotion Aristotle systematically analyzes: (a) the definition, (b) the state of mind of those who feel it, (c) the persons toward whom it is directed, and (d) the causes or reasons. This is the earliest systematic psychology of emotions in Western thought.
2. **Ethos in a speech depends on three qualities: practical wisdom (phronesis), virtue (arete), and good will (eunoia).** A speaker who seems to lack any one of these will fail to be persuasive through character (2.1.5-7).
3. **Aristotle catalogs ten emotion-pairs** arranged chiastically (positive/negative): anger/calmness, friendliness/enmity, fear/confidence, shame/shamelessness, kindliness/unkindliness, pity/indignation, envy/emulation. Understanding these is essential for a speaker to put an audience in the right frame of mind.
4. **Character types differ by age, fortune, and power.** The young are impulsive, hopeful, and trusting; the old are cynical, cautious, and small-minded; those in their prime combine the best of both. Wealth makes people insolent and arrogant; power adds dignity and ambition.
5. **Enthymemes have twenty-eight common topoi** (lines of argument) that apply to all subjects — from opposites, from grammatical inflection, from correlatives, from the more and less, from definition, from authority, from consequences, and more. There are also nine fallacious topoi to recognize and avoid.

## Detailed Notes

### Chapter 1: Introduction — Character and Emotions as Means of Persuasion

- Book 2 resumes the discussion of *pisteis* from 1.2, now focusing on [[ethos]] and [[pathos]] as modes of persuasion alongside [[logos]]
- **Three components of trustworthy character:**
  1. **Practical wisdom** (*phronesis*) — the speaker seems to know what they are talking about
  2. **Virtue** (*arete*) — the speaker seems morally good
  3. **Good will** (*eunoia*) — the speaker seems to wish the audience well
- Speakers fail to persuade through character when they lack one or more of these: they may form wrong opinions (lack of *phronesis*), form right opinions but not share them honestly (lack of *arete*), or be prudent and honest but not care about the audience (lack of *eunoia*)
- **Emotions** (*pathe*) are "things through which, by undergoing change, people come to differ in their judgments, and which are accompanied by pain and pleasure" (2.1.8)
- For each emotion, one must grasp three things: the state of mind of those who feel it, toward whom they feel it, and for what reasons

### Chapters 2-11: The Emotions ([[rhetorical-emotions]])

#### Chapter 2: Anger (*Orge*)
- **Definition:** "desire, accompanied by mental and physical distress, for apparent retaliation because of an apparent slight, directed without justification against oneself or those near to one" (2.2.1)
- Three species of slighting (*oligoria*): contempt (*kataphronesis*), spite (*epereasmos*), and insult (*hybris*)
- **State of mind:** People are more easily angered when distressed, thwarted, or longing for something — each has prepared a path for anger through an underlying emotion (2.2.9-11)
- **Targets:** People become angry at those who laugh at them, mock them, belittle them, do not return favors, are insensitive to their needs, or forget things like their names
- Anger is always directed at specific individuals (not at classes of people, which is hatred)

#### Chapter 3: Calmness (*Praotes*)
- **Definition:** "settling down and quieting of anger" (2.3.2)
- People are calm toward those who: do not slight voluntarily, admit and repent wrongdoing, humble themselves, are serious with them, show greater kindness than offense, are feared or respected
- People are calm when in states of play, laughter, feasting, success, or when anger has cooled with time

#### Chapter 4: Friendliness (*Philia*) and Enmity
- **Definition of friendliness:** "wanting for someone what one thinks are good things — wanting them for that person's sake, not one's own — and being inclined to do these things to the extent one can" (2.4.2)
- Causes of friendship: companionship, shared interests, good humor, lack of reproachfulness, willingness to share faults, treating others as equals
- **Enmity vs. anger:** Anger is directed at individuals and is curable through time; hatred is directed at types (e.g., "all thieves") and is incurable. Anger desires the target's pain; hatred desires the target's non-existence (2.4.31)
- This distinction between anger (personal, temporary, painful) and hatred (categorical, permanent, painless) is psychologically sophisticated — cf. [[fundamental-attribution-error]]

#### Chapter 5: Fear (*Phobos*) and Confidence (*Tharsos*)
- **Definition of fear:** "a sort of pain and agitation derived from the imagination of a future destructive or painful evil" (2.5.1)
- Key insight: things are only feared when they seem *near*, not remote — "all know they will die, but that is not near at hand so they take no thought of it" (2.5.1)
- **Those feared:** people who have been wronged (they seek revenge), rivals, those stronger than us, the calm and dissembling (more dangerous than the quick-tempered because their intentions are never clear)
- **Confidence** is the opposite: people are confident when they think dangers are far off, when they have resources, when they have not wronged others, when they have allies
- **Fear inclines people to deliberation:** "no one deliberates about hopeless things" — fear must leave some hope to be rhetorically useful (2.5.14)

#### Chapter 6: Shame (*Aiskhyne*) and Shamelessness
- **Definition:** "a sort of pain and agitation concerning the class of evils, whether present, past, or future, that seem to bring a person into disrespect" (2.6.2)
- Things that cause shame: signs of cowardice, injustice, licentiousness, stinginess, flattery, boastfulness
- People feel shame before those whose opinion they value — admirers, rivals, the wise, large groups, those they live among
- "Shame is in the eyes" — people feel more shame when visible to those they respect (2.6.18)
- Shame is socially driven: it occurs only in relation to others' perceptions, not as internal guilt — cf. the distinction between shame cultures and guilt cultures

#### Chapter 7: Kindliness (*Kharis*)
- **Definition:** "a service to one in need, not in return for anything nor that the one rendering the service may get anything, but as something for the recipient" (2.7.2)
- Kindliness is great when the recipient is in great need, or when the service is great and difficult to obtain, or when the giver is the first or only one to provide it
- To refute claims of kindliness: show the service was done for self-interest, by chance, or under constraint

#### Chapters 8-9: Pity (*Eleos*) and Indignation (*Nemesis*)
- **Pity defined:** "a certain pain at an apparently destructive or painful evil happening to one who does not deserve it, and which a person might expect himself or one of his own to suffer, and this when it seems close at hand" (2.8.2)
- People who feel pity: those who have suffered in the past, the educated, the elderly, the weak, parents — those who believe they themselves could suffer similarly
- Pitiable things: death, bodily suffering, old age, disease, loss of friends, separation from companions
- **Indignation** is the opposite of pity — pain at undeserved *good* fortune, whereas pity is pain at undeserved *bad* fortune
- Both arise from the same moral character — a sense that outcomes should match desert

#### Chapter 10: Envy (*Phthonos*)
- **Definition:** "a certain kind of distress at apparent success on the part of one's peers" — not because one wants to get anything, but because they have it (2.10.1)
- Those who envy: those who have or seem to have equals, those close in time/place/age/reputation; the ambitious; the wise in their own conceit
- Those envied: those near in time, place, age, or reputation; those who have recently acquired what the envious person wants
- Key distinction: envy is about equals; we do not envy those far above or below us

#### Chapter 11: Emulation (*Zelos*)
- **Definition:** "a kind of distress at the apparent presence among others like him by nature of things honored and possible for a person to acquire, [arising] not because another has them but because the emulator does not" (2.11.1)
- Emulation is positive (striving to attain goods for oneself) while envy is negative (wanting to deprive others)
- Emulation is characteristic of the good; envy is characteristic of the bad
- This concludes the chiastically arranged survey of positive and negative emotions

### Chapters 12-17: Character Types (Ethos and the Audience)

#### Chapter 12: The Young
- Prone to desires, impulsive, fickle in desires, passionate but quickly satisfied
- Courageous (from hope and lack of experience with failure), trusting (not yet deceived), hopeful (live for the future)
- Love honor more than money; prefer fine things over the advantageous
- Make all mistakes "in the direction of excess" — "everything too much" (2.12.14-15)
- Fond of laughter and therefore witty — "wit is cultured insolence" (2.12.16)

#### Chapter 13: The Old
- Opposite of the young in nearly every respect: cynical, suspicious, small-minded, cowardly
- Cautious because of experience; "think" rather than "know"; qualify everything with "perhaps" and "maybe"
- Live by calculation rather than moral character; stingy because they know how hard it is to acquire money
- More inclined to pity (from weakness) than to indignation
- Not witty but querulous; fond of reminiscence

#### Chapter 14: The Prime of Life
- The mean between youth and old age — "having the right amount of both, neither trusting nor distrusting everybody" (2.14.1-2)
- Body in prime from 30-35; mind from 49
- Combine prudence with courage

#### Chapters 15-17: Character from Fortune — Birth, Wealth, Power
- **Wellborn:** ambitious, boastful about ancestors, sometimes contemptuous of equals
- **Wealthy:** insolent, arrogant, ostentatious; "their state of mind is that of those who have all good things" (2.16.1); wealth is "a kind of standard of value of other things" — everything seems purchasable
- **Powerful:** more ambitious and earnest than the wealthy; display "mild and graceful severity"

### Chapters 18-26: Dialectical Features — Logical Argument

#### Chapter 18: Introduction to Logical Argument
- Having treated [[ethos]] and [[pathos]], Aristotle returns to [[logos]]
- Common features (*koina*) applicable to all species: the possible/impossible, past fact, future fact, and degree of magnitude (amplification)

#### Chapter 19: Common Topics — Possible/Impossible; Past/Future Fact
- Systematic list of arguments for showing something is possible or impossible (e.g., if the harder is possible, so is the easier; if the beginning is possible, so is the end)
- Arguments for past fact (e.g., if someone had capacity and motive, they probably acted)
- Arguments for future fact (e.g., if what usually precedes has happened, what follows will probably happen)

#### Chapter 20: Paradigm (Example)
- Two species: historical examples and invented illustrations (fables/comparisons)
- Fables are easier to devise; historical examples are more useful in deliberation (future events follow past patterns)
- When enthymemes are available, use paradigms as supplementary "witnesses"; when not, use paradigms as the main demonstration
- Stesichorus's fable of the horse (warning against giving a general too much power) as a classic example

#### Chapter 21: Maxim (*Gnome*)
- A maxim is a general statement about actions and choices — like an enthymeme with the syllogistic framework removed
- Four species: (1) with supplement that is part of an enthymeme, (2) with supplement not part of an enthymeme, (3) without supplement (self-evident), (4) without supplement (paradoxical — these need a supplement to explain)
- Maxims are most appropriate to older, experienced speakers — "to speak in maxims is unseemly for one too young, as is storytelling" (2.21.9)
- Maxims contribute powerfully to [[ethos]] by making the speech "ethical" — they reveal the speaker's deliberate moral choices (2.21.16)

#### Chapter 22: Enthymemes
- The [[enthymeme]] is a rhetorical syllogism — a kind of syllogism drawn from probable premises
- Two species: **demonstrative** (showing something is or is not the case) and **refutative** (drawing the opposite conclusion from what is agreed)
- Do not draw conclusions from too far back (clarity) or state what is obvious (tedium)
- **Common vs. specific topics:** common topics apply to all subjects; specific topics come from the premises of particular disciplines
- The more specifically one chooses premises, the more one departs from rhetoric into specialized knowledge

#### Chapter 23: Twenty-Eight Common Topoi
The twenty-eight [[topoi]] (lines of argument) for constructing enthymemes:

1. **From opposites** — if the predicate is true of the opposite, confirm or refute
2. **From grammatical inflection** — if "justly" applies, so does "just"
3. **From correlatives** — if one correlate is true, so is the other
4. **From the more and the less** (*a fortiori*) — if the less likely is true, the more likely is too
5. **From looking at the time** — contrasting past expectations with present reality
6. **From turning the argument on the opponent** — using an opponent's words against them
7. **From definition** — grasping the essence of a thing to draw syllogistic conclusions
8. **From varied meanings** — exploiting multiple senses of a word
9. **From division** — eliminating alternatives to prove the remaining one
10. **From induction** — using specific cases to establish a general rule
11. **From authority** — citing judgments of the wise, famous, or respected
12. **From subordinate parts** — arguing from species to genus
13. **From the consequence** — arguing that good and bad follow from the same cause
14. **From contrasting opposites** — using a dilemma where both alternatives lead to the same conclusion
15. **From hypocritical deception** — showing opponents' private views contradict their public ones
16. **From consequences by analogy** — if a similar case has a certain result, so does this one
17. **From results to causes** — if the result is the same, the things that produced it are the same
18. **From contrasted choices** — choosing differently before and after an event reveals true preference
19. **From identifying purpose with cause** — the purpose for which something exists is its cause
20. **From reasons for and against** — factors that impel and deter action
21. **From the implausible** — things that seem implausible are more credible because no one would invent them
22. **From contradictions** — showing the opponent's statements, actions, or dates are inconsistent
23. **From the cause of a false impression** — explaining why something seemed true but was not
24. **From cause and effect** — if the cause exists, so does the effect; if the cause is absent, so is the effect
25. **From a better plan** — showing a better alternative existed but was not taken
26. **From comparison of contraries** — examining what is contrary to what has been done
27. **From what would have been a mistake** — defending by showing the outcome of doing otherwise would have been worse
28. **From the meaning of a name** — drawing conclusions from etymological or figurative significance of names

Refutative enthymemes are better liked by audiences than demonstrative ones because they bring opposites together in brief form.

#### Chapter 24: Fallacious (Apparent) Enthymemes
Nine fallacious topoi:
1. **From verbal style** — phrasing that mimics valid conclusions without logical connection
2. **From combination/division (and omission)** — combining or dividing what should be kept separate
3. **From exaggeration** — amplifying without proving the action occurred
4. **From a non-necessary sign** — treating a fallible sign as conclusive
5. **From accidental result** — confusing coincidence with causation
6. **From affirming the consequent** — invalid logical form (if high-minded people scorn crowds, and X scorns crowds, then X is high-minded)
7. **From post hoc, ergo propter hoc** — assuming temporal sequence equals causation
8. **From omission of when and how** — ignoring qualifying circumstances
9. **From confusing the particular with the general** — a particular probability is not a general one ("to make the weaker argument the stronger")

#### Chapter 25: Refutation (*Lysis*)
- Refutation can be by counter-syllogism or by objection (*enstasis*)
- Objections come from four sources: the original argument, something similar, something opposite, or previous judgments
- Since enthymemes come from four sources (probability, paradigm, *tekmerion*, and sign), and only *tekmeria* are irrefutable, most enthymemes can be refuted

#### Chapter 26: Amplification, Refutation, and Objection
- Amplification is not an element or topic of an enthymeme — it contributes to showing degree of magnitude, not to proving the case
- Objection (*enstasis*) is not an enthymeme — it is pointing out an opinion from which it is clear the opponent's argument is not valid

### Transition to Book 3
- "Since there are in fact three things that should be systematically worked out in discussion of speech, let us regard what has been said as enough about paradigms and maxims and enthymemes and in general about the thought (*dianoia*) and the sources of argument and how we shall refute them. It remains to go through an account of style (*lexis*) and arrangement (*taxis*)." (2.26.5)

## Contradictions or Tensions

- Aristotle's analysis of emotions as temporary states of mind that alter judgment parallels modern research on [[framing-effects]] — emotions function as a frame that changes how identical information is evaluated
- The distinction between anger (personal, at individuals) and hatred (categorical, at types) anticipates the [[fundamental-attribution-error]] — the tendency to attribute others' behavior to character rather than situation
- Aristotle's three components of ethos (phronesis, arete, eunoia) map loosely onto modern credibility research: competence, trustworthiness/character, and caring/good will — cf. [[authority-principle]] and [[liking-principle]]
- The emotion chapters provide detailed behavioral analysis but no examples of rhetorical application — Kennedy notes this as a significant limitation
