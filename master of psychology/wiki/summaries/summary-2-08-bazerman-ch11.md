# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.10 — Making Rational Decisions in Negotiations

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.10, pp.175-192)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Raiffa's "decision-analytic" approach provides a prescriptive framework for the focal negotiator while remaining descriptive about the opponent -- acknowledging that the other side may behave irrationally
2. Three sets of information determine the structure of any negotiation: each party's BATNA, each party's set of interests, and the relative importance of each party's interests
3. Negotiations involve two simultaneous tasks -- creating value (expanding the pie) and claiming value (dividing it) -- and most negotiators focus too much on claiming at the expense of creating
4. Differences between parties (in risk tolerance, time preferences, skill sets, future predictions) are opportunities for value creation, not obstacles
5. Six strategies for value creation: build trust/share information, ask questions, strategically disclose information, negotiate multiple issues simultaneously, make multiple offers simultaneously, and search for post-settlement settlements

## Detailed Notes

### Why Negotiation Matters

Negotiation outcomes are not inevitable. In MBA classroom simulations where all dyads negotiate the same problem with access to the same information, the range of outcomes is enormous. The decisions and behaviors of each negotiator matter profoundly.

### Approaches to Negotiation

**Game theory** provides the most precise prescriptive advice but has two critical limitations:
1. Requires complete description of all options and outcomes -- infinitely complex in practice
2. Requires all players to act rationally -- which they systematically do not

**Raiffa's decision-analytic approach (1982, 2001)** overcomes these limitations by being "asymmetrically prescriptive/descriptive": it provides the best available advice to you as the focal negotiator, given the most likely profile of the other party's actual (not ideal) behavior. Three key information sets:
1. Each party's alternative to a negotiated agreement ([[batna]])
2. Each party's set of interests
3. The relative importance of each party's interests

### Alternatives to a Negotiated Agreement (BATNA)

The BATNA (Fisher, Ury, & Patton, 1981) provides a lower bound for the minimum outcome you should accept. It determines your **reservation point** -- the point of indifference between a negotiated agreement and impasse.

Key rules:
- Accept any agreement better than your BATNA; reject any that is worse
- Never enter a negotiation without knowing your BATNA and estimating the opponent's
- Your most fundamental leverage is your threat to walk away
- Many negotiators say "no" to offers better than their BATNA and "yes" to offers worse -- because they failed to carefully assess it in advance

### The Interests of the Parties

Positions are what parties ask for. Interests are the motives behind positions. Focusing on interests rather than positions enables creative solutions.

**The purchasing officer example:** A Fortune 100 company negotiated with a European supplier. Both agreed on price ($18/lb for 1M lbs/year) but were stuck on exclusivity. When the purchasing officer finally asked "why won't you grant exclusivity?", the producer revealed he needed to sell 250 lbs/year to his cousin. The deal closed immediately with a cousin exception -- a trivial concession that had been blocking a major agreement.

**Preparation is key:** Know how important each issue is to you AND estimate how important each issue is to your counterpart. The best agreements come from trading relatively unimportant issues for more important ones.

### Claiming Value in Negotiation

In single-issue negotiations (e.g., salary), the only task is claiming value -- dividing a fixed amount.

**The bargaining zone:** When reservation points overlap, a positive bargaining zone exists. Example: employer willing to pay up to $97,000, recruit will accept anything above $93,000. Any point between $93,000 and $97,000 is acceptable to both. A key skill is determining the other party's reservation point and aiming for a resolution barely acceptable to them.

The risk of overreaching: if the recruit demands $98,000 and the employer holds at $92,000, both believing the other will cave, a deal in the positive bargaining zone is blocked by stubbornness. As Ben Franklin observed: "The worst outcome is when, by overreaching greed, no bargain is struck, and a trade that could have been advantageous to both parties does not come off at all."

### Creating Value in Negotiation

Most important negotiations involve multiple issues. Through identifying and adding issues, parties can create value -- increasing the total benefit available.

**Camp David Accords (1978):** Egypt wanted sovereignty over the Sinai; Israel wanted security. Positions appeared incompatible (negative bargaining zone on a single dimension). But their underlying interests were compatible: Egypt wanted sovereignty, Israel wanted military protection. The solution: return the Sinai to Egypt in exchange for a demilitarized zone and new Israeli air bases. Trading across issues of differential value created a positive bargaining zone that positional bargaining could not find.

**Types of differences that enable trades:**
- **Risk tolerance:** One partner takes higher salary, the other takes higher ownership share
- **Time preferences:** Exploit differences in budget cycles or patience
- **Skill differences:** Complementary skills create the opportunity for work allocation trades (Northcraft & Neale, 1993)
- **Tax status, accounting treatments, liquidity** (Lax & Sebenius, 2002)

The overarching principle: "To the well-informed negotiator, differences are not stumbling blocks, but opportunities."

### Creating Value through Bets (Contingent Contracts)

When parties disagree about future outcomes, they can bet on them instead of arguing. Contingent contracts resolve impasse by allowing each party to bet on their own (biased) prediction.

**TV syndication example (Bazerman & Gillespie, 1999):** A production company and a TV station disagreed about expected ratings (seller predicted 9 share, buyer predicted 7 share). Each rating point was worth $1M in advertising. Instead of splitting the difference, the price should have been made contingent on actual ratings.

Four benefits of contingent contracts:
1. **Build on differences** to create joint value
2. **Short-circuit debates** over uncertain future events
3. **Diagnose bluffs** -- if someone makes a claim and backs off when you propose a bet, the claim was likely false
4. **Establish performance incentives** -- sales commissions, royalties, and contingency fees are all contingent contracts

### The Tools of Value Creation

Six strategies for collecting information to create value:

1. **Build trust and share information** -- the most direct path, but hardest when trust is low. Even within the same company, divisions often fail to share information in internal negotiations, destroying value.
2. **Ask questions** -- "How are you going to use our products?" "What would an ideal supplier look like?" Most negotiators talk too much and listen too little, treating negotiation as persuasion rather than information gathering.
3. **Strategically disclose information** -- give away information of comparatively minor importance to invite reciprocity. Do NOT reveal your reservation price. Reciprocity in negotiation: "When you scream at people, they tend to scream back. When you apologize, they may do the same."
4. **Negotiate multiple issues simultaneously** -- insist "nothing is settled until everything is settled." Negotiating issue by issue destroys opportunities for tradeoffs. Package offers reveal differential importance across issues.
5. **Make multiple offers simultaneously** -- present several package offers of equal value to you. The opponent's reactions reveal their preferences, enabling better trades. Also signals flexibility and interest in understanding their needs.
6. **Search for post-settlement settlements (PSS)** -- After reaching a deal, propose looking for improvements that make both parties better off (Raiffa, 1985). The signed agreement serves as a safety net, removing risk.

### Preparation Checklist

Ten questions for pre-negotiation preparation:
1. What is your BATNA?
2. What is your reservation price?
3. What are the issues?
4. How important is each issue to you?
5. What is the other party's BATNA?
6. What is their reservation price?
7. How important is each issue to them?
8. Are there viable value-creating trades?
9. Are you and the other party in disagreement about future events? (Is a bet viable?)
10. How will you identify information you don't currently know?
