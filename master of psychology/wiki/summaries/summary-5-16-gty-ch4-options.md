# Getting to Yes Ch.4: Invent Options for Mutual Gain — Summary

**Author(s):** Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton
**Source:** *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, 2nd Edition, Chapter 4
**Phase:** 5 — Negotiation
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **In most negotiations, there are four major obstacles to inventing creative options:** (1) premature judgment, (2) searching for the single answer, (3) the assumption of a fixed pie, and (4) thinking that "solving their problem is their problem."
2. **Separate inventing from deciding.** Since judgment hinders imagination, separate the creative act from the critical one. Invent first, decide later. Brainstorming sessions are the key technique.
3. **Broaden your options.** Even the best brainstorming session tends to converge on the single best answer too quickly. Use the Circle Chart (four types of thinking: Problem, Analysis, Approaches, Action Ideas) to multiply options.
4. **Look for mutual gain.** The third major block to creative problem-solving is [[fixed-pie-bias]] — the assumption that "the less for you, the more for me." Rarely is this true. Both sides can almost always be worse off than they are now.
5. **Dovetail differing interests.** Agreement is often made possible precisely because interests differ — in value placed on time, in forecasts, in aversion to risk.
6. **Make their decision easy.** Since success depends on the other side making a decision you want, you should do what you can to make that decision as easy as possible.

## Detailed Notes

### Diagnosis: Four Obstacles to Creative Options

**Premature judgment.** Under the pressure of a forthcoming negotiation, your critical sense is likely to be sharper. Practical negotiation appears to call for practical thinking, not wild ideas. Nothing is so harmful to inventing as a critical sense waiting to pounce on the drawbacks of any new idea. Judgment hinders imagination.

**Searching for the single answer.** Most people see their job as narrowing the gap between positions, not broadening the options available. "We're having a hard enough time agreeing as it is. The last thing we need is a bunch of different ideas." The first impediment is premature closure; the second is premature criticism.

**The assumption of a [[fixed-pie-bias|fixed pie]].** Each side sees the situation as essentially "either I get what is in dispute or you do." A negotiation over the price of a car appears to be a "fixed-sum" game — $100 more for you on the price means $100 less for me.

**Thinking that "solving their problem is their problem."** A final obstacle is each side's concern with only its own immediate interests. The emotional involvement on one side makes it difficult to achieve the detachment necessary to think up wise ways of meeting the interests of both sides.

### Prescription: Separate Inventing from Deciding

**Brainstorming sessions.** A brainstorming session is designed to produce as many ideas as possible. The key ground rule is to postpone all criticism and evaluation of ideas. Before brainstorming:
1. Define your purpose
2. Choose a few participants (five to eight people)
3. Change the environment (different from regular discussions)
4. Design an informal atmosphere
5. Choose a facilitator

**Daring brainstorming guidelines:**
- Seat the participants side by side facing the problem (the physical reinforces the psychological)
- Clarify the ground rules, including the no-criticism rule
- Brainstorm — come up with a long list from every conceivable angle
- Record ideas in full view
- After brainstorming: star the most promising ideas, invent improvements, set a time to evaluate and decide

**Consider brainstorming with the other side.** More difficult but potentially more valuable. To protect yourself, distinguish the brainstorming session explicitly from a negotiating session — people state official views and speak on the record in negotiations, but in brainstorming, ideas are possibilities, not proposals.

### Broaden Your Options: The Circle Chart

The Circle Chart diagrams four types of thinking arranged in a quadrant:

| | WHAT IS WRONG | WHAT MIGHT BE DONE |
|---|---|---|
| **IN THEORY** | Step II: Analysis (diagnose, categorize, suggest causes, note barriers) | Step III: Approaches (strategies, prescriptions, theoretical cures, broad ideas) |
| **IN THE REAL WORLD** | Step I: Problem (symptoms, facts, disliked situation) | Step IV: Action Ideas (specific steps to deal with the problem) |

You can use one good option on the table to ask about the theory that makes it good, then use that theory to invent more options. This shuttling between the specific and the general is a powerful way to multiply options.

**Look through the eyes of different experts.** Examine the problem from the perspective of different professions — an educator, banker, psychiatrist, lawyer, minister, doctor, feminist, football coach.

**Invent agreements of different strengths.** Think of "weaker" versions: substantive/procedural, permanent/provisional, comprehensive/partial, final/in-principle, unconditional/contingent, binding/nonbinding, first-order/second-order.

**Change the scope of the proposed agreement.** "Fractionate" problems into smaller, more manageable units. Or enlarge the subject matter to "sweeten the pot" — as when India and Pakistan over the Indus River became amenable to settlement only after new irrigation projects were added to the discussion.

### Look for Mutual Gain

**Identify shared interests.** The Townsend Oil-Pageville example: both the oil company (considering a major refurbishment) and the city (wanting tax revenue and new businesses) have shared interests in industrial expansion and economic development, even though they appear locked in a simple tax dispute.

Three points about shared interests:
1. They are latent in every negotiation — may not be immediately obvious
2. They are opportunities, not godsends — make them into something useful, formulate them as a shared goal
3. Stressing shared interests can make the negotiation smoother and more amicable

**Dovetail differing interests.** The Jack Sprat principle — differences between parties create the possibility of gains from trade:
- *Different interests:* Form vs. substance, economic vs. political, internal vs. external
- *Different beliefs:* If both believe they are right, an impartial arbitrator settles it — both sides confident of victory
- *Different values placed on time:* Installment plans work on this principle
- *Different forecasts:* An aging baseball star and team owner can structure a bonus based on performance
- *Different aversion to risk:* Risk can be traded for revenue (the Law of the Sea mining example)

**Ask for their preferences.** Invent multiple options equally acceptable to you and ask which they prefer. Then work with that option, refine it, present variants. Without anyone making a decision, you can improve a plan until you can find no more joint gains.

### Make Their Decision Easy

**Whose shoes?** Focus on one person on the other side and figure out how the problem looks from his or her point of view.

**What decision?** You are trying to generate options that will change their presently perceived choice — make it an easy decision rather than a hard one.

**Making threats is not enough.** Offers are usually more effective. Concentrate on making them aware of the consequences they can *expect* if they decide as you wish.

**A final test:** Write out a sentence or two illustrating what the other side's most powerful critic might say. Then write a response. A final test of an option is to write it in the form of a "yesable proposition."

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The entire chapter is a frontal assault on [[fixed-pie-bias]] — demonstrating that the assumption of zero-sum bargaining is almost always wrong
- The brainstorming technique of separating inventing from deciding connects to dual-process theory: brainstorming engages System 1 (creative, associative) while evaluation engages System 2 (analytical, critical). Mixing them undermines both.
- The "dovetail differing interests" principle provides practical techniques for [[value-creation-in-negotiation]]
