# Anomalies: The Endowment Effect, Loss Aversion, and Status Quo Bias — Summary

**Author(s):** [[kahneman|Daniel Kahneman]], Jack L. Knetsch, and [[thaler|Richard H. Thaler]]
**Type:** Landmark anomalies paper
**Published:** *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 5:1, 193-206, 1991. Reprinted as Ch. 8 in *Choices, Values, and Frames* (2000).
**Phase:** 2 — Decision Science
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Three anomalies -- endowment effect, loss aversion, and status quo bias -- are deeply connected manifestations of the same underlying phenomenon:** people evaluate outcomes as changes from a reference point, and losses from that reference point loom larger than equivalent gains.
2. **The endowment effect is robustly demonstrated in controlled experiments.** In the classic mug experiment, sellers demanded a median of $7.12 while choosers valued the same mug at $3.12. Ownership itself creates value through loss-aversion framing.
3. **Status quo bias is pervasive across domains.** Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988) documented it in hypothetical choices about jobs, investments, and policy. They also found it in real-world data: Harvard employees strongly favored existing health plans over new ones.
4. **Standard economic predictions about trade and market efficiency are systematically violated.** The Coase theorem predicts that initial allocations should not affect final outcomes (given low transaction costs), but endowment effects cause trade volume to be about half of what theory predicts.
5. **Loss aversion has implications for market behavior, labor economics, and finance.** It explains the equity premium puzzle, wage rigidity, asymmetric price elasticities, and the reluctance to sell losing investments.

## Detailed Notes

### The Endowment Effect

The paper reviews multiple experimental demonstrations:

**Mug experiments (Kahneman, Knetsch, & Thaler, 1990):**
- Decorated mugs (retail ~$5) were randomly distributed to half the students
- Sellers (mug owners) set minimum selling prices; choosers (non-owners) indicated their preference between the mug and each possible cash amount
- Median selling price: $7.12; median chooser value: $3.12
- Replicated with median selling price $7.00 and chooser value $3.50
- The ratio of about 2:1 is consistent with [[loss-aversion]] coefficient estimates from risky choice

**Trading experiments:**
- When mugs were allocated randomly and a market was conducted, the actual volume of trade was consistently about half the predicted amount
- Control experiments with tokens (redeemable for cash) produced the predicted volume and no buyer-seller disparity
- This rules out strategic behavior, confusion, or transaction costs as explanations

**Knetsch (1989) mug/chocolate experiment:**
- One class received mugs, another received Swiss chocolate bars
- Given the option to trade, approximately 90% of participants retained their initial endowment
- If preferences were pre-existing and independent of endowment, about half should have traded

### Loss Aversion

The paper argues that all three anomalies flow from the same principle: [[loss-aversion]], the asymmetry of the [[value-function]].

Key evidence for loss aversion in riskless choice:
- **WTA/WTP disparity:** The minimum amount sellers accept (WTA) consistently exceeds the maximum buyers would pay (WTP) by ratios of 2:1 or more, across many goods
- **The disparity survives market experience**, albeit reduced (Brookshire & Coursey, 1987; Coursey, Hovis, & Schulze, 1987)
- **Loss aversion coefficient of about 2** converges across risky and riskless domains: from gambling experiments (50-50 bet to win $25 or lose $10 is barely acceptable, ratio 2.5:1) and from endowment experiments (mug values $7.12 vs $3.12, ratio ~2.3:1)

### Status Quo Bias

**Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988)** documented systematic status quo bias:
- In hypothetical choice problems, designating an option as the status quo significantly increased its selection
- The results were well described by: P(SQ) = 0.17 + 0.83 P(N), where P(SQ) is the probability of choosing the status quo option and P(N) is the probability of that option in the neutral (no status quo) version
- The 0.17 additive boost represents the status quo advantage

**Real-world evidence:**
- Harvard employees strongly favored existing health plans over new alternatives, despite minimal switching costs
- TIAA-CREF pension allocations remained remarkably stable year to year despite large variations in returns
- The status quo bias provides an explanation for brand loyalty, pioneer firm advantage, and the prediction that "rational models... will present excessively radical conclusions, exaggerating individuals' responses to changing economic variables"

### Implications

The paper draws out implications for:
- **Market efficiency:** The Coase theorem's prediction that initial allocations do not affect final outcomes is violated when loss aversion is present
- **The WTA-WTP gap:** Not just a curiosity -- it has implications for cost-benefit analysis, environmental valuation, and legal damages
- **Labor markets:** Loss aversion helps explain downward wage rigidity
- **Finance:** The equity premium puzzle may reflect loss-averse evaluation of portfolio returns

## New Concepts Introduced

- [[status-quo-bias]] — The systematic preference for the current state of affairs, driven by loss aversion (strengthens existing concept)
- WTA-WTP disparity as a formal measure of loss aversion in riskless choice

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **With Coase theorem:** The endowment effect means initial allocations DO affect final outcomes, even with low transaction costs
- **With standard consumer theory:** Indifference curves are not reversible; they "kink" at the reference point

## Quotes Worth Keeping

> "Loss aversion implies the status quo bias. As noted by Samuelson and Zeckhauser, there are several factors, such as costs of thinking, transaction costs, and psychological commitment to prior choices that can induce a status quo bias even in the absence of loss aversion."
