# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.9 — Fairness and Ethics in Decision Making

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.8, pp.132-159)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Fairness considerations systematically override economic rationality -- people reject profitable deals, punish unfair behavior at personal cost, and base fairness judgments on reference points and framing rather than objective outcomes
2. The [[ultimatum-game]] demonstrates that humans universally resist unfair allocations, even when rejection is economically irrational; this holds across cultures and stake sizes
3. [[bounded-ethicality]] -- the central concept of the chapter -- argues that most unethical behavior occurs without conscious intent, driven by cognitive biases that operate below awareness
4. Six manifestations of bounded ethicality: [[overclaiming-credit]], [[in-group-favoritism]], [[implicit-bias]] (via the IAT), [[indirectly-unethical-behavior]], sacred value tradeoffs, and [[conflicts-of-interest]]
5. Disclosure of conflicts of interest can paradoxically increase bias rather than reduce it, because advisers feel "morally licensed" after disclosure while advisees fail to sufficiently discount biased advice

## Detailed Notes

### Part 1: Perceptions of Fairness

#### When Supply and Demand Feel Unfair
Opening scenarios: accepting a $110K offer when peers get $120K; a hardware store raising snow shovel prices after a blizzard. Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler (1986) found 82% viewed post-snowstorm price increases as unfair, despite economic rationality.

Key findings on fairness perceptions:
- Fairness judgments are susceptible to [[framing-effects]]: a 7% wage cut with no inflation (62% said unfair) vs. a 5% raise with 12% inflation (only 22% said unfair) -- same real income change, opposite fairness judgments. This is the "money illusion."
- Scenario 1: Dealer raises price $200 above list during shortage -- 71% say unfair. Scenario 2: Dealer eliminates $200 discount during shortage -- only 42% say unfair. Same $200 increase, different reference points.
- List prices act as powerful anchors for fairness assessments. Departures from status quo are perceived as unfair even when economically justified.
- Thaler (2004): Consumers' emotions determine what is "fair" -- Delta's $2 surcharge for non-Internet tickets, Coke's demand-based vending machines, AIG bonuses during bailout were all market-rational but perceived as unfair.

#### When We Resist "Unfair" Ultimatums
The airplane scenario: Vivian offers $5,000 to split between you and Mark. Mark proposes $4,900/$100. Most people reject this even though $100 > $0.

**[[ultimatum-game]] research (Guth, Schmittberger, & Schwarze, 1982):**
- Proposers typically offer 30-50% of the pie; most common offer is 50/50
- Responders routinely reject offers below 20%
- This holds even when stakes equal several months' wages (Cameron, 1999; Hoffman, McCabe, & Smith, 1996)
- Ochs and Roth (1989): 81% of rejected offers were followed by disadvantageous counteroffers -- parties demanded LESS than what they just rejected

**Neuroimaging evidence (Sanfey et al., 2003):**
- fMRI reveals different brain activation for fair vs. unfair offers
- Unfair offers activate the anterior insula (negative emotional states) AND the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (rational desire to maximize money)
- Greater emotional response for unfair offers from humans vs. computers
- The emotional response provides concrete evidence that fairness judgments are partly emotional processes

**Cross-cultural consistency:** Henrich et al. (2001) studied 15 societies worldwide. Fairness mattered everywhere, though implementation varied by cultural norms. Even capuchin monkeys rebel against unequal rewards for equal work (Brosnan & de Waal, 2003).

#### When We Are Concerned About the Outcomes of Others
People care deeply about relative outcomes, not just absolute ones:
- Organizations create elaborate pay grade systems to manage social comparison
- Depken (2000): Smaller pay differentials within MLB teams correlated with better performance
- Fredrickson, Davis-Blake, and Sanders (2010): Pay differences among top S&P 500 executives negatively correlated with firm performance
- Loewenstein, Thompson, and Bazerman (1989): In interpersonal settings, the outcomes of others serve as reference points that can overwhelm concern for personal outcomes
- Bazerman, Loewenstein, and White (1992): 70% rated $400/$400 as more acceptable than $500/$700 in separate evaluation, but only 22% chose it when choosing directly
- Blount and Bazerman (1996): When evaluating a single experiment, social comparison (others getting $10 vs. $7) was critical; with multiple options available, people could compare across experiments and social comparison mattered less

**Perverse consequences of equality norms:**
- The 50/50 split heuristic can be manipulated (car dealer anchoring negotiation to create a favorable "split")
- Messick (1991): People expect equal splits even when rational analysis supports asymmetry

#### Why Do Fairness Judgments Matter?
- People punish unfair behavior even at personal cost (rejecting ultimatum offers)
- Third-party observers also punish: Fehr and Fischbacher (2004) found 55% of observers paid to punish stingy dictators ($1 cost to observer = $3 penalty to dictator)
- "[[altruistic-punishment]]" activates the dorsal striatum -- a reward center (de Quervain et al., 2004)
- Understanding fairness is essential for managers because employees, colleagues, and customers will judge and react to perceived unfairness

### Part 2: Bounded Ethicality

#### The Core Argument
Traditional ethics focuses on deliberate choices to behave unethically. Bazerman and colleagues argue the bigger problem is unethical behavior that occurs without the actor's awareness -- [[bounded-ethicality]] (Chugh, Bazerman, & Banaji, 2005).

Central claim: understanding and changing ethicality requires going beyond the assumption that ethical failures result from people consciously choosing self-reward over right behavior. The unconscious mind can motivate unethical behavior. [[system-1-system-2]] thinking matters: System 1 allows bounded ethicality biases to develop, leading to decisions that deviate from personal standards.

Chugh (2004): The pace of managerial life exacerbates bounded ethicality because it demands the speed and decisiveness of System 1.

#### Six Manifestations of Bounded Ethicality

**1. [[overclaiming-credit]]:**
- Ross and Sicoly (1979): Married couples' estimates of their own contribution to household tasks summed to ~140%
- Demonstrated across academia, athletics, fundraising (Caruso, Epley, & Bazerman, 2006)
- At organizational level: joint ventures fail partly because each side overclaims credit, views the other's behavior as unfair, and reduces contribution
- **Remedy:** Caruso, Epley, and Bazerman (2007): Having group members "unpack" everyone's contributions (think about what OTHERS did) reduced overclaiming from 139% to 121%. Perspective-taking reduces the bias.

**2. [[in-group-favoritism]]:**
- People favor those who share their nationality, religion, race, gender, alma mater
- Favoring in-group members in allocating scarce resources (jobs, loans, admissions) is functionally equivalent to discriminating against out-groups
- Dasgupta (2004): Nearly 100 studies show greater positive association with in-groups and negative association with out-groups
- Bernhard, Fischbacher, and Fehr (2006): Willingness to punish norm violators is much greater when the victim is similar to oneself
- Messick and Bazerman (1996): Discriminatory mortgage lending is more likely driven by making too many loans to in-group whites than by overt hostility to minorities
- Legacy admissions at elite universities: a form of in-group favoritism that discriminates against more qualified unconnected applicants

**3. [[implicit-bias]] / Implicit Attitudes:**
- When we meet someone, our minds automatically activate stereotypes of race, sex, age (Macrae & Bodenhausen, 2001)
- Bargh, Chen, and Burrows (1996): Subliminal exposure to black faces increased aggression in subsequent tasks
- The **Implicit Association Test (IAT)** (Greenwald, McGhee, & Schwartz, 1998): Measures strength of implicit associations between categories (e.g., White/Black and Good/Bad)
- Nosek, Banaji, and Greenwald (2002): ~75% of white Americans show pro-white implicit attitudes on the IAT
- Rudman and Borgida (1995): Implicit stereotypes predicted discrimination against female job applicants
- McConnell and Leibold (2001): Implicit attitudes predicted nonverbal behaviors toward different groups
- Implicit attitudes are more predictive of spontaneous (System 1) behaviors; explicit attitudes predict thoughtful (System 2) behaviors
- Banaji (2001): Calls implicit attitudes "ordinary prejudice" -- rooted in normal mental processes of categorization
- Henry Louis Gates Jr. arrest (2009): Sgt. Crowley, who taught anti-racial-profiling courses, may have been influenced by implicit racial bias in his System 1 snap judgment
- Correll et al. (2007): In computer simulations, participants shot more unarmed black men than white men
- Morgan Stanley's $54M sex discrimination settlement (2004): EEOC argued the mostly white male leadership could not recognize gender inequities

**4. [[indirectly-unethical-behavior]]:**
- Pharmaceutical example (Paharia, Kassam, Greene, & Bazerman, 2007): Raising drug price from $3 to $9 was judged MORE unethical than selling rights to a smaller firm that raised it to $15 -- even though the indirect route caused more harm
- Real-world case: Merck sold cancer drug Mustargen rights to Ovation Pharmaceuticals, which raised the wholesale price roughly ten times
- Dana, Weber, and Kuang (2007): In a modified dictator game with "hidden payoff" information, 50% chose not to click to see the consequences of their selfish choice, then chose selfishly. Willful ignorance enables selfish behavior.
- Dana, Cain, and Dawes (2006): Given the choice to (1) play a standard dictator game or (2) exit silently with $9 (other party gets nothing and never knows), one-third took the silent exit -- preferring to keep their selfishness secret

**5. When Values Seem Sacred:**
- Tetlock (1986): Value pluralism means the world rarely allows us to hold a single principle sacred; tradeoffs are inevitable
- Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green, and Lerner (2000): When forced to consider tradeoffs between sacred and secular values, people respond with "moral outrage" and "cleansing" rituals
- Haidt (2001, 2007): Moral judgments produce emotional reactions first, which then drive cognitive assessments (emotions precede reasoning, not the reverse)
- Haidt, Bjorklund, and Murphy (2007): 77% of participants refused $2 to sign a (meaningless, non-binding) form selling their soul -- even many who didn't believe in souls

**6. [[conflicts-of-interest]] / The Psychology of Conflicts of Interest:**
- Professionals (financial advisers, surgeons, doctors, lawyers, real-estate agents) routinely face conflicts between personal gain and client welfare
- Most assume they are personally immune to such conflicts -- they are wrong
- Moore, Cain, Loewenstein, and Bazerman (2005): When we are motivated to interpret data favorably, we are incapable of providing objective assessments
- **Disclosure paradox (Cain, Loewenstein, & Moore, 2005):** Advisers whose conflicts were disclosed gave MORE biased advice (higher estimates in a coin-jar guessing game). Disclosure led advisers to make more money and estimators to make less than without disclosure. Professionals forced to disclose may feel "morally licensed" to be more self-serving.
- **Motivated blindness:** When people have an incentive not to notice data, they overlook it
  - Arthur Andersen / Enron: Auditors cannot be truly independent when they depend on clients for future contracts
  - SEC data: When NASDAQ lost 60% of its value, 99.1% of analyst recommendations were still "Strong Buy," "Buy," or "Hold"
  - MLB steroid scandal: Management turned a blind eye because artificially pumped-up players boosted attendance and revenue
  - Catholic Church abuse scandal: Cardinal Law's desire to protect the Church's reputation blinded him to clear evidence of repeat offenders
  - Credit-rating agencies in the 2008 crisis: Agencies paid by the companies they rate gave AAA ratings to risky mortgage securities
- **Remedies:** (1) Eliminate conflicts by avoiding biased advice sources, (2) recognize that honesty does not solve the problem -- even honest people are biased, (3) do not assume you or your adviser is immune

### Conclusion
- The chapter bridges fairness research (how we judge others' actions) and ethics research (how we judge our own behavior's consistency with our values)
- Most ethical failures stem from systematic, predictable cognitive biases, not deliberate corruption
- High self-regard keeps us from monitoring our own behavior, making bounded ethicality more likely
- Ethics training fails when it focuses only on explicit unethical behavior; it should address unconscious biases
- Understanding the unconscious aspects of bounded ethicality offers the best hope for improving individual and organizational ethics (Messick & Bazerman, 1996)

## Existing Concepts Updated
- [[framing-effects]] -- fairness judgments are susceptible to framing (money illusion, reference point anchoring on list prices)
- [[loss-aversion]] -- rejecting unfair offers despite economic loss; status quo as fairness reference point
- [[system-1-system-2]] -- implicit attitudes and bounded ethicality are System 1 phenomena; System 2 can override but requires time and motivation
- [[self-serving-bias-in-decisions]] -- overclaiming credit, self-serving fairness assessments, motivated blindness

## Contradictions or Tensions
- Economic rationality predicts accepting any positive offer in the ultimatum game; human behavior systematically contradicts this, even with very high stakes
- Disclosure is widely advocated as a solution to conflicts of interest, but research shows it can paradoxically increase bias (Cain et al., 2005)
- Fairness norms (e.g., 50/50 splits) can be both prosocial (preventing exploitation) and counter-productive (enabling manipulation by anchoring)

## Quotes Worth Keeping
> "For unethical behavior to legally qualify as fraud, the person engaging in such crimes must know at the time that she is behaving unethically. As always, the most effective lies are those we tell ourselves." -- Bazerman & Moore, citing von Hippel & Trivers, 2011
