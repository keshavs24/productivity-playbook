# SDT Ch.9: Causality Orientations Theory — Summary

**Author(s):** [[ryan-deci|Richard M. Ryan & Edward L. Deci]]
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 3
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways
1. **COT is the third mini-theory within SDT**, addressing individual differences in general motivational orientations -- the relatively stable ways people orient toward environments and regulate behavior
2. **Three causality orientations** -- [[autonomy-orientation]], [[control-orientation]], and [[impersonal-orientation]] -- represent personality-level propensities that influence how people interpret and respond to contexts
3. **Orientations are not types** -- everyone has all three to some degree; the relative strength of each orientation varies across individuals
4. **Causality orientations can be primed** -- experimental activation of autonomous or controlled orientations produces effects parallel to those found with the trait measures
5. **The autonomy orientation** is consistently associated with the healthiest outcomes: well-being, personality integration, openness, prosocial behavior, and effective performance
6. **The hierarchical model** (Vallerand, 1997) organizes motivation at three levels of generality: global (GCO), domain-specific (regulatory styles), and situation-specific
7. **Autonomy is not independence** -- reflective autonomy (volition, SDT sense) differs fundamentally from reactive autonomy (independence, defiance) with opposite correlates

## Detailed Notes

### General Causality Orientations

Deci and Ryan (1985a) proposed three general causality orientations (GCO) as individual-difference constructs:

#### Autonomy Orientation
- The degree to which people orient toward environments by treating them as sources of relevant information
- People take interest in both external events and accompanying inner experiences
- Involves experiencing choice with respect to one's actions and reactions
- When autonomy-oriented, people are "interest-taking" -- finding or creating opportunities for engagement with what they find important
- Tend to use identified and integrated styles of regulation, high intrinsic motivation
- Most associated with positive motivation, health, and wellness outcomes
- **Buffering effect:** Hagger and Chatzisarantis (2011) found people high in autonomy orientation did not show significant decrement in intrinsic motivation following externally controlled contingent rewards -- the individual difference buffered against reward undermining

#### Control Orientation
- The degree to which attention and concerns orient toward external contingencies and controls
- People experience social contexts in terms of rewards and social pressures they must either comply with or defy
- Lose sight of their own values or interests in the process
- Tend to use external and introjected styles of regulation, low intrinsic motivation
- Acutely occupied with "what others might think" and external judgments
- Not reliably linked to positive wellness outcomes
- **More vulnerable to undermining:** Hagger and Chatzisarantis (2011) found intrinsic motivation of people high in control orientation was more readily undermined by tangible rewards

#### Impersonal Orientation
- The degree to which people orient toward obstacles to goal attainment, experience anxiety and incompetence, and react to their lack of control
- Drawn from Heider's (1958) concept of impersonal causality
- People tend to lack intentionality, initiative, and sense of personal causation
- Relatively passive and easily overwhelmed by environmental forces and internal drives
- The least healthy and effective orientation -- salient when people have lost sense of volition, intentionality, and engagement
- Develops through considerable degree of unpredictable thwarting of basic psychological needs
- Often fosters amotivation or akrasia

### COT Propositions

**COT Proposition I:** People have three motivational orientations -- autonomy, controlled, and impersonal -- representing global-level individual differences. These affect people's situation-specific motivation, general need satisfaction, behavior, and experience.

**COT Proposition II:** Causality orientations are developmental outcomes influenced by biological and social-contextual factors impacting need satisfaction. Persistently autonomy-supportive, controlling, or amotivating environments lead to strong autonomy, controlled, and impersonal orientations respectively.

**COT Proposition III:** Causality orientations affect effectiveness and well-being, mediated by domain- or situation-specific motivations and need satisfactions. Autonomy orientation promotes integration and effective performance; controlled orientation promotes introjection and rigidity; impersonal orientation promotes ineffectance and amotivation.

**COT Proposition IV:** All individuals have all three orientations to some degree. Subtle environmental cues can prime different orientations, significantly affecting behavior and experience.

### Assessing General Causality Orientations

**General Causality Orientations Scale (GCOS):** Deci and Ryan (1985a); validated by Vallerand, Blais, Lacouture, and Deci (1987). Gives three subscale scores -- one for each orientation. Can be used separately or together for predictions.

The three GCOS subscales comprise tendencies toward: (1) intrinsic motivation and well-internalized extrinsic motivation; (2) external and introjected regulations; and (3) amotivation and lack of intention.

**Distinct from Big Five (FFM):** Olesen et al. (2010, 2011) confirmed causality orientations are empirically distinct from FFM personality factors. Autonomy and control were particularly distinct from FFM traits; impersonal overlapped somewhat with neuroticism. Causality orientations explained additional variance over FFM.

### Why Study Individual Differences?

From SDT's perspective, individual differences in causality orientations provide accounts of:
- Why some people act autonomously in controlling contexts
- Why some people are controlled even in autonomy-supportive contexts
- How people differentially seek out, interpret, and change contexts consistent with their orientations

People actively interpret and give psychological meaning to contexts rather than passively responding -- causality orientations are a key factor in these interpretations.

### Development and Causality Orientations

Causality orientations are both **outcomes of** and **inputs to** the organismic integration process. A bidirectional, self-reinforcing cycle:
- People who have had autonomy supported develop strong autonomy orientation
- Strong autonomy orientation leads them to interpret new contexts more congruently and openly
- This furthers autonomy development
- Parallel dynamics for controlled and impersonal orientations

### Autonomy Orientation: Empirical Results

- Positively correlated with **self-esteem, ego development, and self-actualization** (Deci & Ryan, 1985a)
- Related to adopting **learning goals** rather than performance goals (Koestner & Zuckerman, 1994)
- Related to low boredom, careful career decision-making (Farmer & Sundberg, 1986; Blustein, 1989)
- Related to focusing on interest and challenge at work (Amabile et al., 1994)
- Connected to **informational identity style** -- actively constructing identity through experimentation and evaluation (Soenens et al., 2005)
- Related to people's tendency to support others' autonomy (Deci & Ryan, 1985a)
- Cardiac patients high on autonomy orientation viewed surgery as challenge rather than threat (King, 1984)
- In romantic relationships, higher autonomy associated with less negative emotion and more positive behaviors (Knee et al., 2002)
- Mothers of securely attached infants higher on autonomy orientation; mothers of resistant infants higher on controlled; mothers of avoidant infants higher on impersonal (Bridges et al., 1983)
- Prosocial behavior predicted by autonomy orientation (Gagne, 2003)
- Neighbors and Knee (2003): People high in autonomy maintained positive affect regardless of social comparison outcomes; those low in autonomy showed negative affect after unfavorable comparisons
- Weinstein and Hodgins (2009): After exposure to disturbing films, those high in autonomy orientation showed better coping, more ownership of feelings, lower energy costs

### Controlled Orientation: Empirical Results

- Positively correlated with **public self-consciousness and Type-A personality** (Deci & Ryan, 1985a)
- Linked to **Machiavellianism** (McHoskey, 1999)
- Associated with **cheating** when given opportunity (Lonky & Reihman, 1990)
- Preference for **image-based advertising** over quality-based (Zuckerman, Gioioso, & Tellini, 1988)
- High value on **wealth** relative to intrinsic aspirations (Kasser & Ryan, 1993)
- Related to **normative identity style** -- relying on expectations of others (Soenens et al., 2005)
- Students high in control adopt **performance goals** and persist rigidly in ego-involved ways after failure (Koestner & Zuckerman, 1994)
- Associated with **gambling, alcohol problems,** and disordered gambling (Neighbors & Larimer, 2004; Neighbors, Larimer, Geisner, & Knee, 2004)
- Linked to **road rage, aggressive driving, traffic citations** (Knee, Neighbors, & Vietor, 2001; Neighbors, Vietor, & Knee, 2002)
- Connected to **interpersonal aggression** and tendency to dehumanize others (Moller & Deci, 2010)
- Sports parents higher in controlled orientation more prone to anger and aggression at children's events (Goldstein & Iso-Ahola, 2008)

### Impersonal Orientation: Empirical Results

- Positively related to **social anxiety, public self-consciousness, self-derogation, depressive symptoms, lower self-esteem** (Deci & Ryan, 1985a)
- Related to lower ego development (Loevinger, 1976) and **powerlessness/self-estrangement** (McHoskey, 1999)
- Related to **performance goals with low confidence** (Koestner & Zuckerman, 1994)
- Associated with **diffuse-avoidant identity style** -- procrastination, denial, maladaptive coping (Soenens et al., 2005; Duriez, Soenens, & Beyers, 2004)
- Women with restrictive anorexia nervosa significantly higher on impersonal orientation (Strauss & Ryan, 1987)
- Individuals with schizophrenia showed lower autonomy and higher impersonal orientations (Cooper, Lavaysse, & Gard, 2015, GCOS-CP)

### Locus of Control vs. Locus of Causality

Important conceptual distinction:
- **Locus of control** (Rotter, 1966): whether one *can* attain desired outcomes -- a capacity belief (internal = "I can control outcomes" vs. external = "I cannot")
- **Perceived locus of causality** (PLOC; de Charms, 1968; Heider, 1958): the degree to which one sees oneself as the *source and endorser* of behavior -- a motivational quality
- External locus of control aligns with impersonal locus of causality (both involve inability to attain outcomes)
- But internal locus of control does NOT map cleanly to either internal or external PLOC -- a person high in internal locus of control could be autonomously or controllingly motivated

### Causality Orientations and Need Satisfaction

High autonomy orientation hypothesized to facilitate greater need satisfaction, independent of contextual autonomy support. Baard, Deci, and Ryan (2004): In work organizations, both autonomy orientation (from GCOS) and autonomy support (from Work Climate Questionnaire) independently predicted need satisfaction, and need satisfaction predicted performance and well-being.

Lam and Gurland (2008): Autonomy orientation predicted autonomous work motivation and job satisfaction/commitment.

### Open Engagement and Social Experiences

Knee and Zuckerman (1996): People high in autonomy displayed less self-serving bias, less self-handicapping, less avoidant coping.

Hodgins et al. (1996): Students high in autonomy were more honest and disclosing, reported more pleasant affect, and felt better about themselves in interactions with parents and others.

Hodgins and colleagues (2003): More autonomous people accepted greater responsibility for wrongdoings, provided more complex apologies, used fewer lies.

People high in autonomy have **higher quality relationships** -- autonomy and relatedness are compatible, not antagonistic (contra Gilligan, 1982; Jordan, 1991).

### Reflective vs. Reactive Autonomy

Critical distinction (Koestner & Losier, 1996):
- **Reflective autonomy** (SDT): volition, sense of choice -- measured by GCOS autonomy subscale
- **Reactive autonomy** (independence): resistance to influence, detachment -- measured by ACL Autonomy/Independence subscale

These two forms have opposite correlates:
- Reflective autonomy: positive daily events, positive mood, pleasant peer interactions, following credible expert advice
- Reactive autonomy: negative daily events, negative mood, negative authority interactions, rejecting credible expert advice
- Reactive autonomy (independence) is incompatible with positive relationships; reflective autonomy (volition) is compatible

### Causality Orientations and Self-Regulation

Causality orientations and regulatory styles represent **different levels of generality**:
- GCOs are global, personality-level orientations
- Regulatory styles are domain- or behavior-specific
- Both predict behavior, but domain-specific measures typically predict domain-specific outcomes more strongly

Williams and Deci (1996): Medical students' GCOS predicted autonomous regulation for studying course material independent of instructor autonomy-supportiveness.

Williams et al. (1996): In weight-loss program, both autonomous individual difference and interpersonal autonomy support independently predicted autonomous motivation, attendance, and maintained weight loss.

### The Hierarchical Model

Vallerand (1997; Vallerand & Ratelle, 2002) proposed three levels of generality:
1. **Global level** -- GCOs representing general motivational tendencies
2. **Domain-specific level** -- regulatory styles for particular domains (athletics, academics, relationships)
3. **Situation-specific level** -- motivation for a particular activity at a particular time and place

Features:
- Three motivation types at each level: autonomous, controlled, and amotivation
- Motivation at each level determined by social context at that level and motivation at the next higher level
- Influence flows both top-down and bottom-up developmentally
- Consequences (performance, affect, physical symptoms) also occur at different levels of generality
- Best prediction when motivational predictors and outcomes are assessed at the same level

### Priming Motivational Orientations

**COT Proposition IV:** All individuals have all three orientations. Subtle cues can prime different orientations, significantly affecting behavior.

**Levesque and Pelletier (2003):** Used scrambled sentence method to prime autonomy (words: interested, involved, challenge, satisfied, mastering) or control (obligation, evaluated, constrained, demanded, forced). Autonomy-primed participants showed more intrinsic motivation, interest, perceived choice, and better puzzle performance. Primes had stronger effect on participants without strong chronic orientations; main effect for motivational chronicity also present.

**Hodgins, Yacko, and Gottlieb (2006):** Primed autonomous, controlled, and impersonal orientations. Results:
- Autonomy-primed: low defense (least desire to escape), least self-serving attributions, least self-handicapping
- Control-primed: moderate defense, somewhat self-serving, moderate self-handicapping
- Impersonally-primed: high defense, most self-serving, most self-handicapping
- Paralleled findings with GCOS trait measures (Knee & Zuckerman, 1996, 1998)

**Hodgins, Brown, and Carver (2007):** Primed autonomy led to higher self-esteem; primed control decreased self-esteem.

**Hodgins et al. (2010):** Autonomy primes led to less defensiveness and better performance in a stressful interview task (assessed via behavioral coding, word choice, physiological measures). Links from autonomy primes to speech performance mediated by level of defensiveness.

**Niemiec (2010):** Contextual cues regarding autonomy and control affect new relationship development. Controlling conditions (monetary payment, ego involvement) led to less closeness, less positive affect, less need satisfaction. Autonomy-supportive conditions (choice, reflection, rationale) increased relationship satisfaction, positive affect, emotional reliance, and well-being.

### Autonomy and Integration

Koestner, Bernieri, and Zuckerman (1992): Separated high-autonomy from high-control groups and examined consistency:
- Autonomy-oriented individuals: correlation between free-choice behavior and self-reported interest > .6; strong behavior-trait correlations; higher correlation between self-ratings and peer ratings
- Control-oriented individuals: near-zero correlation between behavior and self-reported interest; lower behavior-trait and self-peer correlations
- Demonstrated that autonomy is associated with **greater personality integration** -- more congruence among attitudes, behaviors, feelings, and perceptions

Weinstein, Deci, and Ryan (2011): In five experiments, people high in autonomy (or primed with autonomy) accepted both positive AND negative past experiences as part of their current identity. Those high in control (or control-primed) accepted only positive past aspects and rejected/compartmentalized negative ones -- demonstrating that autonomy is associated with more integrated self-knowledge.

### Concluding Comments

GCOs reflect individual differences in general motivational tendencies. They align in parallel with regulatory styles: autonomy orientation with intrinsic, integrated, and identified regulation; controlled orientation with introjected and external regulation; impersonal orientation with amotivation. They also parallel three types of social contexts: autonomy-supportive, controlling, and amotivating. These three parallel sets are organized in the hierarchical model (Vallerand, 1997).

The autonomy orientation is associated with healthiest development and greatest personality integration; controlled orientation with rigid functioning and defensiveness; impersonal orientation with amotivation and poorest well-being.

## Contradictions or Tensions
- The relationship between autonomy and relatedness appears contradictory on the surface (autonomy could suggest detachment) but SDT demonstrates they are compatible -- the key is distinguishing reflective autonomy (volition) from reactive autonomy (independence)

## Quotes Worth Keeping
> "It is thus clear that when autonomy is defined as independence (reactive autonomy), it is incompatible with positive relationships, but when it is defined as volition (reflective autonomy), as is the case in SDT, it is quite compatible with positive relationships." -- Ryan & Deci, Ch.9
