# Emotional Intelligence — Summary

**Author(s):** Peter Salovey, John D. Mayer
**Type:** Paper (journal article, *Imagination, Cognition and Personality*, 1990)
**Phase:** 1
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. **Emotional intelligence is a legitimate subset of social intelligence** -- defined as "the ability to monitor one's own and others' feelings and emotions, to discriminate among them and to use this information to guide one's thinking and actions." It is not a contradiction in terms; emotions organize and adaptively focus cognition.
2. **The EI framework has three branches:** (a) appraisal and expression of emotion, (b) regulation of emotion, and (c) utilization of emotion in solving problems. These branches are further divided into sub-processes targeting self vs. others.
3. **Emotions are organized responses, not disorganized interruptions.** Against the Western tradition that views emotions as chaotic, Salovey and Mayer align with functionalist theories: emotions arise in response to events, cross physiological/cognitive/motivational/experiential subsystems, and adaptively redirect behavior.
4. **Individual differences in EI components are measurable** -- through scales for alexithymia (inability to appraise/express emotion verbally), nonverbal perception accuracy, empathy, mood regulation, and affect-facilitated cognition.
5. **Emotion facilitates thinking in concrete ways:** flexible planning (mood swings generate multiple future scenarios), creative thinking (positive mood broadens associative networks), redirected attention (emotion re-prioritizes processing), and motivation (mood sustains persistence on challenging tasks).
6. **EI has direct implications for mental health.** Deficits in emotional intelligence -- inability to appraise, express, or regulate emotions -- map onto clinical conditions including alexithymia, depression, and sociopathy. The emotionally intelligent person is not simply pleasant but engages in sophisticated self-regulation that includes tolerating painful emotions in service of growth.

## Detailed Notes

### I. The Problem: Is "Emotional Intelligence" a Contradiction?

The Western intellectual tradition has long treated emotion and intelligence as opposites. Emotions were characterized as "acute disturbances," "disorganized responses," and "complete loss of cerebral control." But a second tradition (Leeper, 1948; modern appraisal theories) views emotions as **organizing responses** that adaptively focus cognitive activities and direct action. Salovey and Mayer adopt this functionalist view: emotions are organized, cross multiple psychological subsystems, and can enrich experience.

### II. Defining Emotions

- Emotions are **organized responses** crossing physiological, cognitive, motivational, and experiential systems
- They arise in response to events (internal or external) with positive or negative valence
- Distinguished from **moods**: emotions are shorter and more intense
- The authors view emotions as **adaptive** -- potentially transforming personal and social interaction

### III. Emotional Intelligence and Its Relationship to Other Intelligences

**Intelligence vs. models of intelligence:** The authors make a critical distinction. Intelligence (broadly, per Wechsler) is "the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment." *Models* of intelligence (e.g., Spearman's "g") are more restrictive organizational schemes. EI can be a valid intelligence without necessarily conforming to any particular model.

**Social intelligence:** EI is positioned as a subset of social intelligence (Thorndike, 1920: "the ability to understand and manage people"). Social intelligence has a troubled measurement history -- Cronbach (1960) called it "undefined and unmeasured" after fifty years of research. Problems: social intelligence scales bled into verbal/spatial intelligence; operationalizations were often manipulative ("getting others to do what you want"). Salovey and Mayer argue EI avoids these pitfalls by focusing on emotional processes specifically.

**Gardner's personal intelligences:** EI maps closely onto Gardner's (1983) concept of personal intelligences (intrapersonal + interpersonal). Gardner's intrapersonal intelligence involves "access to one's own feeling life -- one's range of affects or emotions: the capacity instantly to effect discriminations among these feelings and, eventually, to label them, to enmesh them in symbolic codes, to draw upon them as a means of understanding and guiding one's behavior."

### IV. Emotional Intelligence: Conceptualization and Scope

The authors' formal definition: EI involves *the ability to monitor one's own and others' feelings and emotions, to discriminate among them and to use this information to guide one's thinking and actions.*

**The three-branch model (Figure 1):**

```
                    EMOTIONAL INTELLIGENCE
                    /          |          \
        APPRAISAL AND     REGULATION     UTILIZATION
        EXPRESSION OF     OF EMOTION     OF EMOTION
           EMOTION        /      \       /    |    \    \
          /     \      In Self  In Other  Flexible Creative Redirected Motivation
       Self    Other                      Planning Thinking Attention
      / | \      \
  Verbal NV  NV    Empathy
            Perception
```

Three mental processes:
1. **Appraising and expressing emotions** in the self and others
2. **Regulating emotion** in the self and others
3. **Utilizing emotions** in adaptive ways (flexible planning, creative thinking, redirected attention, motivation)

### V. Appraisal and Expression of Emotion

#### Emotion in the Self

**Verbal appraisal:** Learning about emotions depends on introspection and speaking clearly about them. Key dimensions: Pleasant-Unpleasant, Arousal-Calm. Individual differences exist in *ability* to appraise and express emotions, not just style.

**[[alexithymia]]:** A clinical concept -- psychiatric patients unable to appraise and then verbally express their emotions. May involve disconnection between limbic systems and higher cortical activity. Measured via the Beth Israel Hospital Psychosomatic Questionnaire, State and Trait Meta-Mood Scales (SMMS, TMMS), and Toronto Alexithymia Scale. Problems with existing alexithymia measures: most focus on negative emotions only and ignore positive/mixed/neutral states.

**Nonverbal expression:** Much emotional communication occurs through nonverbal channels (Darwin, 1872). Individual differences exist in "nonverbal sending accuracy." Measured via Affect Expression Rating Scale, Affective Communication Test. Emotional expressiveness correlates with [[empathy]] and inversely with depression.

#### Emotion in Others

**Nonverbal perception:** Evolutionarily important to perceive emotions in others. Measured via Affect Sensitivity Test, Communication of Affect Receiving Ability Test (CARAT), Profile of Nonverbal Sensitivity (PONS). Findings: women generally better at recognizing facial emotions (except anger perception); CARAT scores higher among artists than scientists; correlates with Rotter's interpersonal trust scale. Buck concluded nonverbal receiving ability "is not a unidimensional construct."

**[[empathy]]:** The ability to comprehend another's feelings and re-experience them oneself. Rogers saw empathy as a prerequisite for helping others grow. Empathy is a motivator for altruistic behavior. Developmental contributors to empathy: (a) *primary circular reactions* (infant cries in response to other infants), (b) *classical empathic conditioning* (learning situational determinants of affect by observing others' reactions). Empathy scales may measure not only the ability to feel toward others but also general access to one's own feelings.

### VI. Regulation of Emotion

#### Regulation of Emotion in the Self

**Meta-experiences of mood:** A regulatory system that monitors, evaluates, and sometimes acts to change mood. Measured by State and Trait Meta-Mood Scales (SMMS, TMMS). Co-occurrence of mood with meta-experience provides data for building personal "rules" about what brings about certain moods.

**Mood regulation strategies:**
- Choosing associates strategically (associating with non-threatening successful people for positive affect; avoiding threatening comparators)
- Seeking self-evaluation maintenance information
- Acting helpfully toward others ("negative state relief" model of altruism)
- Augmenting negative moods with countervailing positive evaluations (e.g., viewing a bad mood as "under control and soon-to-change" reduces its destructiveness)
- Direct mood modification through the asymmetry of affect and memory: people are more motivated to maintain positive moods and repair negative ones ("mood maintenance" and "mood repair")
- Using conscious (controlled) mechanisms to counteract automatic negative associations

**Aesthetic experience and emotion regulation:** People seek emotional experiences of all kinds, including sorrow (attending tragedies, reading fiction). These aesthetic experiences may function as practice for feeling negative affect with little consequence, building emotional tolerance. "One must experience sorrow, at least temporarily, in order to feel joy."

#### Regulation of Emotion in Others

An emotionally intelligent orator can elicit strong reactions in an audience. Job candidates manage emotional impressions through promptness and dress. Goffman's impression management; Hochschild's "managed heart" (commercialization of emotional labor). Jones studied emotional regulation through ingratiation. Wasielewski theorized charisma as emotional regulation of followers by leaders.

**Dual-edged nature:** Emotionally intelligent individuals may enhance others' moods and motivate charismatically toward worthwhile ends. But those whose skills are channeled antisocially "may create manipulative scenes or lead others sociopathically to nefarious ends."

### VII. Utilizing Emotional Intelligence

Moods and emotions subtly but systematically influence problem-solving components:

**Flexible Planning:** Individuals differ in mood swing frequency/amplitude. Stronger mood swings produce concomitant changes in likelihood estimates of future events (optimism in good moods, pessimism in bad). This "breaking set" helps generate a wider variety of possible outcomes and future plans.

**Creative Thinking:** Positive mood facilitates categorizing features as related; enhances performance on remote associates tasks and Duncker's candle task; happy people more likely to give unusual/creative first associates to neutral cues; more likely to discover category organizing principles and use them to integrate information (Isen et al.).

**Redirected Attention:** When powerful emotions occur, attention is directed to new problems of greater immediate importance. Rather than merely disrupting cognition, affect helps individuals reprioritize internal and external demands and allocate attentional resources accordingly.

**Motivating Emotions:** Mood can sustain persistence on challenging tasks. Anxiety from evaluative situations can motivate more thorough preparation. Imagining negative outcomes can motivate performance. Good moods increase confidence in capabilities. Positive attitudes construct interpersonal experiences that lead to better outcomes.

### VIII. Conclusions and Implications

**EI and health:** The emotionally intelligent person is aware of feelings in self and others, open to positive and negative experience, able to label and communicate emotions appropriately. This leads to effective regulation of affect, contributing to well-being. The emotionally intelligent person is "a pleasure to be around and leaves others feeling better." But this is not mindless pleasure-seeking -- it involves attending to emotion in the path toward growth, tolerating painful feelings for greater objectives, and self-regulation that sometimes requires "sacrifice and emotional toughness."

**Deficits in EI:** People who cannot regulate their own emotions become "slaves" to them. Those who cannot recognize emotions in others are perceived as "cloddish or oafish." Sociopaths over-regulate mood in others for selfish purposes. Inability to recognize one's own emotions leads to "lives of unrewarded experience" and potentially depression or suicide.

**The utility question:** For the EI framework to be useful, the component skills need not intercorrelate (just as attention, memory, and metacognition are independent but collectively constitute cognition). What matters is that the skills (a) involve emotional processing and (b) are necessary for a minimum level of competent, intelligent functioning.

**Future research directions:** Examine emotions in the self; appraisal of others' emotions; regulation mechanisms; adaptive uses of emotion; develop identification methods (laboratory tasks, conventional scales); design interventions to promote EI skills; study EI's role in friendships and close relationships.

## Contradictions or Tensions

- **Emotion as organized vs. disorganized:** This paper directly challenges the traditional Western view (still present in introductory psychology texts at time of writing) that emotions are "disorganized responses" and "complete loss of cerebral control." Salovey and Mayer argue this is fundamentally wrong -- emotions are organized, cross-system responses that serve adaptive functions.
- **EI as prosocial vs. manipulative:** The authors acknowledge that EI skills can be used antisocially -- for manipulation, ingratiation, and sociopathic exploitation. The framework itself is value-neutral; it describes capacities, not virtues.
- **Social intelligence measurement failure:** Despite decades of research, social intelligence remained "undefined and unmeasured" (Cronbach, 1960). The authors argue EI avoids these problems by narrowing the focus to emotional processes, but acknowledge that EI's measurement is still in early stages.
