# Judgment in Managerial Decision Making Ch.9 — Common Investment Mistakes

**Author(s):** Max H. Bazerman, Don A. Moore
**Type:** Book chapter (8th Ed, Ch.9, pp.159-175)
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. Index funds consistently outperform the vast majority of actively managed mutual funds and hedge funds, yet most investors persist in paying high fees for active management due to [[overconfidence-in-calibration]] and optimism bias
2. Five core biases systematically distort investment decisions: overconfidence, optimism, denial of randomness, anchoring/status quo/procrastination, and prospect theory effects (selling winners too early, holding losers too long)
3. The "disposition effect" -- investors sell winning stocks too early (locking in gains) and hold losing stocks too long (avoiding booking losses) -- is a direct consequence of [[prospect-theory]] and [[loss-aversion]]
4. Daytrading is a particularly destructive form of overconfidence: the typical daytrader underperforms the market by 3+ percentage points and faces catastrophic personal financial consequences
5. The single most actionable piece of investment advice from behavioral finance: formulate a long-term asset allocation plan, invest in low-cost index funds, and stop paying attention to the market

## Detailed Notes

### Why Active Management Fails

The Vanguard Index 500 fund has outperformed about 75% of actively managed mutual funds each year. The reason is straightforward:
- Active fund fees are often 2% annually vs. 0.2% for index funds
- Active funds have higher brokerage costs from frequent trading
- The aggregate of all active funds, before fees, roughly matches the market by definition (Sharpe, 1991)
- Past performance is not a reliable predictor of future performance

**Hedge funds fare no better.** Kat and Palaro (2006) found only 18% of nearly 2,000 hedge funds outperformed their benchmarks. The standard "two and twenty" fee structure (2% of assets + 20% of gains) consumes most returns. Survivorship bias (failed funds disappear from databases) makes even these poor results look better than reality (Malkiel & Saha, 2005).

**The probability illusion:** With ~8,000 mutual funds, ~2,000 will beat the market in any given year by chance alone. Of those, ~500 will beat it again, and ~125 a third year. This creates the illusion of consistently skilled managers when the pattern is largely random.

### The Psychology of Poor Investment Decisions

Five categories of bias affect investors:

#### 1. Overconfidence Produces Excessive Trading

Overconfidence in one's ability to predict market direction leads to excessive trading. Barber and Odean (2000) studied 66,465 households:
- Average portfolio turnover was 75% annually
- The 20% of accounts with highest turnover earned returns of 16.4% during a booming market -- vs. 17.9% for the overall market
- Highest-turnover investors lost 6.5 percentage points relative to the market

**Gender differences (Barber & Odean, 2001):** Examining 35,000 accounts, women achieved better returns than men -- not because they picked better stocks, but because they traded less. Male turnover averaged 77% annually vs. 53% for women. The added transaction costs from men's more frequent trades (driven by greater overconfidence) wiped out returns.

#### 2. Optimism about Investment Decisions

Moore, Kurtzberg, Fox, and Bazerman (1999) created an investment simulation using 10 real funds over 10 years:
- Starting with $100,000, the optimal strategy (all in index fund) would have grown to $380,041
- The average participant ended with $349,620 due to choosing too many active funds with high fees
- Participants consistently predicted their portfolios would grow faster than they actually did (predicted 8.13% per half-year vs. actual 5.50%)
- At the end, participants believed they had matched the market -- they had actually underperformed by 8%
- They overstated their performance by one entire level on a 7-point scale (Goetzmann & Peles, 1997 found similar results)

Most investors never compare their returns to market benchmarks, protecting their optimistic illusions.

#### 3. Denying that Random Events are Random

Investors see patterns in randomness, treating "hot" funds as skillful rather than lucky. This is the same [[gamblers-fallacy]] that creates belief in the "hot hand" in basketball.

**Regression to the mean in investing:** Bogle (1994), Carhart (1997), DeBondt and Thaler (1992) all found fund performance regresses toward the mean. Study participants expected "hot" funds to stay hot -- their expectations were actually *negatively* correlated with actual returns.

**DeBondt and Thaler (1985):** "Loser" portfolios (extreme losers from the past 3 years) dramatically outperformed "winner" portfolios over the following 5 years, because investors overbuy past winners and oversell past losers.

#### 4. Anchoring, the Status Quo, and Procrastination

- **Naive diversification:** Benartzi and Thaler (2001) found professors allocated retirement funds roughly 50:50 between TIAA (bonds) and CREF (stock), never adjusting. When firms offered 4 funds (3 stock, 1 bond), employees put 75% in stock. One stock fund + three bond funds? 75% in bonds. People diversify across *categories offered* like picking one dish from each column in a Chinese restaurant.
- **Status quo bias:** Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988) found that when told they *inherited* an investment, participants overwhelmingly kept it rather than switching to a better-suited option
- **Procrastination:** Automatic enrollment in 401(k) plans increased initial participation from 49% to 86% (Madrian & Shea, 2001). People know they should invest but procrastinate on allocation decisions while paradoxically being overactive in trading within categories.

#### 5. Prospect Theory, Selling Winners, and Keeping Losers

**The disposition effect (Odean, 1998):** Investors have a strong preference to hold stocks below purchase price (avoiding "becoming losers") and sell stocks above purchase price ("becoming winners").

The reference point is the initial purchase price. From [[prospect-theory]]:
- Stocks above purchase price = gains domain = risk averse = sell to lock in the sure gain
- Stocks below purchase price = loss domain = risk seeking = hold, hoping for recovery

This is irrational for three reasons:
1. High transaction costs from frequent selling
2. Winners that are sold tend to outperform losers that are kept (Odean, 1999)
3. Tax disadvantage: selling winners creates capital gains tax; selling losers creates tax benefits

Calvet, Campbell, and Sodini (2009): investors become MORE eager to sell winners as performance improves -- the bias intensifies with success.

### Active Trading / Daytrading

Online trading costs fell 75% between 1996-1998, enabling a wave of daytrading. Jordan and Diltz (2003) studied 324 daytraders during the 1998-1999 bubble: only 36% made money, and nearly all profits were short-term capital gains taxed at up to 35%.

Key insight: "When a daytrader is buying a stock, it is because someone else has sold it." The counterparty is typically an institutional investor with better information, more experience, and faster hardware. Daytrading is systematically disadvantageous.

### Action Steps

1. **Determine investment goals** -- define what you need and when you need it
2. **Maximize tax-advantaged savings** -- always contribute enough to get full employer match
3. **Formulate an asset allocation plan** -- long-term investors should weight stocks more heavily (Benartzi & Thaler, 2001)
4. **Invest in low-cost index funds** -- avoid loads, minimize expense ratios
5. **Invest regularly** -- consistent contributions, then ignore the market
6. **Stop paying attention** -- "there is little reason to be thinking about your investments more than a few hours per year"

### Why the Market is Hard to Predict

Keynes's beauty contest analogy: investing is not about picking the best stocks but about predicting what other investors will think are the best stocks -- a recursive problem with no stable solution.
