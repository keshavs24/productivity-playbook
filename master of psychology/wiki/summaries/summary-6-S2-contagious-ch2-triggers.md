# Contagious Ch.2: Triggers — Summary

**Author:** Jonah Berger
**Source:** *Contagious: Why Things Catch On* (2013), Chapter 2
**Phase:** 6 — Advanced Persuasion
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-15

## Key Takeaways

1. **Interesting products get more *immediate* word of mouth, but not more *ongoing* word of mouth.** Interestingness alone fades quickly. What sustains ongoing buzz is being frequently triggered by the environment.
2. **Top of mind leads to tip of tongue.** People talk about whatever is most accessible in their minds, and environmental stimuli (sights, smells, sounds) constantly shift what is top of mind. [[environmental-triggers]] are the workhorse of ongoing word of mouth.
3. **Triggers are more effective when they are frequent and strongly linked.** Coffee is a better trigger for Kit Kat than hot chocolate because people drink coffee far more often. But the link must also be strong — associating a product with too many things dilutes each individual association.
4. **Triggers should be positioned where the desired behavior happens.** The "Man Drinks Fat" antisoda PSA triggered its message right when people were about to drink soda. A clever bath-mat PSA triggered its message in a bathroom — where you can't buy a bath mat.
5. **Negative publicity can increase sales** if it triggers awareness of a product or idea that people wouldn't have otherwise remembered. A scathing *New York Times* review increased sales of books by new/unknown authors by 45%.

## Detailed Notes

### Cheerios vs. Disney World
Disney World is far more interesting, exciting, and emotionally engaging than Cheerios. Yet Cheerios generates more word of mouth. The reason is [[environmental-triggers]]: hundreds of thousands of people eat Cheerios every day, see the bright orange boxes in the cereal aisle, and are thus frequently triggered to think about and mention the brand. Disney World visits are rare and have few environmental reminders.

Twitter data confirms: Cheerios mentions spike every weekday between 5:00 and 8:00 a.m. (breakfast time) and shift slightly later on weekends. Triggers drive talking.

### BzzAgent and the Interest Paradox
BzzAgent is a word-of-mouth marketing firm with 800,000+ agents who try products and naturally share them in conversations. [[berger]] and colleague Eric Schwartz analyzed hundreds of BzzAgent campaigns to test whether more interesting products get talked about more.

The surprising finding: **interest, novelty, and surprise had no relationship to overall word of mouth.** More interesting products got more *immediate* word of mouth but did not sustain higher levels of ongoing word of mouth over time. The distinction between immediate and ongoing word of mouth is critical — for most products, ongoing word of mouth matters more.

### How Triggers Affect Behavior
**Accessible thoughts lead to action.** At any given moment, some thoughts are more top of mind than others. Environmental stimuli — sights, smells, sounds — constantly shift which thoughts are accessible.

**Mars bars and NASA's Pathfinder:** In mid-1997, Mars candy company noticed an unexpected spike in sales. They hadn't changed anything. What happened? NASA's Pathfinder mission had landed on Mars (the planet). Media coverage about "Mars" acted as a trigger that reminded people of the candy brand.

**French/German music and wine sales:** Researchers Adrian North, David Hargreaves, and Jennifer McKendrick played French or German music in a supermarket and measured wine purchases. French music led to more French wine purchased. German music led to more German wine. The music triggered thoughts about the respective countries, which spilled over into purchasing behavior.

**Fruits and vegetables study:** Berger and psychologist Grainne Fitzsimons gave college students a slogan — either "Live the healthy way, eat five fruits and veggies a day" or "Each and every dining-hall tray needs five fruits and veggies a day." Students who saw the "tray" slogan were significantly more likely to eat fruits and vegetables, because the dining-hall trays they encountered daily triggered the message. The "live healthy" group showed no behavior change. The trigger mattered more than the slogan's appeal — students actually rated the tray slogan as less attractive.

**Voting location study:** [[berger]], Marc Meredith, and Christian Wheeler found that people assigned to vote in schools were significantly more likely to support a school-funding ballot initiative than those voting in churches, firehouses, or other locations. The school environment triggered school-related thoughts. More than ten thousand more people voted in favor when the polling place was a school. The effect persisted after controlling for regional political preferences and demographics.

### Rebecca Black's "Friday"
Rebecca Black's universally panned song "Friday" became one of the most viral videos of 2011 (300+ million views). YouTube search data showed a consistent pattern: spikes every Friday. The day of the week acted as a recurring trigger, driving weekly surges in interest despite the song being equally terrible every day.

### Triggered to Talk
Most conversations are small talk — not carefully curated performances but casual exchanges driven by whatever is accessible. If something is top of mind because of environmental cues, people talk about it. This is why mundane, frequently triggered products like Ziploc bags and moisturizer generate lots of ongoing word of mouth — daily activities (wrapping leftovers, applying moisturizer) keep them top of mind.

More frequently triggered products got 15 percent more word of mouth in the BzzAgent data. Triggers not only generate more immediate word of mouth but also more ongoing word of mouth. Top of mind means tip of tongue.

### Kit Kat and Coffee — Growing the Habitat
In 2007, Colleen Chorak was tasked with reviving the Kit Kat brand at Hershey. The "give me a break" campaign had fizzled. Chorak researched when people eat Kit Kats: during breaks, often with coffee. She linked Kit Kat to coffee in a new campaign — "Kit Kat and coffee." Radio spots repeatedly paired the two. **Within a year, sales rose 8%. After twelve months, sales were up by a third.** The $300-million brand grew to $500 million.

Coffee is a particularly effective trigger because it is *frequent* — people drink it multiple times a day, year-round. Hot chocolate would have been a flavorful match but a weaker trigger (seasonal, less frequent).

**Growing the habitat** means expanding the set of triggers that remind people of your product. Berger's research with BzzAgent and Boston Market showed that messages associating Boston Market with dinner (not just lunch) increased word of mouth by 20% among people who previously only associated the brand with lunch.

**Poison parasite strategy:** Making a rival's message act as a trigger for your own. An antismoking campaign spoofed Marlboro's iconic cowboys ("Bob, I've got emphysema"), hijacking Marlboro ads as triggers for the antismoking message.

### What Makes an Effective Trigger
1. **Frequency:** How often does the trigger stimulus occur? Coffee (daily, multiple times) beats hot chocolate (seasonal, occasional). "Weekends are made for Michelob" was far more successful than "Holidays are made for Michelob."
2. **Strength of the link:** Fewer, stronger associations beat many weak ones. "Red" triggers too many things to be strong for any single one. "Peanut butter" strongly triggers "jelly."
3. **Proximity to desired behavior:** The trigger should fire where and when the behavior happens. A bath-mat PSA in the shower is memorable but useless — you can't buy a bath mat in a bathroom.

### Negative Publicity as a Trigger
Economists Alan Sorensen, Scott Rasmussen, and [[berger]] analyzed hundreds of *New York Times* book reviews. Negative reviews hurt sales for well-known authors. But for books by new or relatively unknown authors, negative reviews increased sales by 45%. A scathing review of a book called *Fierce People* noted "the change in tone is so abrupt that the dissonance it creates is almost distasteful" — yet sales more than quadrupled. Even negative word of mouth can increase sales if it makes people aware of a product or idea they otherwise would have forgotten. Even the Shake Weight, widely ridiculed, went on to do $50 million in sales.
