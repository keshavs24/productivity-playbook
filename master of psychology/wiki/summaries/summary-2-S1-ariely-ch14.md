# Predictably Irrational Ch.14: The Context of Our Character, Part II — Summary

**Author(s):** Dan Ariely
**Type:** Book chapter
**Phase:** 2
**Date ingested:** 2026-04-14

## Key Takeaways

1. People cheat far more when dealing with nonmonetary objects (tokens, stock options, office supplies) than when dealing with cash directly
2. Cash has a unique honesty-enforcing quality — the further removed a transaction is from actual cash, the easier it is to rationalize dishonesty
3. Even one step of removal from cash (tokens exchangeable for money seconds later) more than doubled the amount of cheating
4. As society moves toward cashless transactions, the moral constraints that cash provides are disappearing, potentially increasing systemic dishonesty

## Detailed Notes

### The Coke vs. Dollar Bills Experiment
**Setup:** Ariely slipped six-packs of Coke into shared refrigerators in MIT dorms. In other fridges, he left plates containing six one-dollar bills.

**Result:** All Cokes vanished within 72 hours. The dollar bills remained untouched for 72 hours until Ariely removed them.

**Interpretation:** Students who would never steal a dollar from a fridge had no qualms about taking a Coke worth the same amount. The Coke is one step removed from cash — it's easier to rationalize ("everyone takes Cokes," "it's communal").

### The Red Pencil Thought Experiment
- Taking a red pencil from work for your daughter: Most people are comfortable with this
- Taking 10 cents from the petty cash box to buy a red pencil: Most people are very uncomfortable
- The economic outcome is identical, but the moral weight is completely different based on proximity to cash

### The Token Experiment (Mazar, Amir, and Ariely)
**Setup:** MIT cafeteria. 20 math problems (find two numbers adding to 10). 50 cents per correct answer. Three conditions:

**Condition 1 — Control (no cheating):** Hand worksheets to experimenter. Average: 3.5 problems solved.

**Condition 2 — Cash cheating:** Tear up worksheets, self-report to experimenter, get paid in cash. Average claimed: 6.2 (cheated by 2.7 questions).

**Condition 3 — Token cheating:** Tear up worksheets, self-report, receive TOKENS (one per claimed problem). Walk 12 feet to another experimenter who exchanges tokens for 50 cents each. Average claimed: 9.4 (cheated by 5.9 questions — more than double the cash-cheating condition).

**Extreme cheaters:** Of 2,000 participants across all honesty studies, only 4 claimed perfect scores (total cheating rate: 4 in 2,000). But in the token condition alone, 24 of 450 participants cheated "all the way" — equivalent to 320 per 2,000. Tokens released some people from moral constraints completely.

### Prediction Failure
When students were asked to predict whether people would cheat more for tokens than cash, they said no — the tokens represented real money and would be treated the same. They were completely wrong. People don't see how fast they can rationalize dishonesty when it's one step removed from cash.

### Real-World Examples of Non-Cash Dishonesty
- **Airline miles:** Friend saved 25,000 frequent-flyer miles, found all dates blacked out, was told to use 50,000 miles. The airline effectively stole $450 — but would never have mugged him for cash.
- **Credit card two-cycle billing:** Banks reach into the past to charge interest on already-paid purchases. A man in Ohio charged $3,200 and found his debt ballooned to $10,700 in penalties, fees, and interest.
- **Insurance claims:** Consumers creatively stretch claims by about 10% — reporting a lost 27-inch TV as 32-inch. They would never steal cash directly from the insurance company.
- **"Wardrobing":** Buy clothes, wear them, return them. Industry loses ~$16 billion annually — same as home burglaries and auto theft combined.
- **Expense reports:** People justify questionable expenses more easily when: (a) receipts go to assistants (one more step removed), (b) purchases are made at airports far from home (easier to rationalize), (c) the item could plausibly be a business expense (buying a stranger an eight-dollar drink at a bar = easy to justify; buying a stranger a five-dollar mug = clearly out of bounds).

### Corporate Non-Cash Dishonesty
- Enron executives (Lay, Skilling, Fastow) took millions from pension funds of elderly people through accounting manipulation — but would they have physically hit an old woman with a blackjack and taken cash from her fingers? Almost certainly not.
- Companies are increasingly "stealing" in ways one step removed from cash: stock option backdating, creative accounting, underwriting parties for politicians

### Ariely's Own Experience with Dishonesty
Someone broke into his Skype account and charged his PayPal. The thief probably didn't think of himself as dishonest: he stole calling time (not cash), from Skype (not a person), and the cost went to PayPal automatically (further removed). Multiple layers of fuzziness made the theft feel like a technical exploit rather than stealing.

### The Moral Boundary Problem
- In experiments, most participants had a clear limit — they cheated some but stopped before going all the way. This was probably the LOWER boundary of human dishonesty (ethical people, public setting, immediate exchange)
- If the nonmonetary currencies were less immediately convertible (like stock options vesting over years), or if behavior was less publicly observable, dishonesty would likely be even higher
- A "Gordon Gekko" figure declaring "greed is good" using nonmonetary incentives could lead principled people astray — nonmonetary currencies let us bypass our conscience

### The Vanishing of Cash
- Cash is a drag on bank profits — electronic instruments are very profitable
- Credit card profits: from $9 billion (1996) to $27 billion (2004)
- As cash disappears, the one thing that keeps our moral compass aligned — the visceral reality of handling money — disappears too
- Willie Sutton robbed banks "because that's where the money was." By the same logic, modern thieves write fine print for credit card companies or blackout dates for airlines — it's not where the cash is, but it's where you'll find the money.
